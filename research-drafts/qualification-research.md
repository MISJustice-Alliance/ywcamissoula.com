# Research findings — peace-officer qualification, Montana OP 24-0341

**Research cutoff:** October 26, 2026, 19:34 UTC (system `date -u`); **status:** draft research, not a published memorandum or a citator certification. The two requested primary filings were read in full from the Montana judicial document service: the State's June 28, 2024 response (DocId 479757, 21 PDF pages including service certificate) and the Supreme Court's August 13, 2024 order (DocId 490058, 11 PDF pages). Pinpoints below refer to the printed/PDF pages of these documents. The court's docket was also checked.[1][2][3]

## Question and short answer

Under the statute applicable to the 2022 investigations, did deputies whose preappointment mental-health assessments were performed by a physician assistant retain peace-officer authority, and what did the Supreme Court actually dispose of? **No** on these facts: the Court held that the noncompliant assessments triggered the express forfeiture in Mont. Code Ann. § 7-32-303(5); POST and sheriff certifications, later compliant evaluations, lack of individualized prejudice, and the State's de facto-officer/reserve-officer arguments did not save these investigations. It granted supervisory control **only** to Patrick, Ovitt, and Green, reversing the district-court reversals of their justice-court suppression orders. It denied the other eight petitioners without deciding their pending district-court appeals. *Patrick v. Mont. Fourth Jud. Dist. Ct.*, No. OP 24-0341, slip op. at 4–5, 8–10 (Mont. Aug. 13, 2024) (order).[1]

## Record and positions (advocacy is not a holding)

- **Procedural posture.** The Mineral County Justice Court suppressed evidence in *State v. Patrick*, *State v. Ovitt*, and *State v. Green*; Judge Shane Vannatta reversed those three suppression orders on the State's appeals. In eight other cases, the Justice Court dismissed charges **without prejudice** and the State's appeals were then pending before Judge John Larson. *Patrick*, slip op. at 1–2, 5. This is a supervisory-control order, not a final judgment acquitting anyone or a decision resolving the eight other prosecutions.[1]
- **Undisputed qualification defect.** David Kunzelman investigated and arrested each of Patrick, Ovitt, and Green on DUI matters in 2022; Eric Lindauer assisted in Patrick's stop and observed Ovitt's arrest; Shawn Visintin backed up Green's stop. None of these deputies had received the statutorily required evaluation from a qualified provider at the relevant time; their earlier evaluations had been performed by a physician assistant. The State did **not** dispute those evaluations failed § 7-32-303(2)(g). Kunzelman had a compliant evaluation on October 2, 2022, Lindauer on March 23, 2023; the Court said the record did not establish whether Visintin ever had one before leaving employment. *Id.* at 3–5. The contemporaneous statutory alternative also allowed a properly scored standardized instrument; the record does not say that alternative was satisfied. Mont. Code Ann. § 7-32-303(2)(g)(i)–(ii) (2023).[1][5]
- **Petitioners' contention.** The deputies forfeited peace-officer position, authority, and arrest powers under § 7-32-303(5), so an investigation and arrest could not be justified by officer certification or by *State v. Updegraff*, 2011 MT 321, 363 Mont. 123, 267 P.3d 28, which concerned officers operating outside their jurisdiction. Petitioners also invoked the limited private-person arrest statute, § 46-6-502. *Patrick*, slip op. at 7; State's Resp. to Pet. for Writ of Supervisory Control 14–15, No. OP 24-0341 (Mont. June 28, 2024).[1][2]
- **State's response, not law adopted by the Court.** The State requested denial: the three could appeal (including, with State consent, a conditional guilty plea preserving the suppression issue), the alleged failure was a technical nonprejudicial statutory defect, and judicial economy alone did not support supervisory control. For the other eight, Judge Larson had made no ruling to supervise. On the merits, it argued that sheriff/POST certifications, reserve-deputy qualifications under § 7-32-213, *Updegraff*, the de facto-officer doctrine in *Wood v. Butorovich*, 220 Mont. 484, 716 P.2d 608 (1986), and exclusionary-rule reasoning under *State v. Pipkin*, 1998 MT 143, 289 Mont. 240, 961 P.2d 753, supported the district court. State's Resp. 2–3, 11–19. The response's descriptions of DUI evidence and BAC values at 4–10 are litigation recitations from the district-court orders, **not** new appellate findings of guilt.[2]

## Court holdings and precise relief

1. **Gatekeeping.** The petition was not served on Judge Larson as Mont. R. App. P. 14(6) required, so the Supreme Court said it **lacked jurisdiction** to entertain the eight petitioners' claims. Judge Larson also had not ruled, precluding a showing that he proceeded under a mistake of law. *Patrick*, slip op. at 2. Judicial economy alone did not justify supervisory control; nevertheless, the Court accepted the legal question for Patrick, Ovitt, and Green because their cases might take different paths, including settlements foreclosing an appeal, and the answer could affect other prosecutions. *Id.* at 2–3, 10. Do not characterize the eight denials as merits defeats on officer qualifications.[1]
2. **Statutory consequence.** Section 7-32-303(2)(g) required a qualified mental-health assessment; a physician assistant was neither the specified physician nor the specified mental-health professional. The express § 7-32-303(5) forfeiture meant these deputies were **not peace officers when they investigated and arrested the three petitioners**. *Id.* at 4, 8–9. The Court distinguished *Updegraff* (proper officers outside territorial jurisdiction) and *State v. Robertson*, 2019 MT 99, 395 Mont. 370, 440 P.3d 17 (failure to prove the asserted qualification defect), and held that the de facto-officer doctrine from *Wood* could not override the contrary statute. *Id.* at 5–9. It rejected a separate requirement that the defendants prove prejudice attributable to the deputies' status. *Id.* at 9.[1]
3. **Limited private-person powers.** Section 46-6-502 permits an immediate arrest on probable cause and requires immediate notice to law enforcement and transfer of custody, but does not authorize a private person to investigate, search, or collect evidence. The Court found no credible basis that the deputies had acted within those limits. *Id.* at 9. The ruling is tied to the identified statutory defect and those investigations, **not** a general holding that every irregular officer action always requires suppression.[1]
4. **Disposition.** The Court **accepted and granted** the petition for Dawson Dennis Patrick, Dylan Todd Lee Ovitt, and Samual Murrell Green; **denied** it for John Arispe, John Anthony Blackburn, James Horodyski, James London, Harley McDonald, Troy Parker, Brandon Rosenhahn, and Andrew Suitonu; and **reversed the district court's orders reversing suppression** in *State v. Patrick*, DC-23-35; *State v. Ovitt*, DC-23-34; and *State v. Green*, DC-23-36. *Id.* at 10. Justice Beth Baker would have denied the supervisory petition. *Id.* at 11. The order does not itself dismiss the three charges or announce an ultimate conviction/acquittal. The eight justice-court dismissals had been without prejudice, and their State appeals were pending as of the order. *Id.* at 1, 10–11.[1]

## Subsequent treatment and currency limits

- **Same-case docket:** The official OP 24-0341 case-information feed lists the August 13, 2024 order as final disposition and an **“Event - Case Closed” dated September 27, 2024**; it lists no later filing in this Supreme Court docket. The closing entry is administrative docket evidence, not evidence of the outcomes of the underlying eleven criminal cases.[3]
- **Later judicial citations:** Targeted searches for the docket number, case caption, deputy's name, and § 7-32-303(5), including CourtListener opinion search and public web indexing, did **not locate a later opinion citing or overruling this order** by the cutoff. CourtListener searches for the exact docket and caption returned zero, while a further query was rate-limited; the official case feed covers only this proceeding. This is a bounded negative search, **not** a comprehensive Shepard's/KeyCite result or a representation that no unindexed trial-court order or later case exists. The eight district-court appeal outcomes and any eventual disposition of all eleven prosecutions remain unverified.[3]
- **Statutory currency:** The official 2025 code lists a 2025 amendment to § 7-32-303 (Ch. 499, § 2, Laws 2025), but its displayed subsections (2)(g) and (5) continue to require the specified evaluator/qualified scoring and to say an officer failing subsection (2) qualifications “forfeits the position, authority, and arrest powers.” Compare Mont. Code Ann. § 7-32-303(2)(g), (5) (2023), with id. (2025). That textual comparison alone does not establish the amendment's purpose, its application to a later factual record, or legislative approval/disapproval of this order.[4][5]
