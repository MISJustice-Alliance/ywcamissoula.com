## 1. Verified disposition of Holland v. Simmerman — CORRECTING the seed assumption

**Critical correction:** Holland v. Simmerman is an **Eighth Circuit** decision (No. 25-3020), not Ninth Circuit. Appeal from the Eastern District of Missouri (Cape Girardeau), underlying district case 1:24-cv-00201. Decided August 25, 2026. Panel: Kelly, Grasz (author), Kobes. Montana and this investigation sit in the **Ninth Circuit**, so Holland is **out-of-circuit, persuasive-only authority** for any Montana § 1983 claim — it cannot be cited as controlling Ninth Circuit law, and every legal-relevance claim below is qualified accordingly. This correction supersedes the seed research question's framing.

**Facts:** George Holland, in mental-health crisis, held a pistol during a standoff with Sikeston, MO police. Officer Martin Simmerman fired 15 rounds in three volleys, striking Holland six times; Holland died. Family sued under § 1983 (excessive force, Monell, substantive due process) and Missouri state law (wrongful death, battery).

**Procedural posture:** Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss. Defendants attached three body-camera videos; district court treated the videos as blatantly contradicting the complaint and dismissed the entire complaint **with prejudice**, granting Simmerman qualified immunity (Count I) and official immunity (Counts II–III), and dismissing the Monell claim (Count IV) for want of an underlying violation. Counts V–VI (substantive due process / Monell due process) were also dismissed with prejudice below.

**What the Eighth Circuit actually held (verified against the court's own text, courtlistener.com/opinion/10957483):**
- **First volley:** QI **affirmed**. The video blatantly contradicts the complaint's claim that Holland took no menacing action before being shot; the court credited the video over the pleading for this volley only.
- **Second and third volleys:** QI **reversed**. The video does not show Holland's actions once he retreated into the corridor behind a wall — it shows only Simmerman firing and the gun later sliding onto the floor. Because the video does not "conclusively disprove" the complaint's account of the later volleys, the Hollands' excessive-force claim as to those shots survives dismissal and is **remanded**.
- **Fourteenth Amendment component of Count I:** treated as **abandoned** on appeal (Hollands did not argue it); that portion stays dismissed.
- **Counts II–III (state-law wrongful death / battery, official immunity):** **not independently adjudicated** by the court of appeals — remanded for the district court to reconsider in light of the Count I ruling and a new intervening Missouri Court of Appeals decision, *McGaugh v. Naudet*, 733 S.W.3d 441 (Mo. Ct. App. 2026) (official immunity for intentional torts), which the court flagged in footnote 5 as something the district court should weigh on remand.
- **Count IV (Monell municipal-liability claim):** **remanded, not adjudicated as a proven policy or pattern.** It had been dismissed below solely because the predicate individual violation was dismissed; since the excessive-force claim now partly survives, the Monell claim gets another chance to be pled/argued — the Eighth Circuit did **not** find a municipal policy or custom, did not reach deliberate indifference, and expressed no view on the merits of any Monell theory.
- **Counts V–VI:** waived/abandoned on appeal; remain dismissed with prejudice. **This is the ruling favorable to the officer/city** the assignment asked me to include — the due-process theories are foreclosed, and the first-volley excessive-force theory is foreclosed too (QI affirmed there).
- **Disposition line:** "We affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion."

**Separate opinion (Judge Kelly, concurring in part, dissenting in part):** Agrees with the majority on the second/third-volley analysis. Dissents only on the **first volley**: given video quality, she could not rule out that Holland was pointing the pistol at the wall or at himself rather than at officers, so she would hold the video does **not** blatantly contradict the complaint even for the first volley, and would reverse QI there too. She also separately emphasizes that the "totality of the circumstances" / Graham factors inquiry is fact-intensive and that this case's motion-to-dismiss posture makes summary-judgment precedent "less helpful."

**Subsequent history (verified through today, 2026-11-16):** Panel rehearing and rehearing en banc were **both denied on September 29, 2026** (PACER/CourtListener docket). No petition for certiorari was located in the sources checked. **Not independently verified:** docket status after September 29, 2026, and any filings in the remanded district court case (1:24-cv-00201) — the remand is recent and a public updated docket entry was not retrieved in this pass. Treat the remand's current procedural stage on the district court docket as unresolved pending a dedicated PACER/RECAP check, which was not performed here (read-only legal-research time budget).

## 2. What Holland actually contributes to the Missoula investigation

Holland is **not** a case about institutional notice-and-response over time, complaint intake, oversight-body review, or victim advocacy — it is a single-incident deadly-force qualified-immunity appeal at the pleading stage. Its contribution to this investigation is **methodological, not substantive**, and narrow:

**A. CORRECTED — examine materially distinct stages without letting an earlier justification dispose of later conduct, while still reading the record collectively.** The Eighth Circuit's actual move is more disciplined than pure segmentation: it asked, stage by stage, whether the *specific* justification offered for one volley also covered the next — and refused to let the first volley's video-supported justification silently carry over to volleys the video did not show. But the court (and especially Judge Kelly's concurrence/dissent) kept the Graham "totality of the circumstances" inquiry intact as the ultimate standard; the opinion does not endorse breaking one encounter into legally independent atoms. The transferable lesson for this investigation is therefore narrower than "always segment": **examine each institutional decision point on its own stated justification, and do not let an earlier stage's resolved justification (e.g., ODC's November 2025 substantive Tipp determination) silently answer a later, differently-grounded stage (e.g., the July 2026 "already addressed" characterization) — while still reading the full multi-stage record together to see whether the actors' stated reasons cohere.** Segmentation is a check against overclaiming resolution; it is not license to analyze each letter as if the others didn't exist. This cautions against "artificial fragmentation" in either direction — treating one institutional stage as if it resolved everything, or treating each stage as hermetically unrelated to the others.

**B. CORRECTED — the actual motion-to-dismiss rule, not a symmetric "neither side" principle.** My earlier framing overstated this as a general evidentiary holding; it is narrower and asymmetric. At the Rule 12(b)(6) stage, the court does not treat an inconclusive record as proving nothing for anyone — it applies the ordinary plausibility standard (*Iqbal*/*Twombly*) to the complaint, displaced only where video "blatantly contradicts" the pleaded facts (*Waters v. Madson*). Because the corridor-volley video did not conclusively disprove the Hollands' account, the complaint's *allegations* were credited as true for pleading purposes — not because the record was read as neutral, but because the usual rule (credit the plaintiff's well-pleaded facts) was never displaced for that volley. That is the real transferable lesson for this investigation: an institution's or this site's own record-silence does not, on its own, let either a defense account or a complainant account substitute for what is actually documented — the default is to describe what the existing record actually shows and say plainly where it is silent, not to fill the silence with an inference either way. This still supports the `ywca-gitbook-evidence` skill's absence-of-evidence/evidence-of-absence distinction, but the citation should describe the MTD mechanism accurately rather than invoking an invented general rule.

**C. A caution against overreading dismissals.** Holland's own case history is a worked example of the investigation's own stated norm ("a dismissal does not by itself prove factual innocence or the opposite") — here, a full dismissal with prejudice is partially undone two volleys out of three, on the same record, without any new facts. That is directly useful as an illustration (not proof) when writing about Montana dispositions that a reader might otherwise treat as final.

**D. CORRECTED — what the Monell discussion in Holland actually limits.** Monell/Count IV *is* expressly discussed in Holland — it is wrong to suggest otherwise. The correct limitation is narrower: (i) no municipal policy, custom, or pattern was ever adjudicated as proven; (ii) no element of municipal liability (policy, causation, deliberate indifference, final-policymaker action) was independently resolved by the court of appeals; (iii) the Monell claim was remanded *solely as a consequence* of the predicate individual-liability reversal — the district court had dismissed Count IV only because Count I failed, and now that part of Count I survives, Count IV gets reconsidered on that basis alone, not because the Eighth Circuit found anything about the City of Sikeston's policies. This is still a narrow, process-only point with no substantive transfer to any Montana municipal-liability theory. Beyond that, Holland supplies no authority on attorney-discipline or officer-certification review bodies (POST/ODC have no analog in the opinion), private victim-advocacy organizations (YWCA — the opinion never discusses non-governmental actors), or "institutional inertia" as a legal theory — the phrase and concept do not appear in the opinion and must not be presented as if the court endorsed it. The opinion is also silent on 34 U.S.C. § 12601 (DOJ pattern-or-practice authority), confidentiality/victim-advocacy duties, and professional-discipline procedure.

## 3. Strongest supported local connections vs. weakest analogies

**Strongest (methodological, properly qualified):**
- Segmenting multi-stage institutional responses by discrete decision point (as in the existing cross-institution matrix in `institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis.md`) mirrors Holland's volley-by-volley analysis and can be cited as a persuasive, out-of-circuit illustration of why one record (e.g., ODC's November 2025 Tipp determination) cannot resolve what a later, differently-grounded record (the July 2026 "already addressed" letters) actually decided.
- The record-gap discipline (incomplete record is neither proof nor disproof) directly reinforces, with real appellate language, what `when-a-complaint-closes.md` and the evidence skill already argue analytically without a comparative case citation.

**Weakest / must not be made:**
- Treating Holland as Monell authority for a Missoula municipal-liability theory — it reversed nothing on Monell and decided nothing about policy or custom; citing it as "the court found a pattern" would be false.
- Treating Holland as establishing any duty, standard, or liability theory for prosecutors (in their charging/disclosure function), POST, ODC, the Commission on Practice, or YWCA of Missoula — none of those actor types appear in the opinion, and the use-of-force qualified-immunity framework does not transfer to licensing/discipline/advocacy bodies without independent authority for each.
- Treating the separate opinion's narrower disagreement (about whether the video resolves the direction Holland pointed the gun) as support for a broader "institutions always construe ambiguous video/records against complainants" narrative — Judge Kelly's dissent is fact-specific to image resolution and gun-barrel direction, not a general evidentiary presumption.
- Using Holland's "out-of-circuit" status loosely — it is Eighth Circuit, applying Eighth Circuit and Missouri law; it is not binding anywhere in the Ninth Circuit and should be flagged as persuasive-only wherever cited.
