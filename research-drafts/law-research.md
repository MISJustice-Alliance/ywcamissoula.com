# Montana criminal-disclosure and officer-authority research — working memorandum

**Research date:** October 26, 2026. **Scope:** Montana law and controlling U.S. Supreme Court law; Ninth Circuit decisions govern federal litigation in Montana, but do not displace Montana Supreme Court construction of Montana statutes. This is an issue map, not an opinion on an unidentified record. The applicable offense, appointment date, training/certification record, discovery requests, plea terms, and trial/postconviction posture must be established before applying it. Midpage was not available in the connected tool catalog; no Midpage analysis was performed. Montana Legislature and Montana Judicial Branch texts are preferred; CourtListener and reproductions of the actual opinions are identified below where the judicial-host PDF could not be reliably fetched.

## 1. Brady/Giglio at trial and at a plea

- **Federal floor.** *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, **87–88** (1963), holds suppression of requested favorable, material guilt-or-punishment evidence violates due process regardless of good faith; the remedy in that case was a new trial **on punishment only**, not automatic vacation of the guilt verdict. *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150, **153–55** (1972), applies the duty to a material promise affecting a crucial witness's credibility, imputes one prosecutor's promise to the office even if trial counsel did not know it, and orders a new trial. Materiality and prejudice are distinct from the duty to preserve/produce evidence.[1][2]
- **Adverse plea limit.** *United States v. Ruiz*, 536 U.S. 622, **629–33** (2002), reverses the Ninth Circuit and holds the federal Constitution does **not** require disclosure of material **impeachment** evidence before a plea agreement; it also rejects a pre-plea constitutional duty to provide affirmative-defense information in the agreement before it. Critically, the proposed agreement acknowledged disclosure of known information establishing **factual innocence**; *Ruiz* does not squarely decide whether suppression of such direct innocence evidence vitiates a plea, and it does not construe Montana's broader statute or ethics rule. Do not cite it for an across-the-board exemption from state discovery.[3]
- **Ninth Circuit, cautiously.** *Parker v. County of Riverside*, 78 F.4th 1109, **1115–18** (9th Cir. 2023) (majority, official slip op. at **7–11**), repeats existing circuit precedent, *Sanchez v. United States*, 50 F.3d 1448, **1453–54** (9th Cir. 1995): a plea-voluntariness Brady claim asks whether disclosure probably would have led the defendant to reject the plea and go to trial. But *Parker* actually **rejects** a § 1983 Brady theory where charges were dismissed without any affected judicial proceeding; strategic harm or prolonged pretrial detention alone was insufficient. It leaves possible a distinct *Tatum* prolonged-detention claim, not adjudicated there. Judge R. Nelson's argument that *Sanchez* conflicts with *Ruiz* is **concurrence, not the majority holding** (slip op. at 12–19). *Parker* does not settle whether the exact material is affirmative innocence evidence rather than impeachment or whether Montana statutory disclosure was breached.[4]
- **Montana trial decisions.** *State v. Severson*, 2024 MT 76, **¶¶ 15–18 & nn.5–6, 27–31**, 416 Mont. 201, 546 P.3d 765, distinguishes the independent § 46-15-322(1)(e) duty, states the three-part Brady test (favorability, suppression, reasonable probability of different result), rejects a categorical continuance-request waiver, and notes Montana abandoned its old fourth “defense diligence” element in *State v. Reinert*, 2018 MT 111, ¶ 17 n.1. Favorability can include leads directly to admissible exculpatory evidence and impeachment of a key witness. **Limit:** *Severson* reversed for **cumulative** errors and remanded for a new trial; it did not award dismissal solely for a Brady violation, and did not address guilty-plea disclosure.[7]
- **Recent adverse Montana application.** *Temple v. State*, 2025 MT 185, **¶¶ 14–20**, treats an implied promise or witness's evidenced expectation that a prosecutor will seek a benefit as potentially disclosable impeachment even without a signed agreement or guaranteed judicial sentence reduction. It distinguishes mere gratuitous post-trial benefits from a pretrial understanding. **Limit:** *Temple* **affirmed denial of postconviction relief** on its particular evidentiary/materiality record; it is not authority that every later benefit proves an undisclosed deal or that every failure to disclose warrants a new trial. Verify the complete record of the particular expectation and other evidence before analogizing.[8]

## 2. Montana disclosure statute and procedural limits

- **§ 46-15-322(1), (4)–(6), MCA (2025).** **Upon request**, the prosecutor must make available listed witness names/statements, defendant statements, expert reports/results, certain tangible objects/documents, and **all information tending to mitigate or negate guilt or reduce a potential sentence**, within possession/control. Subsection (4) reaches prosecutor's staff and **other participants in investigation/evaluation**. Subsection (5) permits a court, on a showing of **substantial need and inability without undue hardship to obtain the equivalent**, to order additional material from any person; it does not require the prosecutor to prepare witness-testimony summaries. Subsection (6) requires certain rebuttal witness names and statements by five days before trial, subject to a good-cause later time. § 46-15-327 adds prompt continuing disclosure of newly discovered material. Distinguish the statute's request and category predicates from constitutional Brady materiality.[5][6]
- *City of Bozeman v. Howard*, 2021 MT 230, **¶¶ 12–15**, enforces preservation: Brady/Giglio and § 46-15-322(1)(e) arguments raised belatedly in a municipal reply and omitted in district-court appeal were not considered; a **general fishing request** for an arresting officer's personnel file did not independently establish subsection (5) substantial need over privacy interests. It reiterates no unfettered constitutional right to search government files. Develop a specific theory of relevance, request and record ruling; do not present speculation as a proven Giglio file.[9]
- *State v. Torres*, 2021 MT 301, **¶¶ 23–25**, recognizes broad pretrial disclosure and the statute's notice/anti-surprise purpose, but identifies an **impeachment-rebuttal witness exception** distinct from enumerated § 46-15-322(6) rebuttal categories. A trial-discovery claim needs the particular witness use and resulting prejudice, not merely a general assertion that every witness had to be named.[10]

## 3. Peace-officer qualification: time-sensitive § 7-32-303

- **2019 and 2023 legislative editions** both provide at **§ 7-32-303(2)(d)–(e)** felony-penitentiary-eligible-conviction disqualification and good moral character/background standards; **(2)(g)–(h)** mental/physical fitness evaluation rules; **(2)(k), (5)–(10)** certification eligibility, basic/equivalency training, specified break-in-service exceptions and council extension. Subsection **(5)** says an officer appointed after September 30, 1983, who fails the **subsection (2) minimum requirements** or required **(6)–(8) training** forfeits position, authority and arrest powers. **It does not say every allegation or every administrative rule infraction automatically voids an arrest, a conviction, or a prosecution.** Subsection (11) separately criminalizes acting after POST certification revocation/denial for misconduct or during suspension. Read date of appointment, course deadline, extension and POST action before alleging forfeiture.[11][12]
- **2025 legislative edition** adds **Ch. 499, L. 2025** to the history and reworks **(1)** to define council and peace officer; **(6)(a)** permits certain council-approved accredited college/university academy courses and adjusts cross-references while preserving the subsection (5) forfeiture sentence and (11) suspension/revocation prohibition. The 2019 and 2023 editions have **no 2025 amendment**. These are legislature-posted **editions**, not evidence that a provision was in effect on every intervening incident date or that Ch. 499 was effective on a particular day; inspect the enacted chapter's effective/applicability clauses and the controlling offense/appointment date before a historical legal conclusion. No officer-specific POST/employment documents were reviewed.[11][12][13]
- *State v. Robertson*, 2019 MT 99, **¶¶ 1–2, 11–14** (2019 § 7-32-303 challenge), affirmed denial of a motion to exclude an arresting officer's testimony on asserted physical-exam deficiency. **Limited use:** a qualification dispute is not itself a blanket evidentiary exclusion; the Court also addressed separate suppression and lost-video issues. The full merits discussion and subsequent treatment of its qualification analysis should be checked in the authoritative opinion before quoting a broader holding.[22]

## 4. Mandamus, prosecutorial discretion and private arrest

- **Mandamus.** § 27-26-102(1)–(2), MCA (2025), permits a writ compelling a duty specially enjoined by law only where no plain, speedy, adequate ordinary remedy exists. *Richards v. Gernant*, 2020 MT 239, **¶¶ 16, 18–20, 29–31** (official Montana Judicial Branch PDF), requires a **clear, nondiscretionary duty**; it refused to compel a county clerk to record a survey claiming an exemption the reviewer had denied. That is a ministerial-duty case, **not** a holding about compelling criminal charges.[14][15]
- The county attorney's prosecutorial duties appear in **§ 7-4-2712(1)–(2), MCA** (institute proceedings before magistrates on information of committed offenses; draw indictments/informations). This text does not establish a private complainant's right to select charges, dictate probable-cause findings, or obtain a conviction by writ. *Schoof v. Nesbit*, 2014 MT 6, **¶¶ 40–41**, found a proposed mandamus claim concerning **recovery of allegedly illegal county payments** premature/hypothetical because the underlying policy had not first been invalidated; its statute was **§ 7-4-2714**, a civil recovery duty. **Do not transpose *Schoof* into a holding requiring criminal prosecution.** No directly on-point Montana opinion compelling a prosecutor to file the requested criminal charge was verified in this search.[21][16]
- **Private arrest is narrow.** § 46-6-502(1)–(2), MCA (2025), allows a private person to arrest on probable cause that an offense is being or has been committed **and** circumstances requiring **immediate** arrest, with reasonable force only, followed by immediate notice to nearest available law-enforcement agency/officer and transfer of custody. *City of Missoula v. Iosefo*, 2014 MT 209, **¶¶ 1–3**, affirmed denial of suppression after an off-duty officer working private security arrested an impaired driver in a festival's pedestrian area; highly fact-bound, not authority for a private warrant, delayed investigative detention, or mandatory prosecution. Do not attempt an arrest as a substitute for judicial process.[17][18]

## 5. Remedies and ethics; avoid automatic-relief claims

- **Before/during criminal proceedings:** § 46-15-329(1)–(5), MCA, allows a court a **just** sanction for discovery noncompliance: production, continuance, contempt for intentional violation, preclusion, or mistrial when needed to avert miscarriage of justice. The text does **not** make dismissal, automatic suppression of all evidence, civil damages, or vacatur automatic. Preservation, timing, materiality/prejudice, and requested relief matter. *Severson* ordered a new trial on cumulative error, not automatic dismissal; *Parker* rejects a Brady damages theory with no affected judicial proceeding.[19][7][4]
- **Postconviction:** § 46-21-105(1)–(3), MCA, requires grounds in an original/amended original petition and limits serial petitions and grounds reasonably available on direct appeal. A potentially undiscovered claim requires examination of this statute and the distinct limitations provision, the plea/waiver record, and procedural history; this memo does not certify timeliness or entitlement to reopen.[23]
- **Ethical duties:** Montana Rules of Professional Conduct **3.8(a)** bars charges known unsupported by probable cause; **3.8(d)** requires **timely** defense disclosure of known information tending to negate guilt/mitigate offense and, at sentencing, unprivileged mitigating information to defense and tribunal absent protective order; **3.8(g)–(h)** address credible material postconviction innocence evidence and clear-and-convincing innocence evidence. Rules **3.3(a)** (candor), **3.4(a),(d)** (evidence/discovery), and **8.4(c),(d)** (dishonesty/prejudice to justice) can separately matter on established facts. **Adverse limit:** the rules' Preamble **¶¶ 20–21** describes disciplinary enforcement and expressly says a rule violation itself does **not** create a civil cause of action, presumptive legal duty, automatic disqualification, or other nondisciplinary remedy. Disciplinary complaint and litigation remedy are different questions.[20]

## Treatment and source limitations

Targeted later-case searches on October 26, 2026 located *Temple* (2025) applying *Severson* (2024), and *Parker* (2023) reaffirming the pre-plea *Sanchez* formulation while limiting a no-proceeding civil Brady claim. Searches also tested later uses of *Robertson*, *Temple*, and *Parker*, but **did not complete comprehensive citing-reference, negative-treatment, shepardization, or pending-petition review**. CourtListener case search returned results, but its opinion API returned **401** and additional requests were throttled; Montana judicial-host PDFs for some cases intermittently failed, and browser access returned **402**. No Midpage citator/search was performed. A search hit or absence thereof cannot establish that an authority is still good law. Obtain official opinions and a licensed citator check before filing; verify the precise historical statutory edition and Ch. 499 effective date against the incident date. The opinions cited through CourtListener/Justia are the court's opinion texts as reproduced there, **not those sites' editorial summaries**.
