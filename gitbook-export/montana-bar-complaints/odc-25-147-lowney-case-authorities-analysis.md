# Tipp malpractice and Lowney ODC grievance: civil intake case analysis

### Executive snapshot

This page summarizes two related but procedurally distinct matters arising from the same underlying Missoula criminal case:

1. **ODC File No. 25-147 - Bryan C. Tipp.** This is the defense-counsel grievance submitted in July 2025. The Montana Office of Disciplinary Counsel rejected the grievance, but the alleged malpractice theories remain relevant because the client is seeking civil representation.
2. **Brian Lowney ODC grievance.** This is the prosecutorial-misconduct grievance submitted April 28, 2026 against Deputy County Attorney Brian Lowney and the Missoula County Attorney's Office. This grievance is still under review by the Montana ODC.

The goal of this summary is practical: to help potential civil attorneys, civil-rights organizations, legal clinics, journalists, and oversight reviewers quickly understand the case posture, the strongest supporting authorities, and the key proof questions.

The Tipp materials allege that defense counsel failed to preserve civil-rights and tort claims, failed to communicate statute-of-limitations risks, failed to investigate investigator conflicts, and accepted or failed to challenge case-resolution terms that restricted protected speech. The Lowney grievance separately alleges Brady/Giglio violations, reliance on conflicted investigators, prosecution of conduct previously identified as protected speech, coercive bail and filing practices, and a deferred-prosecution condition requiring speech removal or silence.

### Case background

The Tipp track concerns **defense representation**. The July 2025 ODC 25-147 grievance alleges that Bryan Tipp represented Elvis Nuno in Missoula criminal proceedings from roughly 2018 through the January 2021 dismissal of charges. The central defense-counsel allegation is not merely that Tipp declined to file civil litigation. The sharper allegation is that he allegedly discouraged civil action, failed to communicate deadlines, failed to investigate or refer civil-rights claims, and allowed key limitation periods to expire while the criminal case remained unresolved.

The original complaint frames the alleged failure around "give the matter time" advice, lost 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims, defamation claims, intentional-infliction claims, and alleged conflicts between a quiet criminal resolution and preservation of civil accountability. The supplemental lost-claims appendix then converts that theory into a timeline and damages model, alleging that a competent lawyer would have warned of deadlines, coordinated criminal and civil strategy, and filed protective civil actions or referred civil counsel before limitations expired.

The Lowney track concerns **prosecutorial conduct**. The April 28, 2026 grievance addresses the prosecution side of the same underlying criminal matter and remains pending under Montana ODC review. It alleges that the prosecution relied on a YWCA complaint letter after a municipal-court hearing had treated that letter as protected speech, used reports from an officer allegedly removed for impropriety, failed to disclose Detective Connie Brueckner's alleged YWCA board conflict, and resolved the case through a deferred prosecution agreement that required removal or suppression of protected speech.

### Strongest defense-counsel authorities

Montana disciplinary precedent supports treating missed deadlines and poor client communication as professional-conduct issues rather than merely private dissatisfaction. In [In Matter of Johnson, 597 P.2d 740 (Mont. 1979)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/in-matter-of-johnson-876910?utm_source=mcp), the Montana Supreme Court approved discipline where an attorney failed to file a lawsuit until after the statute of limitations had run and misled clients about case status. The case is useful because it connects missed limitations, inadequate preparation, neglect, and client misinformation in a disciplinary setting.

[Matter of Halprin, 798 P.2d 80 (Mont. 1990)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/matter-of-halprin-1258624?utm_source=mcp) is stronger for modern MRPC framing. The Montana Supreme Court found violations of Rule 1.3 and Rule 1.4 where an attorney failed to act with diligence and failed to keep the client reasonably informed in a matter where the statute of limitations had apparently run. For ODC 25-147, Halprin supports the argument that deadline loss plus lack of communication can be analyzed as competence, diligence, and communication misconduct.

[Spencer v. Beck, 245 P.3d 21 (Mont. 2010)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/spencer-v-beck-889236?utm_source=mcp) matters because the alleged malpractice arose from criminal representation. Spencer explains Montana's "two-track" approach: a criminal defendant can pursue malpractice in civil court while criminal/postconviction issues proceed separately. It also confirms that a criminal-defense malpractice claim is governed by the three-year period running from discovery of the alleged act, error, or omission, regardless of whether postconviction relief is sought or granted.

### Section 1983 timing

[Wallace v. Kato, 549 U.S. 384 (2007)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/wallace-v-kato-145756?utm_source=mcp) is central to the missed-deadline theory. Wallace holds that § 1983 accrual is governed by federal law, even though the limitations length is borrowed from the state personal-injury period. It also rejects a broad rule that civil-rights claims wait until a future criminal case ends. That makes Wallace important where counsel allegedly told a client to wait while claims tied to arrest, detention, retaliation, or prosecution continued aging.

The practical point is narrow but important: "wait until the criminal case is over" is not a universal limitations rule. Some claims may accrue before dismissal, and courts may stay parallel civil litigation rather than treat the criminal case as automatically tolling all civil-rights claims.

### Brady and investigator-conflict authorities

The Lowney grievance's Brady theory is strongest where the alleged nondisclosed information was known to the prosecution team or police acting on the State's behalf. [State v. Ilk, 422 P.3d 1219 (Mont. 2018)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-ilk-6534248?utm_source=mcp) states that a prosecutor has an affirmative duty to learn favorable evidence known to others acting for the government, including police. Ilk also confirms the duty exists even without a defense request.

[G. Temple v. State, 573 P.3d 1200 (Mont. 2025)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/g-temple-v-state-11124152?utm_source=mcp) adds an important modern Montana formulation: prosecutors are responsible for information within their offices and evidence possessed by investigating agencies or those working on the office's behalf, regardless of actual knowledge. Temple also emphasizes the limits of Brady: the information must still be favorable and material, and cross-agency constructive knowledge has boundaries when the other agency was not part of the prosecution team.

Applied to the grievance, these cases support the theory that a police investigator's undisclosed institutional conflict can be Brady/Giglio material if it bears on bias, credibility, motive, or the integrity of the investigation. The proof burden remains materiality: the record must show a reasonable probability that disclosure would have changed the proceeding, undermined confidence in the result, or materially affected the DPA and dismissal posture.

### First Amendment prosecution theory

[Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250 (2006)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hartman-v-moore-145662?utm_source=mcp) recognizes that official retaliation for protected speech, including retaliatory prosecution, can violate the First Amendment. But Hartman is also a limiting authority. Retaliatory-prosecution claims generally require pleading and proving absence of probable cause because courts presume regularity in prosecutorial decision-making.

For public analysis, Hartman means the strongest version of the claim is not simply that protected speech appeared somewhere in the case. The stronger version is that the protected speech allegedly became a factual basis for prosecution despite an existing protected-speech ruling, and that the remaining evidence allegedly did not supply probable cause independent of the speech.

### DPA speech-removal theory

The speech-restriction issue is the clearest public-facing constitutional issue because it concerns a government benefit: dismissal or deferred prosecution allegedly conditioned on removing public criticism or remaining silent.

[Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593 (1972)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/perry-v-sindermann-108609?utm_source=mcp) provides the broad rule: the government may not deny a benefit on a basis that infringes protected speech interests, even when the person has no entitlement to the benefit. This supports the argument that a dismissal benefit cannot be used as leverage to suppress protected speech without a constitutionally sufficient justification.

[United States v. Jonathan Wells, 29 F.4th 580 (9th Cir. 2022)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-jonathan-wells-6325612?utm_source=mcp) provides a criminal-condition framework. Even where supervised-release or probation conditions may burden constitutional rights, the restriction must be reasonably related to deterrence, public protection, or rehabilitation, and it must involve no greater liberty deprivation than reasonably necessary.

[United States v. Maynard Richards, 385 F. App'x 691 (9th Cir. 2010)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-maynard-richards-149564?utm_source=mcp) is unpublished and therefore only persuasive, but it is factually useful. The Ninth Circuit vacated a probation condition barring public comments about a named person and family members because the restriction violated the First Amendment. It supports the common-sense point that broad public-comment bans are constitutionally suspect.

[Rodriguez v. MARICOPA CTY. COMMUNITY COLLEGE DIST., 605 F.3d 703 (9th Cir. 2010)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/rodriguez-v-maricopa-cty-community-146838?utm_source=mcp) is broader First Amendment support. It rejects censorship based on offensiveness and explains that there is no categorical harassment exception to the First Amendment. Its fit depends on whether the speech at issue was public commentary rather than true threats, direct harassment, or unlawful contact.

### Limiting authority and expected defenses

[Town of Newton v. Rumery, 480 U.S. 386 (1987)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/town-of-newton-v-rumery-111837?utm_source=mcp) is the most important limiting authority. Rumery rejects a per se rule invalidating agreements that release § 1983 claims in exchange for dismissal of criminal charges. It holds that such agreements may be enforceable where voluntary, deliberate, informed, supported by legitimate prosecutorial interests, and not contrary to public policy.

Rumery does not defeat the speech-waiver argument by itself, but it changes the question. The issue is not whether every waiver in a criminal resolution is invalid. The issue is whether this alleged speech-removal term was voluntary, informed, narrowly justified, and consistent with the public interest, especially if the speech had already been treated as protected and the restriction served institutional reputation interests rather than public safety.

Expected defenses include:

* **Scope of representation:** Tipp may argue he was criminal counsel, not civil counsel. The response is that the complaint alleges affirmative advice, deadline silence, and failure to refer, not merely refusal to file a separate civil suit.
* **Strategic judgment:** Counsel may argue that delaying civil action protected the criminal defense. The response is that Wallace and Montana malpractice timing make blanket delay risky unless the client receives informed advice about limitation consequences.
* **Materiality:** The prosecutor may argue any nondisclosed conflicts or hearing facts would not have changed the outcome. The response must tie each nondisclosure to probable cause, bail, DPA leverage, or dismissal posture.
* **Voluntary agreement:** The State may argue the DPA was a negotiated voluntary resolution. The response must develop coercion, lack of informed consent, lack of narrow tailoring, and absence of legitimate public-protection need.
* **Probable cause:** Hartman requires careful handling. A public-facing claim should identify what evidence allegedly remained after protected speech, conflicted-investigator evidence, and unsupported technical speculation are removed.

### Civil intake assessment

The strongest portion of the potential civil malpractice case against Tipp is the deadline-and-communication theory. Montana cases directly support discipline where attorneys neglect client matters, allow limitation problems to arise, and fail to keep clients informed. For civil intake, the key point is that the matter is not only about whether Tipp was hired to file civil claims. The case turns on whether he allegedly discouraged action, failed to warn of deadlines, failed to provide a meaningful civil referral, and left the client without a preservation strategy while claims expired.

The strongest portion of the Lowney grievance is the Brady/Giglio investigator-conflict theory if the record can prove that the alleged conflicts were known or readily known to the prosecution team, bore on bias or credibility, and were material to probable cause, bail, case leverage, or the DPA. Ilk and Temple are strong Montana authorities, but materiality is the key proof point. Because the Lowney grievance remains under ODC review, it should be treated as a live oversight matter and a supporting factual track for civil-rights advocacy, not as a resolved disciplinary outcome.

The DPA speech-removal issue is the most publicly accessible constitutional issue. Perry and Wells support skepticism toward criminal-resolution terms that burden speech without a legitimate and narrow public-safety rationale. Rumery supplies the main defense and should be addressed directly, not ignored.

### Materials useful for potential civil counsel

Potential civil counsel will likely need to evaluate:

* **Representation scope and communications:** retainer terms, emails, advice about civil claims, any statements about waiting, and whether limitations warnings or referrals were given.
* **Claim-by-claim limitations:** § 1983, defamation, intentional infliction of emotional distress, tortious interference, abuse of process, malicious prosecution, and any state-law claims.
* **Underlying-case merit:** whether the lost claims were viable enough to support malpractice causation and damages.
* **Damages proof:** contracts, tax records, business records, client loss documentation, medical records, and expert damages analysis.
* **Prosecutorial-misconduct record:** Brady/Giglio evidence, investigator-conflict documentation, charging affidavits, hearing transcripts, DPA terms, bail records, and dismissal materials.
* **Advocacy posture:** whether civil-rights organizations can support record review, counsel referral, amicus-style research, public records requests, or pattern-and-practice analysis.

### Bottom line

The best-supported legal framing is:

1. **Tipp civil malpractice track:** ODC rejected the July 2025 disciplinary complaint, but missed-deadline and failure-to-communicate allegations remain potentially relevant to a civil malpractice intake.
2. **Lowney ODC track:** the April 28, 2026 prosecutorial-misconduct grievance remains under Montana ODC review and supplies a separate Brady/Giglio, First Amendment, and abuse-of-process record.
3. **Shared constitutional issue:** the DPA speech-removal or silence condition is the bridge between the two tracks because it may support both prosecutorial-misconduct analysis and defense-counsel malpractice analysis if the waiver was not voluntary, informed, narrowly tailored, and tied to legitimate public protection.
