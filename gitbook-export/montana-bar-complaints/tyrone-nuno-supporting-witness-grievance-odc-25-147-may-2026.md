## **V. VIOLATIONS OF THE MONTANA RULES OF PROFESSIONAL CONDUCT**

The conduct documented in Section IV constitutes violations of multiple Montana Rules of Professional Conduct. While Mr. Tipp's response to Elvis's grievance argues that eventual dismissal of charges demonstrates competent representation, Montana disciplinary proceedings operate under different standards than legal malpractice claims. The Montana Supreme Court has consistently held that the Rules of Professional Conduct "are designed to provide guidance to lawyers and structure for regulating conduct through disciplinary agencies," not to establish civil liability. [Carlson v. Morton, 745 P.2d 1133, 1136 (Mont. 1987\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/879401?expected=Carlson+v.+Morton%2C+745+P.2d+1133+%28Mont.+1987%29&found=Carlson+v.+Morton%2C+745+P.2d+1133%2C+1136+%28Mont.+1987%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false). Disciplinary violations focus on whether an attorney's conduct comports with mandatory ethical standards—not whether malpractice damages can be proven.

Montana courts have also established that the same conduct can support both disciplinary proceedings and civil litigation, and that a favorable outcome in civil proceedings does not immunize an attorney from disciplinary scrutiny. [Schuff v. A.T. Klemens & Son, 988 P.2d 502, 509-10 (Mont. 2000\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885205?expected=Schuff+v.+A.T.+Klemens+%26+Son%2C+16+P.3d+1002+%28Mont.+2000%29&found=Schuff+v.+A.T.+Klemens+%26+Son%2C+988+P.2d+502%2C+509-10+%28Mont.+2000%29&totalCitationsChecked=6&verified=false). The Montana Supreme Court retains "singular jurisdiction over lawyer discipline," and district courts addressing civil matters must refer potential ethical violations to the Commission on Practice for independent investigation. The critical inquiry here is whether Mr. Tipp's conduct during the 18-to-20-month representation met the mandatory standards governing Montana attorneys—not whether the case was ultimately dismissed.

### **A. Rule 1.1: Competence**

Rule 1.1 of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct requires that "\[a\] lawyer shall provide competent representation to a client." Competent representation requires the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation.

Mr. Tipp's failure to investigate my March 2018 complaint against Officer Ethan Smith and the resulting removal of Smith from the case constitutes a failure of competence. This was not obscure or difficult-to-discover evidence. I was Elvis's father, lived with him throughout the representation, attended in-office meetings with Mr. Tipp, and had personally filed a formal complaint that resulted in a supervising officer removing the investigating officer for impropriety. Yet Mr. Tipp never asked me a single question about the complaint, the harassment, the substance of my conversations with Sergeant DelRidge, or the reasons for Smith's removal.

In [Matter of Morris, No. 01-170 (Mont. Oct 12, 2001\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885695?verified=true), the Montana Supreme Court found violations of Rule 1.1 where an attorney "failed to provide competent representation" to his clients. The Court imposed a suspension of not less than one year, ordered fee refunds, and required training in law office management and client communication as a condition of reinstatement. Mr. Tipp's failure to interview an available witness with material exculpatory evidence—a witness who was present at case meetings—falls below the standard of thoroughness and preparation required for competent criminal defense representation.

Furthermore, Mr. Tipp's failure to investigate the potential conflicts of interest arising from Detective Brueckner's alleged service on the YWCA Board—the very organization at the center of the prosecution—after Smith's removal for impropriety demonstrates a lack of the legal knowledge and skill required to identify and challenge investigative misconduct. A competent criminal defense attorney confronted with mid-investigation replacement of a removed officer would investigate the replacement's background and potential institutional conflicts.

### **B. Rule 1.3: Diligence**

Rule 1.3 of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct provides that "\[a\] lawyer shall act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing a client." This rule requires not just activity, but meaningful advocacy directed toward the client's interests.

The Montana Supreme Court has recognized that failure to act with diligence during representation—even when a case is eventually resolved—constitutes sanctionable conduct. In [Matter of Morris, No. 01-170 (Mont. Oct 12, 2001\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885695?verified=true), the Court found that an attorney who "failed to act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing" his clients violated Rule 1.3, warranting suspension of not less than one year. Similarly, in [Matter of Halprin, 798 P.2d 80, 82 (Mont. 1990\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/1258624?expected=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80+%28Mont.+1990%29&found=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80%2C+82+%28Mont.+1990%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false), the Court found that an attorney who "failed to act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing his client" in a wrongful death case violated Rule 1.3, contributing to the Court's decision to impose disbarment.

Mr. Tipp's 18-to-20-month failure to pursue dismissal despite material evidence of investigative misconduct falls below the standard of diligence required by Rule 1.3. As documented in Section IV.F, the case came to an end only after my ex-wife and I directly demanded resolution—"a description of a family reaching its limits and forcing a conclusion that should have come much sooner." This is not reasonable diligence; it is passivity that prolonged my son's suffering, financial ruin, and constitutional deprivations for months beyond what competent advocacy would have required.

Moreover, Mr. Tipp's complete failure to investigate my complaint against Officer Smith—despite my repeated presence at meetings—demonstrates lack of diligence in pursuing material evidence favorable to the defense. The complaint was filed in March 2018, resulting in Smith's immediate removal. Yet Mr. Tipp took no action to interview me, obtain documentation of the complaint, investigate the reasons for removal, or challenge investigative integrity based on this evidence. This pattern of inaction over an 18-to-20-month period is inconsistent with the "reasonable diligence" mandate of Rule 1.3.

### **C. Rule 1.4: Communication**

Rule 1.4 of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct requires that "(a) A lawyer shall: (1) promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance with respect to which the client's informed consent... is required; (2) reasonably consult with the client about the means by which the client's objectives are to be accomplished; (3) keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter; \[and\] (4) promptly comply with reasonable requests for information."

Montana courts have repeatedly sanctioned attorneys for failure to maintain adequate communication with clients. In [Matter of Morris, No. 01-170 (Mont. Oct 12, 2001\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885695?verified=true), the Court found violations of Rule 1.4(a) where an attorney "failed to keep \[clients\] reasonably informed about the status of their matters, and failed to promptly comply with their reasonable requests for information," imposing a suspension of not less than one year. In [Matter of Halprin, 798 P.2d 80, 82 (Mont. 1990\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/1258624?expected=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80+%28Mont.+1990%29&found=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80%2C+82+%28Mont.+1990%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false), the Court found that an attorney who "failed to keep \[the client\] reasonably informed about the status of the legal matters handled by him" violated Rule 1.4(a), contributing to disbarment.

Mr. Tipp's conduct violated Rule 1.4 in multiple respects. First, as documented in Section IV.C, when Elvis raised legitimate and material concerns during the representation, "Mr. Tipp would dismiss or minimize them rather than engage with them seriously." This pattern was "consistent enough that it was noticeable to me as a lay observer with no legal training." An attorney who systematically dismisses client concerns rather than engaging with them is not "reasonably consult\[ing\] with the client about the means by which the client's objectives are to be accomplished."

Second, Mr. Tipp failed to communicate with the most obvious potential witness—the client's father, who attended meetings, lived with the client, and had filed the complaint that removed the investigating officer. Mr. Tipp's failure to interview me or even ask basic questions about my complaint demonstrates a complete breakdown in communication with persons directly connected to the defense.

Third, according to Elvis's Supplemental Submission \#3, Mr. Tipp pressured Elvis to surrender device passwords and relinquish First Amendment rights as conditions of the non-prosecution agreement—"matters my son describes as never having been adequately explained or fairly counseled to him." An attorney who pressures a client to waive constitutional rights without adequate explanation violates the duty to reasonably consult with the client and obtain informed consent.

### **D. Rule 1.7: Conflict of Interest**

Rule 1.7 of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct provides that "a lawyer shall not represent a client if the representation involves a concurrent conflict of interest," including where "there is a significant risk that the representation of one or more clients will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to... a third person or by a personal interest of the lawyer."

The Montana Supreme Court has held that conflicts of interest violate Rule 1.7 "whether or not 'actual prejudice' to \[the client's\] defense occurred as a result" of the conflicted representation. [Matter of Solomon Neuhardt, 321 P.3d 833, 840 (Mont. 2014\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/2659240?expected=Matter+of+Solomon+Neuhardt%2C+321+P.3d+833+%28Mont.+2014%29&found=Matter+of+Solomon+Neuhardt%2C+321+P.3d+833%2C+840+%28Mont.+2014%29&totalCitationsChecked=4&verified=false). In Neuhardt, the Court found that an attorney who represented two clients with materially adverse interests—where one client provided incriminating information against the other—violated Rule 1.7 even without proof of actual prejudice. The Court emphasized that the attorney "failed to appreciate the conflicting interests" and imposed a 90-day suspension and public censure.

Mr. Tipp's conduct demonstrates a conflict between his professional responsibilities to Elvis and his apparent interest in maintaining cooperative relationships with Missoula law enforcement and prosecutorial institutions. This conflict is evidenced by several facts documented in Section IV:

First, Mr. Tipp's systematic dismissal of legitimate client concerns and his pattern of being "rarely a forceful advocate for my son's position" suggests prioritization of institutional harmony over zealous advocacy. An attorney whose default posture is accommodation rather than challenge when confronted with evidence of investigative misconduct may be operating under a conflict between client interests and professional relationships with opposing parties.

Second, Mr. Tipp's use of me—the client's elderly father—as a pressure mechanism to force Elvis to submit to Mr. Tipp's preferred positions demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the duty of loyalty. As documented in Section IV.D, "when my son would push back on positions Mr. Tipp was taking, Mr. Tipp would at times bring me privately into his office and apply pressure on me to, in turn, pressure Elvis to submit to Mr. Tipp's demands." This tactic prioritized Mr. Tipp's litigation strategy over the client's autonomous decision-making, creating a conflict between Mr. Tipp's personal interest in maintaining control and his duty to defer to the client's objectives.

Third, Mr. Tipp's complete failure to investigate Detective Brueckner's alleged service on the YWCA Board—the organization at the center of the prosecution—after Smith's removal for conflicts suggests an unwillingness to challenge institutional relationships even when doing so would serve the client's interests. A defense attorney who fails to investigate obvious conflicts involving the investigating detective and the complaining organization may be constrained by his own professional relationships with those institutions.

Under the standard established in Neuhardt, proof of actual prejudice is not required. The question is whether Mr. Tipp's representation was "materially limited" by interests other than zealous advocacy for Elvis. The pattern of conduct documented here—systematic dismissal of client concerns, use of family pressure tactics, failure to investigate institutional conflicts, and prolonged passivity resulting in an 18-to-20-month ordeal—demonstrates precisely such a limitation.

### **E. Rule 8.4: Misconduct**

Rule 8.4 of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct provides that "\[i\]t is professional misconduct for a lawyer to: ... (c) engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation; \[or\] (d) engage in conduct that is prejudicial to the administration of justice."

Mr. Tipp's September 2025 response to Elvis's grievance characterizes my son's complaint as fabricated lies and extortion. Having been personally present throughout the representation and having direct knowledge of the events described, I can state unequivocally that this characterization is false.

I personally filed the complaint against Officer Smith in March 2018\. I personally witnessed Mr. Tipp's use of me as a pressure mechanism against Elvis. I personally observed Mr. Tipp's pattern of dismissing legitimate concerns. I personally experienced the 18-to-20-month ordeal that destroyed my son's career, finances, and mental health. Elvis's core allegations—that Mr. Tipp failed to listen, failed to investigate material evidence, accommodated institutional interests over zealous defense, and caused unjustifiable delay—are corroborated by my direct observations.

Characterizing a legitimate grievance supported by documentary evidence and witness testimony as "lies" and "extortion" when the attorney knows or should know that the client's father can corroborate the allegations constitutes dishonesty under Rule 8.4(c) and conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice under Rule 8.4(d). An attorney who responds to disciplinary complaints by attacking the complainant's character rather than addressing the substance of the allegations undermines the disciplinary process itself.

Moreover, the underlying conduct—failure to investigate exculpatory evidence, failure to interview available witnesses, use of family pressure tactics, and prolonged passivity causing severe client harm—itself constitutes conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice under Rule 8.4(d). Criminal defense representation that systematically fails to challenge investigative misconduct, ignores documented evidence of officer impropriety, and prioritizes institutional accommodation over client advocacy undermines public confidence in both the legal profession and the justice system.

## **VI. ANALOGOUS DISCIPLINARY AUTHORITY**

The conduct documented in this grievance—failure to investigate material evidence, failure to interview available witnesses, conflicts of interest prioritizing institutional relationships over client advocacy, and inadequate communication—finds direct support in Montana disciplinary precedent and analogous federal constitutional authority. This section demonstrates that Mr. Tipp's violations are neither novel nor minor, but fit squarely within established patterns of sanctionable attorney misconduct.

### **A. The Distinction Between Disciplinary Proceedings and Malpractice Claims**

As a threshold matter, Mr. Tipp's reliance on the eventual dismissal of charges as proof of adequate representation misunderstands the fundamental distinction between disciplinary proceedings and legal malpractice actions. The Montana Supreme Court has consistently held that ethical rules serve different purposes than tort standards and operate under different frameworks.

In [Carlson v. Morton, 745 P.2d 1133, 1136 (Mont. 1987\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/879401?expected=Carlson+v.+Morton%2C+745+P.2d+1133+%28Mont.+1987%29&found=Carlson+v.+Morton%2C+745+P.2d+1133%2C+1136+%28Mont.+1987%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false), the Court established that "the Canons of Professional Ethics and the later Model Rules of Professional Conduct have been used exclusively in disciplinary proceedings in Montana." The Court emphasized that the Preamble to the Model Rules explicitly provides: "Violation of a Rule should not give rise to a cause of action nor should it create any presumption that a legal duty has been breached. The Rules are designed to provide guidance to lawyers and to provide a structure for regulating conduct through disciplinary agencies. They are not designed to be a basis for civil liability."

The Court reaffirmed this principle in [Byers v. Cummings, 87 P.3d 465, 470 (Mont. 2004\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/886988?expected=Byers+v.+Cummings%2C+87+P.3d+465+%28Mont.+2004%29&found=Byers+v.+Cummings%2C+87+P.3d+465%2C+470+%28Mont.+2004%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false), holding that "Rules of Professional Conduct do not establish substantive legal duties" in malpractice cases and that "in a legal malpractice case a plaintiff must prove that the defendant breached a legal duty established through expert testimony, not a breach of various disciplinary rules."

These holdings establish that disciplinary proceedings focus on whether an attorney's conduct comports with mandatory professional standards—not whether civil damages can be proven. Critically, the same conduct can support both disciplinary action and civil litigation. In [Schuff v. A.T. Klemens & Son, 16 P.3d 1002, 1009-10 (Mont. 2000\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885205?expected=Schuff+v.+A.T.+Klemens+%26+Son%2C+16+P.3d+1002+%28Mont.+2000%29&found=Schuff+v.+A.T.+Klemens+%26+Son%2C+16+P.3d+1002%2C+1009-10+%28Mont.+2000%29&totalCitationsChecked=6&verified=false), the Court referred attorney conduct to the Commission on Practice for disciplinary investigation even after resolving the underlying civil dispute, emphasizing that "this Court's singular jurisdiction under Article VII, Section 2(3), of the Montana Constitution over matters pertaining to lawyer discipline and claimed violations of the M.R.Pro.C." requires independent scrutiny of ethical violations regardless of civil outcomes.

Applying these principles here, Mr. Tipp's argument that dismissal of criminal charges demonstrates competent representation is irrelevant to the disciplinary inquiry. The question is not whether Elvis can prove malpractice damages, but whether Mr. Tipp's conduct during the 18-to-20-month representation violated the mandatory ethical standards governing Montana attorneys. A favorable outcome does not immunize attorney misconduct from disciplinary scrutiny.

### **B. Montana Precedent Imposing Substantial Sanctions for Failure to Investigate, Failure to Communicate, and Lack of Diligence**

Montana disciplinary authorities have consistently imposed significant sanctions—including suspensions of one year or more—for attorney conduct involving failure to investigate material evidence, inadequate client communication, and lack of diligence. These cases establish both the sanctionability of Mr. Tipp's conduct and appropriate disciplinary measures.

In [Matter of Morris, No. 01-170 (Mont. Oct 12, 2001\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/885695?verified=true), the Montana Supreme Court adopted the Commission on Practice's findings that the attorney violated Rules 1.1 (Competence), 1.3 (Diligence), and 1.4(a) (Communication) when he "failed to provide competent representation," "failed to act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing" his clients, and "failed to keep \[clients\] reasonably informed about the status of their matters, and failed to promptly comply with their reasonable requests for information." The Court imposed a suspension of not less than one year, ordered refund of client fees, and required training in law office management and client communication as conditions of reinstatement.

The parallels to Mr. Tipp's conduct are direct. Like the attorney in Morris, Mr. Tipp failed to provide competent representation by ignoring material exculpatory evidence (my March 2018 complaint against Officer Smith), failed to act with reasonable diligence by allowing an 18-to-20-month prosecution to continue without pursuing dismissal based on investigative misconduct, and failed to maintain adequate communication by systematically dismissing client concerns and refusing to interview available witnesses. If Morris warranted a one-year suspension for these violations, Mr. Tipp's conduct—involving criminal defense representation with constitutional implications and quantifiable harm to the client—warrants comparable or greater sanctions.

In [Matter of Halprin, 798 P.2d 80, 82 (Mont. 1990\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/1258624?expected=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80+%28Mont.+1990%29&found=Matter+of+Halprin%2C+798+P.2d+80%2C+82+%28Mont.+1990%29&totalCitationsChecked=5&verified=false), the Court imposed disbarment where the attorney "failed to act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing his client" in a wrongful death case, "failed to keep \[the client\] reasonably informed about the status of the legal matters handled by him," "failed to keep records of client funds," and "leav\[ing\] his practice... without first protecting his client's interests, or giving reasonable notice." The Court emphasized that failure to investigate claims, failure to communicate with clients, and abandonment of client interests constitute severe ethical violations warranting the most serious sanctions.

While Mr. Tipp's conduct may not rise to the level of complete abandonment found in Halprin, the pattern of inadequate investigation, dismissal of client concerns, and prolonged passivity causing severe client harm places his violations within the spectrum of sanctionable conduct established by Montana precedent. The Court's willingness to impose disbarment for failure to investigate and failure to communicate demonstrates that these violations are treated with utmost seriousness—particularly where clients suffer significant harm as a result.

### **C. Montana Precedent on Conflicts of Interest in Criminal Defense Representation**

The Montana Supreme Court has held that conflicts of interest violate Rule 1.7 regardless of whether actual prejudice to the client can be proven. In [Matter of Solomon Neuhardt, 321 P.3d 833, 840 (Mont. 2014\)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/2659240?expected=Matter+of+Solomon+Neuhardt%2C+321+P.3d+833+%28Mont.+2014%29&found=Matter+of+Solomon+Neuhardt%2C+321+P.3d+833%2C+840+%28Mont.+2014%29&totalCitationsChecked=4&verified=false), the Court found that an attorney who represented both a husband and wife in a federal drug investigation—where the wife provided incriminating information against the husband—violated Rule 1.7 even without clear and convincing evidence of actual prejudice. The Court emphasized that the attorney "failed to appreciate the conflicting interests between husband and wife or to explain those conflicts to his clients."

The Court quoted U.S. Supreme Court authority establishing that "joint representation of conflicting interests is suspect because of what it tends to prevent the attorney from doing," including preventing an attorney from "challenging the admission of evidence prejudicial to one client but perhaps favorable to another." The Court held that "whether or not 'actual prejudice' to \[the client's\] defense occurred," the attorney violated Rule 1.7 by representing clients with materially adverse interests without proper disclosure or waiver.

The Court imposed a 90-day suspension and public censure, finding that the attorney's "display... of an inability to appreciate the existence of even a potential for conflict in his representation" demonstrated "a pattern of his lack of understanding of the rules governing a lawyer's conduct."

### **D. Federal Constitutional Authority as Analogous Persuasive Precedent**

While this is a disciplinary proceeding under Montana Rules of Professional Conduct rather than a constitutional ineffective assistance claim, federal authority establishing the Sixth Amendment duty to investigate provides persuasive guidance on the professional standards governing criminal defense representation.

The U.S. Supreme Court held in Strickland v. Strategic choices made after less than complete investigation are reasonable only to the extent that reasonable professional judgments support the limitations on investigation. In Wiggins v. Smith, the Court emphasized that defense counsel's failure to investigate available mitigating evidence—even when the case resulted in a conviction that might have occurred regardless—constituted deficient performance under the Sixth Amendment.

While Strickland and Wiggins address constitutional ineffectiveness rather than ethical violations, they illuminate the professional standard of care expected of criminal defense attorneys.
