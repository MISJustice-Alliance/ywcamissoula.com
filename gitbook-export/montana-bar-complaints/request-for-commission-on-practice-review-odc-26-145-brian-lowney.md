## I. Procedural basis and requested action

Pursuant to Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement ("MRLDE") Rule 10(C)(3) and Rule 14, Complainant Elvis R. Nuno submits this timely written request for review of the Office of Disciplinary Counsel's June 9, 2026 dismissal of ODC File No. 26-145, concerning attorney Brian C. Lowney.

ODC dismissed the grievance on a limitations ground only. The dismissal letter states that the "charges happened in 2019," that this was more than six years before filing, and that no facts supporting tolling under MRLDE 11(C) or an exception under MRLDE 11(D) were alleged.

Complainant respectfully requests that a Review Panel of the Commission on Practice disapprove or modify ODC's dismissal, direct further investigation, and require ODC to address the grievance under the correct Rule 11 framework and the substantive professional-conduct standards governing prosecutors.

This request is timely. ODC's June 9, 2026 letter states that review must be requested on or before July 9, 2026.

## II. Issues presented for review

1. Whether ODC erred by treating the grievance as a single 2019 charging event rather than evaluating discovery accrual, continuing-offense timing, tolling, and exceptions under MRLDE Rule 11.
2. Whether ODC's dismissal prematurely avoided substantive allegations that a Montana prosecutor maintained or resolved a criminal case in a manner inconsistent with probable cause, Brady/Giglio disclosure duties, and the prosecutor's duty to seek justice rather than victory.
3. Whether ODC erred by ignoring that Complainant did not discover the majority of the material facts concerning Detective Connie Brueckner's YWCA board conflict, the prosecution's reliance on Officer Ethan Smith's reports after Smith's removal, and the P.S. email forwarding/rerouting chain until June 2025 and afterward.
4. Whether the deferred prosecution agreement and later dismissal posture require scrutiny because the record alleges that dismissal leverage was used to suppress protected speech and shield institutional actors from accountability.
5. Whether ODC erred by failing to analyze the "no time limit" exception under MRLDE Rule 11(D)(2) where the grievance alleged prosecutorial misuse of state authority that may constitute felony-grade civil-rights deprivation or a related federal felony.

## III. ODC's limitations ruling applied the wrong legal frame

ODC's letter identifies the wrong question. The question is not simply when "charges happened." MRLDE Rule 11 asks when the alleged professional misconduct was discovered or should have been discovered, when every element of the violation occurred, whether the violation was continuing, whether tolling applies, and whether any exception removes the time bar.

The current Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement state that a formal complaint must be filed within six years from when the alleged misconduct "is discovered or, with due diligence, should have been discovered," subject to Rule 11's exceptions and tolling provisions. The same rule states that, for a continuing offense, the violation occurs when the offensive conduct ends. It also tolls the six-year period while civil, criminal, or administrative proceedings based on the same acts or circumstances are pending, and while the lawyer conceals facts about the violation. The rule further provides no time limit for alleged misconduct that constitutes a felony, regardless of whether the lawyer is charged or convicted. [Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement, No. AF 06-0628 (Mont. May 27, 2020)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/rules-for-lawyer-disciplinary-enforcement-4536816?utm_source=mcp&lines=192-202); see also [current Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement, Rule 11](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=213755).

ODC's one-sentence limitations analysis did not address:

* the discovery date for the alleged misconduct;
* whether later-discovered prosecutorial communications, investigator conflicts, witness-bias material, and DPA-related facts delayed accrual;
* whether the alleged failure to complete dismissal and respond to known retaliation constituted continuing misconduct;
* whether related administrative proceedings involving the same acts and circumstances tolled the period; or
* whether alleged civil-rights deprivation or obstruction conduct implicated Rule 11(D)'s felony exception.

That omission matters because ODC's letter did not reject the grievance on credibility, evidentiary insufficiency, or lack of professional-duty violation. It rejected the matter because of a date assumption. The Review Panel should correct that threshold error and require ODC to evaluate the record under the complete rule.

## IV. The grievance alleged later-discovered and continuing misconduct, not merely a 2019 event

The grievance is not limited to the date charges were filed. It alleges a course of prosecutorial conduct that includes the charging affidavit, the continued maintenance of charges after exculpatory and impeachment information existed, the deferred prosecution agreement, delayed or incomplete dismissal after the DPA period, and a failure to act on known retaliation during and after the speech-restriction period.

The grievance also identifies categories of evidence whose significance could not reasonably be evaluated until later case-file assembly: Officer Ethan Smith's removal for an appearance of impropriety, Detective Connie Brueckner's YWCA board conflict, E'Lise Chard's sworn admissions at the protection-order hearing, the P.S. email entering prosecution-adjacent channels, and communications between defense counsel and the County Attorney's Office about dismissal and ongoing harassment.

At a minimum, those allegations required ODC to ask when Complainant discovered, or with due diligence should have discovered, the facts constituting Lowney's alleged professional misconduct. ODC's letter instead treated "charges happened in 2019" as dispositive. That is not the Rule 11 inquiry.

### Continuing conduct identified by the grievance

The grievance and supporting case files identify continuing conduct in at least four forms:

1. **Continuing case control and delayed dismissal.** A prosecutor's control of a Montana criminal action runs "from the time it begins until it ends," subject to restrictions imposed by law. [State Ex Rel. Fletcher v. District Court of Nineteenth Judicial District, 859 P.2d 992 (Mont. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-ex-rel-fletcher-v-2613197?utm_source=mcp&lines=31-34). If the DPA period lapsed and dismissal was required, delayed action after notice was not merely a historical charging event.
2. **Continuing suppression or non-correction of favorable information.** The grievance alleges non-disclosure of material impeachment and exculpatory information that remained relevant throughout the prosecution and resolution period.
3. **Continuing effect of the DPA speech condition.** The alleged speech-removal and silence condition operated after the charging event and during the deferred period, while third parties allegedly continued public and private retaliation.
4. **Later-discovered facts.** The full significance of the prosecutor's role depended on case-file records and third-party materials that were not reasonably available to Complainant at the time ODC treated as dispositive.

For Rule 11 purposes, these allegations required investigation or at least a reasoned limitations analysis. They could not be dismissed by characterizing the entire grievance as "charges happened in 2019."

## V. June 2025 discovery of essential facts independently defeats ODC's time-bar premise

The strongest limitations answer is factual and narrow: Complainant did not possess the essential facts supporting the Lowney grievance until June 2025 and afterward. The grievance was not based merely on dissatisfaction with charges from 2018-2019. It was based on later-assembled evidence showing how the prosecution allegedly used, failed to disclose, or failed to correct material facts that were not reasonably discoverable by Complainant earlier.

Montana discovery-rule authority supports this framing. The Montana Supreme Court has stated that a claim does not accrue until the facts constituting the claim have been discovered, or with due diligence should have been discovered, when the facts are concealing by their nature or the defendant's conduct prevents discovery. [Lake County v. State, 559 P.3d 1263 (Mont. 2024)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lake-county-v-state-10753814?utm_source=mcp&lines=205-209). Montana also recognizes equitable tolling where the nature of the defendant's actions concealed the claim, even if the concealment does not rise to intentional fraud, so long as the plaintiff was actually prevented from filing despite the diligence reasonably expected in the circumstances. [Schoof v. Nesbit, 316 P.3d 831 (Mont. 2014)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/schoof-v-nesbit-2648684?utm_source=mcp&lines=239-242).

ODC's letter did not ask when Complainant discovered the essential facts. That omission is decisive. Under Montana law, the limitations inquiry turns on the facts essential to the claim, not merely the later legal theory. [Town of Clyde Park v. Younkin, 99 P.3d 196 (Mont. 2004)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/town-of-clyde-park-v-886779?utm_source=mcp&lines=37-41). The June 2025-and-afterward record development supplied those essential facts.

### A. Brueckner/YWCA conflict was not an obvious public fact during the prosecution

Complainant did not know during the criminal case that Detective Connie Brueckner, who assumed a central investigative role after Officer Smith, allegedly served as a YWCA board member while investigating a matter involving a YWCA employee and Complainant's protected complaint against YWCA. That fact is not a legal conclusion; it is an essential fact supporting the Brady/Giglio, probable-cause, and conflict-of-interest allegations.

The current GitBook record treats this as a primary verification point, not a mere inference. See [YWCA Brueckner conflict: allegations summary](../allegations-against-ywca-of-missoula-board-member-detective-connie-brueckner-pat.md), [Legal Analysis: YWCA of Missoula, Board Conflicts, and Police Integration](../legal-analysis-ywca-of-missoula,-board-conflicts,-and-police-integration.md), and [Connie Brueckner investigative research report](../additional-evidence-and-documentation/connie-brueckner-investigative-report.md).

Because this conflict was allegedly undisclosed by law enforcement, the prosecution, and defense counsel, it is exactly the kind of concealed or self-concealing fact that makes ODC's single-date analysis unreliable. It was not enough that Complainant knew he had been charged. He needed to know that a conflicted investigator allegedly helped build the case, that the conflict connected directly to the complaining witness's institution, and that the conflict was not disclosed in the criminal proceeding.

### B. The prosecution's reliance on Smith after removal became clear only after later case-file assembly

The Lowney grievance also depends on when Complainant discovered the depth of the prosecution's reliance on Officer Ethan Smith's reports after Smith was allegedly removed from the case for an obvious appearance of impropriety. Smith's removal is relevant in itself, but the limitations issue turns on the later-discovered combination: removal plus prosecutorial reliance.

The published record now separately documents the Smith complaint and timeline. See [MPD Officer Misconduct: 2018 Complaint — Officer Ethan Smith](../officer-ethan-smith-complaint-march-2018.md), [MPD Officer Misconduct: Ethan Smith Timeline](../mpd-officer-misconduct-ethan-smith-timeline.md), and [Appendix A: Comparison of Officer Smith vs. Detective Brueckner Conflicts](./mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-bryan-tipp-of-tipp-colburn-lockwood-p.c.-july/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-supplemental-submission-1-august-2025/appendix-a-comparison-of-officer-smith-vs.-detective-brueckner-conflicts.md).

This is not a new legal theory layered onto old facts. It is the later discovery of facts essential to the disciplinary theory: that the prosecutor's charging affidavit allegedly depended on work from an officer whose conflict had already been identified and acted upon. Under the discovery-rule cases, that is the relevant question.

### C. The P.S. email became material when the forwarding chain and case posture were reconstructed

The P.S. email is also a later-discovered essential fact. The issue is not simply that a favorable character-reference email existed. The issue is how it moved through YWCA and prosecution-adjacent channels, how it was reframed, and whether anyone recognized it as favorable, impeachment, or credibility evidence.

The public addendum states that P.S. sent an email to YWCA on August 14, 2018, favorable to Complainant and critical of E'Lise Chard; E'Lise Chard forwarded it to Detective Arianna Adams and Kim Harvey with language characterizing it as being "on Elvis' behalf" while acknowledging uncertainty; and Kim Harvey forwarded the chain again on August 23, 2018. See [Brian Lowney Prosecution Addendum](../additional-evidence-and-documentation/ps-email-addendum-august-2018/ps-brian-lowney-addendum-public.md) and [Core Exhibit Analysis: P.S. Email and Forwarding Chain](../additional-evidence-and-documentation/ps-email-addendum-august-2018/ps-email-addendum-public.md).

This should be framed as rerouting/non-disclosure and mishandling, not overstated as proof that Lowney personally possessed the email on a specific date without a full discovery log. But that restraint strengthens the review request: the record shows enough to require investigation into whether favorable information entered the prosecution ecosystem, whether it was reframed as suspicious rather than favorable, and why it was not used to correct the prosecution narrative.

### D. ODC should have treated discovery timing as a fact question requiring investigation

Montana authority recognizes that when evidence conflicts about when a claimant discovered the facts constituting a claim, the limitations question can be factual rather than a one-line legal disposition. [Nelson v. Nelson, 50 P.3d 139 (Mont. 2002)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/nelson-v-nelson-886116?utm_source=mcp&lines=97). The Review Panel should therefore reject ODC's unsupported assumption that the limitations period began merely because criminal charges existed years earlier.

The appropriate finding is limited: the Review Panel need not decide now that every allegation is timely on the merits. It should find that ODC could not dismiss the grievance without addressing the June 2025-and-afterward discovery of the Brueckner conflict, the Smith-removal reliance pattern, and the P.S. email handling.

## VI. Montana law supports investigation of the prosecutor-duty allegations

The Review Panel should not treat the grievance as an attempt to relitigate a criminal case. The grievance asks whether a Montana lawyer acting as a prosecutor complied with professional duties.

The Montana Supreme Court has expressly recognized that prosecutors, as members of the bar, must obey the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct, including the rule prohibiting prosecution of a charge the prosecutor knows is unsupported by probable cause. [Montana Ass'n of Counties v. State Ex Rel. Fox, 404 P.3d 733 (Mont. 2017)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/montana-ass-n-of-counties-4216916?utm_source=mcp&lines=265-268). The Court has also recognized that a prosecutor may not seek victory at the expense of constitutional rights and must seek justice rather than simply an indictment or conviction. [State Ex Rel. Fletcher v. District Court of Nineteenth Judicial District, 859 P.2d 992 (Mont. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-ex-rel-fletcher-v-2613197?utm_source=mcp&lines=38-41).

The probable-cause issue is not abstract. Montana law requires probable cause before a judge may approve a prosecutor's application for leave to file an information. [State v. Partain, 567 P.3d 932 (Mont. 2025)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-partain-10873593?utm_source=mcp&lines=180-183). Fletcher adds that, once charges are instituted, a prosecutor must reveal to the court information negating probable cause. [State Ex Rel. Fletcher v. District Court of Nineteenth Judicial District, 859 P.2d 992 (Mont. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-ex-rel-fletcher-v-2613197?utm_source=mcp&lines=42).

Those principles directly support Commission review. The grievance alleged that Lowney's charging affidavit and prosecution posture relied on materials from an officer removed for an appearance of impropriety, failed to account for a municipal-court protected-speech ruling, failed to disclose investigator conflicts and witness admissions, and continued a prosecution that allegedly lacked a non-tainted basis. If those allegations are true, they implicate Rule 3.8(a), Rule 3.8(d), Rule 3.4(a), and Rule 8.4(d). ODC did not reach that analysis.

## VII. Brady/Giglio authority supports investigation of the Smith, Brueckner, and YWCA-conflict evidence

The grievance's Brady/Giglio theory is not speculative legal rhetoric. Montana and federal cases recognize that favorable evidence includes impeachment evidence, bias evidence, interest evidence, and information held by law enforcement or others acting on the government's behalf.

In Montana, prosecutors have a duty to learn of favorable evidence known to others acting on the government's behalf, including law enforcement. [State v. Chavis, 440 P.3d 640 (Mont. 2019)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-chavis-6536120?utm_source=mcp&lines=61-63). Montana also recognizes that Brady evidence includes favorable material and that impeachment evidence may include a witness's expectation, bias, or interest. [G. Temple v. State, 573 P.3d 1200 (Mont. 2025)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/g-temple-v-state-11124152?utm_source=mcp&lines=61-62); [G. Temple v. State, 573 P.3d 1200 (Mont. 2025)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/g-temple-v-state-11124152?utm_source=mcp&lines=111-118).

The grievance alleges at least four categories of favorable or impeachment information:

1. **Officer Ethan Smith's removal.** If Smith was removed because of an "obvious appearance of impropriety" arising from his relationship with E'Lise Chard, that removal was impeachment and investigation-integrity evidence. A charging affidavit relying on Smith's reports made that information material to probable cause and credibility.
2. **Detective Connie Brueckner's YWCA board role.** A detective investigating conduct centered on a YWCA employee and a complaint against YWCA while serving as a YWCA board member had an institutional interest or bias issue that warranted disclosure and review.
3. **E'Lise Chard's sworn hearing admissions.** If the complaining witness stated under oath that no meaningful contact or threatening communications occurred, that evidence bore directly on the stalking elements and on probable cause.
4. **The P.S. email and forwarding chain.** A third-party letter favorable to Complainant and critical of E'Lise Chard appears to have entered prosecution-adjacent channels; whether it was recognized, disclosed, or improperly reframed is a proper investigative question.

ODC's dismissal did not address these categories. A limitations disposition that ignores whether the alleged Brady/Giglio evidence was concealed, later discovered, or part of a continuing prosecution process is incomplete.

## VIII. The DPA speech condition requires public-policy and prosecutorial-misconduct review

The grievance alleges that dismissal was conditioned on Complainant removing or suppressing speech concerning institutional misconduct even though Judge Warren had already ruled that the YWCA complaint letter was protected First Amendment speech. That allegation warrants review because courts closely scrutinize government agreements that exchange dismissal or settlement benefits for waiver of constitutional or civil-rights accountability.

The United States Supreme Court does not hold that every release-dismissal agreement is void, but it does require attention to voluntariness, prosecutorial misconduct, and public policy. A release-dismissal agreement is enforceable only where it is voluntary, there is no evidence of prosecutorial misconduct, and enforcement would not adversely affect relevant public interests. [Town of Newton v. Rumery, 480 U.S. 386 (1987)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/town-of-newton-v-rumery-111837?utm_source=mcp&lines=131). The Ninth Circuit has held that a prosecutor may not condition voluntary dismissal of a criminal charge on a stipulation designed to forestall the defendant's civil case. [Hugh Wendell MacDonald v. James A. Musick, 425 F.2d 373 (9th Cir. 1970)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hugh-wendell-macdonald-v-james-289819?utm_source=mcp&lines=34).

The same public-policy concern applies with special force where the alleged condition suppresses speech about institutional misconduct. Before government may require a citizen to surrender a constitutional right as part of a settlement or contract, it must have a legitimate reason for including the waiver in that particular agreement. [Thomas Davies v. Grossmont Union High School District, 930 F.2d 1390 (9th Cir. 1991)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/thomas-davies-v-grossmont-union-559562?utm_source=mcp&lines=154). A legitimate reason will ordinarily require a close nexus between the government's specific interest and the specific right waived. [Thomas Davies v. Grossmont Union High School District, 930 F.2d 1390 (9th Cir. 1991)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/thomas-davies-v-grossmont-union-559562?utm_source=mcp&lines=155).

The Review Panel need not decide the ultimate constitutional issue now. It need only recognize that the grievance alleged a serious professional-duty question: whether a prosecutor used the threat or burden of continued prosecution to obtain suppression of speech and practical insulation from civil-rights accountability. That question should not have been avoided through a one-line limitations ruling.

## IX. Protected-speech retaliation and selective enforcement are proper investigative subjects

Prosecutorial discretion is broad, but it is not unlimited. The Ninth Circuit recently reaffirmed that prosecution may not be deliberately based on an unjustifiable standard, including the exercise of protected statutory and constitutional rights. [United States v. Nathan Wilson, 123 F.4th 1021 (9th Cir. 2024)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-nathan-wilson-10763727?utm_source=mcp&lines=146).

The grievance does not ask ODC to adjudicate a federal selective-prosecution claim. It asks ODC to investigate whether Lowney's professional conduct crossed ethical lines where the prosecution allegedly:

* relied on speech a municipal judge had already identified as protected;
* escalated stalking charges in response to complaints about YWCA conduct;
* failed to disclose investigator and witness conflicts tied to the YWCA;
* imposed a DPA condition suppressing the same protected speech; and
* failed to address retaliatory harassment during the DPA silence period.

Those facts, if substantiated, would support a professional-discipline inquiry even if a court later applied a different standard to a civil-rights claim.

## X. The nuclear option: MRLDE Rule 11(D)(2)'s no-limit felony exception

ODC also failed to address the most serious Rule 11 exception. MRLDE Rule 11 provides no time limit for alleged misconduct that constitutes a felony, regardless of whether the lawyer is criminally charged, prosecuted, or convicted. [Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement, No. AF 06-0628 (Mont. May 27, 2020)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/rules-for-lawyer-disciplinary-enforcement-4536816?utm_source=mcp&lines=192-202); see also [current Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement, Rule 11](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=213755).

This is a preservation and review argument. The Review Panel need not decide that Lowney committed a federal felony. It should decide only that ODC could not dismiss the grievance without analyzing whether the alleged facts, if proven, trigger Rule 11(D)(2). The grievance alleges that a prosecutor used criminal-case dismissal leverage to require suppression of speech that a municipal judge had already recognized as protected First Amendment activity. If that allegation is substantiated, it is not merely a contract or plea-negotiation issue. It is a potential deprivation-of-rights issue under color of law.

Section 242 criminalizes willful action under color of law that deprives a person of rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. [18 U.S.C. § 242](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title18-section242). The Supreme Court describes the statute as requiring action that is willful, under color of law, and deprives a person of federally protected rights. [United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259 (1997)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-lanier-118098?utm_source=mcp&lines=38). The specific-intent requirement is demanding: the actor must intend to deprive a person of a federal right made definite by the Constitution, statute, or decisions interpreting them, or act in reckless disregard of that right. [Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/screws-v-united-states-104135?utm_source=mcp&lines=132-138); [United States v. Juan Dale Reese, United States of America v. Scott Matthew Dwyer, United States of America v. Daniel Wayne Broussard, United States of America v. Larry Marcel Houston, 2 F.3d 870 (9th Cir. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-juan-dale-628300?utm_source=mcp&lines=210-214).

Lanier is important because it rejects an artificially narrow notice rule. A § 242 theory does not require a prior case with identical facts if the unlawfulness is apparent in light of preexisting law; general constitutional rules can give fair warning when they apply with obvious clarity to specific conduct. [United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259 (1997)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-lanier-118098?utm_source=mcp&lines=79-81); [United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259 (1997)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-lanier-118098?utm_source=mcp&lines=102). The alleged sequence here is unusually concrete for fair-warning purposes: a court had already ruled the YWCA complaint speech constitutionally protected, yet the prosecution allegedly used dismissal leverage to require removal or suppression of the same protected speech.

First Amendment retaliation doctrine supplies the right at issue. The Supreme Court recognizes that, as a general matter, government officials may not subject a person to retaliatory action for protected speech. [Nieves v. Bartlett, 587 U.S. 391 (2019)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/nieves-v-bartlett-9226038?utm_source=mcp&lines=67-69). In retaliatory-prosecution cases, protected speech, retaliatory motive, causation, and lack of probable cause are central because official reprisal through criminal prosecution is a recognized constitutional injury. [Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250 (2006)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hartman-v-moore-145662?utm_source=mcp&lines=45); [Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250 (2006)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hartman-v-moore-145662?utm_source=mcp&lines=114). The Ninth Circuit likewise applies an objective chilling standard and recognizes that a plaintiff states First Amendment retaliation by alleging protected activity, adverse action that would chill a person of ordinary firmness, and protected activity as a substantial or motivating factor. [Jonathan Capp v. County of San Diego, 940 F.3d 1046 (9th Cir. 2019)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/jonathan-capp-v-county-of-4444434?utm_source=mcp&lines=95-104); [Jonathan Capp v. County of San Diego, 940 F.3d 1046 (9th Cir. 2019)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/jonathan-capp-v-county-of-4444434?utm_source=mcp&lines=133-141).

The felony-exception analysis must be framed precisely. The base form of § 242 carries a maximum of one year, while felony-grade penalties attach when the statutory aggravating facts are present. [18 U.S.C. § 242](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title18-section242). That caveat does not excuse ODC's omission. It means ODC had to address whether the alleged misuse of prosecution power, together with any related civil-rights, conspiracy, obstruction, or deprivation facts in the record, constituted felony misconduct for Rule 11(D)(2) purposes. ODC did not do that. It instead stated only that no Rule 11(D) exception was alleged.

The Review Panel should therefore require ODC to analyze Rule 11(D)(2) expressly. If the conduct alleged constitutes felony-grade deprivation of rights under color of law, conspiracy against rights, obstruction, or another federal felony, Rule 11 imposes no disciplinary time limit. If ODC believes the felony exception does not apply, it should state why, identify the elements it found missing, and explain that conclusion against the case-file allegations rather than resolving the issue by characterizing the matter as a stale 2019 charge.

## XI. ODC's comparison to a single 2019 charge date is factually incomplete

The dismissal letter's statement that "your charges happened in 2019" is not only legally incomplete; it appears factually imprecise. The underlying prosecution materials in the published grievance record identify a September 25, 2018 charging affidavit, a deferred prosecution period, and dismissal-related events extending into 2020-2021. The grievance also alleges later-discovered facts and continuing consequences tied to the prosecutor's handling of dismissal and the DPA conditions.

Even if ODC intended to refer generally to the criminal case period, Rule 11 does not permit the analysis to stop there. The Review Panel should require ODC to identify:

1. what specific alleged violation ODC treated as accruing in 2019;
2. why later alleged conduct was excluded;
3. why discovery accrual was not applied to concealed or later-assembled evidence;
4. why tolling under Rule 11(C)(3) and 11(C)(4) did not apply; and
5. why Rule 11(D)'s felony exception did not require analysis of the alleged civil-rights deprivation theory.

Without those findings, the dismissal cannot be meaningfully reviewed.

## XII. Substantive allegations requiring investigation

ODC's letter did not reject the grievance on the merits. If the Review Panel corrects the limitations error, the following substantive allegations warrant investigation:

### A. Probable cause and meritorious charging

The grievance alleges that the stalking theory depended on conduct already identified as protected speech, speculative technical assumptions, and third-party impressions unsupported by forensic evidence. It further alleges that sworn statements by the complaining witness undercut the required course-of-conduct and fear elements.

Under Montana authority, prosecutors are subject to the Rules of Professional Conduct and must not prosecute charges they know lack probable cause. [Montana Ass'n of Counties v. State Ex Rel. Fox, 404 P.3d 733 (Mont. 2017)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/montana-ass-n-of-counties-4216916?utm_source=mcp&lines=265-268). That makes the grievance's probable-cause allegations squarely disciplinary in nature.

### B. Disclosure of evidence negating probable cause

The grievance alleges that Lowney or the County Attorney's Office failed to disclose or account for evidence that negated probable cause, including Smith's removal, Brueckner's YWCA conflict, and Chard's hearing admissions. Fletcher states that a prosecutor must reveal to the court information negating probable cause after charges are instituted. [State Ex Rel. Fletcher v. District Court of Nineteenth Judicial District, 859 P.2d 992 (Mont. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-ex-rel-fletcher-v-2613197?utm_source=mcp&lines=42).

### C. Brady/Giglio disclosure

The grievance alleges that impeachment and exculpatory evidence was not disclosed or not treated as favorable. Montana recognizes that prosecutors must learn favorable evidence known to law enforcement, [State v. Chavis, 440 P.3d 640 (Mont. 2019)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-v-chavis-6536120?utm_source=mcp&lines=61-63), and that impeachment evidence includes bias and expected-benefit evidence. [G. Temple v. State, 573 P.3d 1200 (Mont. 2025)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/g-temple-v-state-11124152?utm_source=mcp&lines=111-118).

### D. DPA speech waiver and public-policy harm

The grievance alleges that the DPA imposed speech-removal and silence conditions as the price of resolving charges. Under Rumery, MacDonald, and Davies, a government-demanded waiver of constitutional or accountability rights requires close scrutiny for voluntariness, public-policy harm, legitimate government interest, and prosecutorial misconduct. [Town of Newton v. Rumery, 480 U.S. 386 (1987)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/town-of-newton-v-rumery-111837?utm_source=mcp&lines=131); [Hugh Wendell MacDonald v. James A. Musick, 425 F.2d 373 (9th Cir. 1970)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hugh-wendell-macdonald-v-james-289819?utm_source=mcp&lines=32); [Thomas Davies v. Grossmont Union High School District, 930 F.2d 1390 (9th Cir. 1991)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/thomas-davies-v-grossmont-union-559562?utm_source=mcp&lines=154).

### E. Failure to complete dismissal and respond to known retaliation

The grievance alleges that Lowney received notice that the deferred prosecution period had lapsed and that Complainant was being subjected to harassment or retaliation, yet failed to act promptly to dismiss or to address that conduct. Because Fletcher recognizes the prosecutor's control of a criminal case from beginning to end, the post-DPA dismissal period is part of the prosecutor-duty analysis, not a collateral afterthought. [State Ex Rel. Fletcher v. District Court of Nineteenth Judicial District, 859 P.2d 992 (Mont. 1993)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/state-ex-rel-fletcher-v-2613197?utm_source=mcp&lines=31-34).
