# A Deal Without a Finish Line: The Catch-22 Behind Edmonds’ Latest Terms

*Elvis Nuno — MISJustice Alliance | Updated October 22, 2026*

The earlier open letter asked Washington a basic question: **What remains to be done, under what authority, and what will finally end the case?** The newly reviewed correspondence makes that question more urgent, not less. It identifies conditions the Edmonds prosecutor says remain outstanding, but it does not supply a complete accounting of prior compliance, a definite agreement, or a court-approved promise that completing the proposed conditions will end every obligation in the Edmonds matter.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

Elvis Ryland Nuno is seeking a defined resolution of an Edmonds, Washington, probation matter while living in Montana and caring for an elderly father. The dispute concerns electronic home monitoring (EHM), mental-health documentation, outside warrants, and the consequences of guilty pleas he says were obtained under coercive circumstances. The public-interest question is whether the government has fairly accounted for what he already did and clearly stated what remains.

The central defense argument deserves to be stated directly: **if Mr. Nuno reasonably believed that revealing an officer’s alleged threat would cause a widowed mother to lose her only child, denying coercion could have been part of the coercion’s effect.** A contradiction between that denial and a later account is significant evidence. It is not, by itself, an answer to whether fear produced the denial.

A second problem concerns the proposed resolution. In the correspondence, prosecutor Aaron Walls identifies remaining obligations but offers no definite commitment that the defense’s proposed performance will resolve every known probation issue. Mr. Nuno also identifies prior monitoring and an earlier evaluation whose existence is independently supported by the evaluator’s own 2016 correspondence. The fair question is what those acts satisfied, what proof remains missing, and what precisely will end this particular case.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

## The proceedings and the proposed resolution

The correspondence describes an Edmonds probation matter in warrant status. On January 21, 2026, public defender Paolo Georgiades sought a remote warrant-quash appearance, citing Mr. Nuno’s Montana residence, transportation and work constraints, and care for his father following surgery. On January 23, Walls expressed no objection to a Zoom appearance.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

On February 4, the prosecutor identified 60 days of EHM, proof of mental-health treatment, and action on other warrants as outstanding. He also described a 120-day jail position and closure “absent new information,” while linking his willingness to quash the Edmonds warrant to an effort to address another warrant. The record does not establish that these statements constituted an agreed disposition.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

On September 29, Georgiades proposed 60 days of out-of-state EHM and mental-health records as sufficient to close probation. He also raised the age of the proceedings, caregiving hardship, difficulty retrieving an earlier evaluation, and an allegation that an officer threatened a witness’s custody of her child. Walls questioned the coercion account, including whether Mr. Nuno was “lying now or lying then” given his plea-stage denial of threats. His response did not expressly accept the proposed resolution or provide a complete accounting of prior compliance.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

This article concerns Mr. Nuno’s **September 2019 Edmonds plea** and the present dispute over its resolution. His notarized declaration places the alleged child-custody threat before that plea and describes his decision to plead as an effort to protect the threatened mother and child.[[2]](#sources-and-authorities)

## The Catch-22 the credibility question leaves unanswered

### A sworn account, not merely a negotiating assertion

Mr. Nuno set out the Catch-22 allegation in § XIV of his **notarized sworn declaration**, executed December 10, 2025. The signature section declares the account true to the best of his knowledge, information, and belief under penalty of perjury and acknowledges potential prosecution for false information. The following page records an online notarization. The declaration thus predates the September 2026 negotiation and places his account in a formal, accountable form.[[2]](#sources-and-authorities)

That establishes that Mr. Nuno made the allegation under oath; it does not establish that the officer made the threat. His description of his own knowledge, fear, and reason for pleading is firsthand evidence of his claimed state of mind. Proof of the officer’s words depends on whether he personally heard them, what the threatened witness can say, and corroborating records. Notarization verifies the formal sworn act, not the truth of every assertion or legal conclusion.

The declaration identifies the witness as his then-partner, a widowed single mother with one young child, and states that she could have given favorable defense testimony. It alleges that an Edmonds officer threatened custody loss and permanent separation unless she cooperated or testified against him. It also offers to identify her to investigators with safeguards against retaliation.[[2]](#sources-and-authorities)

The Catch-22 described in Mr. Nuno’s sworn account turns on **uncertainty about devastating harm**, not certainty that the officer would carry out the alleged threat. He says he believed revealing the coercion in court could expose a widowed single mother and her only child to immediate retaliation and custody separation. He did not have to know that removal was inevitable—or that the officer possessed lawful authority to accomplish it—to fear that disclosure could put them in danger.[[2]](#sources-and-authorities)

The stakes were profoundly unequal. On his account, admitting the threat meant risking catastrophic harm to two innocent people who had no responsibility for his plea decision. Remaining silent meant bearing the consequences himself: surrendering trial rights and accepting a plea he maintains was false. A reasonable person motivated by concern for others could regard that personal sacrifice as preferable to gambling with a vulnerable mother’s relationship with her only child.

**Even a risk believed to be small could weigh heavily when the possible consequence was permanent family separation.** The point is not to assign the threat a numerical probability; no such probability has been established. It is that uncertainty offers little reassurance to someone who believes another person’s family could be destroyed if he guesses wrong. A widowed mother and her child, already deprived of one parent, would bear a consequence he could not undo.

This protective motive is central to the defense explanation for the plea-stage denial. The prosecutor’s reported question—whether Mr. Nuno was lying then or now—identifies a contradiction but does not address why he says disclosure appeared dangerous. **The denial cannot fairly be evaluated without considering the alleged threat that he says made truthful disclosure seem unsafe.**

### Washington authority directly addresses the denial-of-coercion argument

Washington’s Supreme Court addressed this precise logical point in *State v. Frederick*: a denial of improper influence during a plea hearing is strong evidence of voluntariness, but it does not foreclose later proof of coercion. The court permitted the defendant to present coercion evidence in the habitual-offender proceeding before it, while emphasizing the heavy burden of overcoming the plea record. The decision was later overruled on other grounds concerning its separate habitual-offender analysis; its coercion discussion must be cited with that history disclosed.[[3]](#sources-and-authorities)

In *State v. Osborne*, the same court reaffirmed that principle but denied relief because the defendant offered only an unsupported allegation against repeated assurances of voluntariness. Together, the cases support both sides: a sworn account deserves evidentiary evaluation, and an oath alone does not necessarily overcome the plea record.[[4]](#sources-and-authorities)

In *Blackledge v. Allison*, the Supreme Court recognized the substantial presumption supporting statements made during a plea proceeding while rejecting an absolute rule that would foreclose contrary claims involving duress or misrepresentation. The Court also permitted rejection of conclusory or incredible allegations. Its decision concerned federal habeas review on a particular record; it does not guarantee Mr. Nuno a hearing in a Washington court.[[5]](#sources-and-authorities)

*Lynumn v. Illinois* supplies a close factual analogy concerning the nature of the alleged pressure. Police threatened a mother with loss of her children and financial assistance if she failed to cooperate; the Court held her resulting confession involuntary under the circumstances. **The person threatened there was the confessing defendant.** Applying its reasoning to Mr. Nuno requires additional proof connecting the threat against another person to his plea.[[6]](#sources-and-authorities)

*Machibroda v. United States* further illustrates why allegations concerning events outside a courtroom cannot always be resolved from the courtroom record alone. Its federal statutory setting and detailed allegations limit the analogy.[[7]](#sources-and-authorities)

Washington’s CrRLJ 4.2(d), (f), and (g) requires voluntariness, provides for withdrawal to correct manifest injustice, and includes a declaration addressing threats to others. The actual form and colloquy used in each case must be obtained; the current model form is no substitute for the historical record.[[8]](#sources-and-authorities)

### The substantial counterargument

The City may fairly insist that an accepted plea and contemporaneous denial carry substantial weight. Washington’s *State v. Cameron* recognizes the need for special care with third-party plea arrangements, but rejected an insufficiently supported coercion claim involving charges against the defendant’s wife. A difficult choice involving another person is not necessarily unlawful coercion.[[9]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno states that supporting evidence can be provided, but disclosure implicates the safety of the same vulnerable mother and child whom he says he sought to protect. **The defense therefore asks the court to establish express protections before requiring disclosure of their identities or sensitive evidence.** Requiring exposure first and addressing protection afterward would recreate the very dilemma described in his sworn declaration.[[2]](#sources-and-authorities)

The requested process should begin with counsel presenting the protection request without publicly identifying the mother or child. The court should then determine what lawful safeguards can be ordered, including restricted access to identifying information, an appropriately authorized confidential submission, limits on use and further disclosure, and a defined procedure for reporting and addressing alleged retaliation. Any protective order should specify its scope, who is bound, and how it will be enforced.

The defense’s position is that meaningful protection must precede sensitive disclosure so that providing evidence does not expose the mother and child to further danger. A court cannot promise that no harm will ever occur or bind every outside agency, but it can be asked to address those limits expressly and establish the strongest lawful protections available. The need for that process should not be mistaken for an unwillingness to substantiate the account.

## What an evidence-focused response would examine

A focused review should obtain, through counsel and lawful procedures:

- A voluntary, detailed declaration from the mother identifying the alleged speaker, words, date, location, context, and any contemporaneous disclosure.
- Mr. Nuno’s account of when and how he learned of the threat, why he believed it, and which plea it affected.
- Relevant reports, recordings, messages, officer assignments, and any child-protection referral or custody proceeding.
- Each plea form, transcript, counsel file, prior withdrawal motion, and ruling.
- An explanation of what safe disclosure options were available or appeared unavailable.

An existing no-contact order does not establish that Mr. Nuno could not have learned of an alleged threat through counsel, records, or another lawful intermediary. The actual communication route must be proved. Neither this article nor the investigation should encourage prohibited contact.

### Months of notice, but no identified path to inquiry

Mr. Nuno reports that the prosecutor has known of the coerced-plea allegation for many months, and that the question whether he was lying then or is lying now has remained the prosecutor’s sole substantive position on that allegation. On that account, the problem is sustained resistance to examining the claim, rather than an initial request for clarification. The dates and contents of the earlier communications should be preserved alongside the September exchange.[[10]](#sources-and-authorities)

The question frames Mr. Nuno as a liar whichever account is accepted, while leaving his explanation for the earlier denial unaddressed. His notarized declaration describes fear that truthful disclosure could expose a vulnerable mother and child to retaliation. Repeating the inconsistency without identifying a protected means of receiving corroboration leaves that explanation untested.

**The practical effect, as Mr. Nuno describes it, is stonewalling without an express refusal to investigate.** The language stops short of categorically rejecting an inquiry, yet identifies no investigator, referral, request for protected evidence, or next step. In that limited sense it “plays it safe”: it communicates a credibility accusation without expressly committing the office either to investigate or to refuse. This describes the wording and its reported effect, not proof that the prosecutor chose it to avoid professional accountability.

Whether the prosecutor has a specific enforceable duty to investigate depends on the evidence received and the applicable legal standard. An express refusal would not by itself establish misconduct, and avoiding those words would not resolve the underlying duty. The appropriate question is what the office actually did after notice—not merely whether its response avoided a categorical refusal.

An evidence-focused response would address the protective motive described in the declaration and identify a safe process for testing the account.

Washington RPC 3.8(g) imposes particular duties concerning qualifying new evidence of innocence. An allegation that a plea was coerced does not automatically establish that factual-innocence threshold.[[11]](#sources-and-authorities)

## Procedural obstacles remain separate from the merits

Even a serious constitutional allegation must fit an available procedural route. Washington’s RCW 10.73.090 generally limits collateral attacks on facially valid judgments entered by competent courts to one year after finality; RCW 10.73.100 provides specified exceptions. CrRLJ 7.8 also requires examination for relief from a limited-jurisdiction judgment.[[12]](#sources-and-authorities)

The relevant finality dates, prior litigation, diligence, exception requirements, and consequences of successive litigation are unresolved here. Calling the allegation coercion does not establish a timeliness exception. Negotiating probation relief also does not itself reopen or invalidate the conviction.

That distinction permits a narrower request: the City can consider substantiated circumstances in its present resolution position without first conceding that the conviction must be vacated.

## Claimed compliance deserves an accounting

Mr. Nuno reports prior Washington EHM, substantial Montana monitoring, and an evaluation by Dr. Marta Miranda that he says was completed and submitted. The evaluator’s own correspondence supports the report’s existence; the monitoring records and governing orders remain necessary to determine which legal obligations were satisfied.

The prosecutor may legitimately require proof. But missing paperwork, disputed sufficiency, actual nonperformance, and a request for updated treatment are different problems. Before additional performance is demanded, an itemized response should explain which problem applies.

| Disputed item | Required reconciliation | Present limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reported 30 Washington EHM days | Identify the cause, dates, governing order, provider proof, and balance calculation | Same-case post-conviction service may satisfy an existing obligation; that has not been verified |
| Reported 18 months of Montana EHM | Identify the separate case and consider the burden as mitigation | No automatic transfer of credit is established |
| Earlier evaluation | Locate the order, report, receipts, transmittals, and provider confirmation | Completion, submission, and satisfaction of the ordered requirement remain distinct |
| Mental-health treatment proof | Specify the required provider, period, treatment, and completion evidence | An evaluation alone may not satisfy a treatment requirement |
| Outside warrants | Identify each warrant and the action within Mr. Nuno’s control | Edmonds cannot guarantee another court’s disposition |

In *Harris v. Charles*, Washington’s Supreme Court rejected constitutional claims to mandatory credit for misdemeanor pretrial EHM. It does not resolve whether verified service already performed under the same post-conviction order satisfied that order.[[13]](#sources-and-authorities)

**The correspondence shows no completed reconciliation of claimed performance with the remaining balance.** If performance is rejected, the parties should identify the controlling requirement, deficiency, and means of curing it.

## The outside warrant: repeated inquiries without a substantive answer

The prosecutor’s insistence that Mr. Nuno address a reported felony warrant must be considered alongside his efforts to learn what it concerns. Mr. Nuno reports making repeated communications seeking that information without receiving a substantive explanation. At most, he says, he has been told that the matter concerns unpaid fines—without being told the amount, why it is owed, or why it is associated with a warrant described as a felony warrant.[[14]](#sources-and-authorities)

**Attempting to obtain the information needed to resolve a warrant is an effort to address it.** Whether those efforts satisfy a particular court requirement is for that court to determine, but they should be accounted for before the situation is characterized as simple inaction. A demand to resolve an outside matter needs an achievable next step, particularly where the person seeking to comply cannot obtain a clear statement of the alleged obligation.

The description “felony warrant” needs clarification. A warrant issued in an existing felony case is different from a new felony charge. The label alone cannot explain whether the warrant concerns an unpaid obligation, failure to appear, an alleged supervision violation, or another basis. An unpaid balance does not, by itself, establish that a new felony has been charged.

The issuing court should provide:

- The court, cause number, issuance date, current status, and copy of the warrant and supporting order.
- The specific alleged violation and the judgment or condition said to have been breached.
- An itemized financial ledger showing the original obligation, payments, credits, additions, and present balance.
- The notice and appearance history, including any payment hearing and findings about ability to pay or willfulness.
- A defined procedure for counsel-assisted review and the steps required to obtain a ruling on recall or quashing.

If nonpayment is the basis for seeking incarceration, *Bearden v. Georgia* requires attention to the reasons for nonpayment, bona fide efforts to pay, and adequate alternatives before probation is revoked for inability to pay. The distinction between inability and willful refusal matters; the actual warrant and governing judgment must establish which issue is presented here.[[15]](#sources-and-authorities)

The immediate request is straightforward: **identify what is owed, why it is owed, what the warrant enforces, and what Mr. Nuno can do to resolve it.** Edmonds cannot dispose of another court’s warrant, but its prosecutor can acknowledge the inquiries already made and define what further effort will satisfy his negotiating demand.

In an October 27, 2016 letter, Dr. Marta Miranda acknowledged writing a psychological report for Edmonds Municipal Court and receiving payment. She described an amendment process, including sending revised material to probation, and copied Omar Gámez at the court. **This is independent documentary support that the evaluation existed and was connected to the court’s supervision process.** It does not, by itself, prove the clerk received the report or that its contents satisfied every treatment requirement.[[16]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno returned to court seeking protection of sensitive information in the evaluation. His November 3, 2025 motion sought sealing or redaction of a report dated approximately September 19, 2016. The published hearing account records that, at the December 2, 2025 hearing, the judge could not find the evaluation in the case files, despite Mr. Nuno’s position that it had been filed twice, and continued the matter for further evidence.[[17]](#sources-and-authorities)

According to Mr. Nuno, that same hearing brought a second discovery: cases he had believed long resolved were still in warrant status. He had approached the court to address the handling of sensitive information; he instead learned both that the report could not be located and that unresolved proceedings exposed him to detention. He reports that the public defender then spent many months investigating the evaluation’s whereabouts and the circumstances of its disappearance, without obtaining an answer identifying why it was removed or who removed it.[[18]](#sources-and-authorities)

The consequential question is therefore not simply, “Why has Mr. Nuno failed to produce it?” It is also: **what happened to a report prepared for this court, what records show its receipt and use, and why should an unexplained institutional record gap be treated as personal noncompliance?**

### Accounting for the court’s custody of the evaluation

The concern is therefore more substantial than a single unsuccessful search. The judge’s reported inability to locate the evaluation was followed by months of public-defender inquiry that, according to Mr. Nuno, still produced no explanation. **The evaluation’s custody history remains unanswered despite an effort to investigate it—not because Mr. Nuno simply failed to ask.**[[17]](#sources-and-authorities)[[18]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno contends that the report was removed in violation of proper custody and recordkeeping. The unresolved institutional questions are concrete: where was it last held, what happened to it, who authorized any removal or disposition, and why has counsel’s investigation not produced an account? Those questions call for a documented response from the responsible custodians.

Washington GR 15 supplies concrete standards. For specified records sealed by court order, it requires preservation of docket identifiers, secure storage and, for paper records, a substitute sheet, together with the sealing order and findings. Court-ordered destruction has separate documentation requirements. The rule also permits routine destruction under applicable retention schedules. **Sealing is controlled access and preservation; it is not an explanation for an untraceable disappearance.** Whether these requirements were violated depends on what happened to this particular record.[[26]](#sources-and-authorities)

The search should cover the evaluation’s original filing location, any later transmission to the 2019 Edmonds proceeding, and the relevant probation repositories. An earlier report’s existence and its receipt or acceptance in the 2019 matter are separate questions. The City should explain that connection when identifying what evaluation or treatment documentation remains outstanding.

The September–October 2026 email chain supplies concrete evidence of counsel’s recovery effort. Georgiades contacted Miranda on September 29 with an urgent document request and asked Mr. Nuno to complete her release form so he could obtain the evaluation. The next day, counsel explained that Miranda required a release despite being told he represented Mr. Nuno. On October 1, Mr. Nuno forwarded what he identified as the signed release and requested redaction of sensitive third-party treatment information. **The correspondence documents active efforts to recover the report while protecting information whose disclosure he had already challenged.** It records the request and authorization process, not delivery of the evaluation.[[23]](#sources-and-authorities)

The responsible offices should identify:

- The original referral, report date, transmitting party, receipt, and docket or probation-file entry.
- Every relevant court and case number, including confidential, sealed, archived, scanned, and probation records.
- Any transfer, removal, return, destruction, migration, access, or disposition records that exist.
- The governing retention classification and any applicable preservation hold.
- Whether the evaluator, former counsel, probation, or prosecution retains a copy or transmittal.
- Whether DOH matter 2017-3122PY contains the report, amendments, submission receipts, or records identifying a prior custodian.
- What searches have actually been made and what replacement proof will be accepted.

**The prosecutor’s response does not supply that custody history or address the reported inability of the court to locate the evaluation.** On Mr. Nuno’s account, he completed the evaluation and submitted the report; the court subsequently could not locate the record entrusted to it. Responsibility for accounting for a court-held record belongs with its institutional custodians. A demand that he produce it again leaves that responsibility unanswered and shifts the practical burden of the missing record onto the person whose liberty may depend on proving compliance.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno’s and counsel’s efforts to obtain another copy are efforts to mitigate that problem, not an acceptance of responsibility for the report’s disappearance. **Those efforts should not be converted into a fresh compliance obligation or treated as proof that the original requirement was never satisfied.** Before seeking adverse consequences based on the report’s absence, the City should address the evidence of prior submission, obtain an accounting from the relevant custodians, and explain the authority and necessity for any renewed demand. The stakes include detention and continued restrictions on liberty: a serious break in record custody calls for institutional accountability, rather than an assumption that Mr. Nuno must repair it at his own expense.

A due-process claim involving lost evidence is a separate legal question. *State v. Wittenbarger* distinguishes materially exculpatory evidence from evidence that is only potentially useful; the latter ordinarily requires proof of bad faith. A missing historical evaluation does not automatically satisfy either test. Its immediate importance here is the ability to establish compliance, contest asserted deficiencies, and avoid unnecessary repetition of an invasive process.[[27]](#sources-and-authorities)

### October 21: reported move toward self-representation and a discovery demand

In an October 22 update to the editor, Mr. Nuno reports that approximately ten months of representation by the Edmonds public-defender office had produced neither the evaluation nor an explanation of its absence. He says counsel could not locate it in the digital systems or archives searched, or identify an audit trail, removal motion, or order accounting for it. This describes the unsuccessful searches as he understands them; counsel’s search log and custodians’ responses are needed to establish their actual coverage.[[38]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno also reports that, on October 21, he chose to end that representation and proceed *pro se*—representing himself—because of the case’s age, complexity, and current posture, and sent a *Demand for Discovery* to prosecutor Aaron Walls. His stated purpose is to obtain the government’s evidentiary files and redirect the search toward the prosecution and responsible City and court custodians. The October 21 written demand is reviewed and summarized below.[[49]](#sources-and-authorities) His reported decision does not establish that the court has approved withdrawal or accepted a waiver of counsel. CrRLJ 3.1(e) requires court consent when a case has been set for trial, and CrRLJ 7.6(f) separately protects representation at a probation hearing unless waived.[[38]](#sources-and-authorities)[[39]](#sources-and-authorities)

He characterizes the prosecutor’s position as a threat of 120 days in jail for failing to produce the evaluation again. That account must be distinguished from the narrower documentary description already published here: the February 4 correspondence identifies a 120-day jail position alongside several disputed obligations. It does not establish that the entire proposed term was attributed exclusively to the missing evaluation, or that a judge imposed it. A complete charging or violation notice and the later communications are needed to determine precisely what conduct is alleged and what sanction is sought.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)[[38]](#sources-and-authorities)

**Seeking a replacement copy does not account for the disappearance of an institutional copy.** The prosecution should identify the requirement it seeks to enforce, the evidence of noncompliance, and its efforts to obtain the relevant records; each custodian should account for records entrusted to that office. Whether the historical report satisfied a particular order remains a separate question. A reported records failure cannot fairly be evaluated by assuming either that Nuno never complied or that every later condition was already satisfied.

He specifically asks that an unlocated record be distinguished from a destroyed or withheld one.[[49]](#sources-and-authorities)

### What retention law requires—and what it does not

Washington law supplies several distinct duties. RCW 40.14.070 governs approved local-government records disposition. The *District and Municipal Courts Records Retention Schedule*, version 7.0, approved October 4, 2023, is the current court schedule reviewed here; the “2025-06” web directory is not its adoption date. Its “Criminal Court Case Files—Other” series, DM52-06A-01, generally requires three years after final disposition; separate categories cover DUI, domestic-violence cases, exhibits and probation. Probation case-management records not filed in the official court record, DM52-06H-01, generally require three years after completion or termination of probation. The schedule prohibits destruction during ongoing or reasonably anticipated litigation. These are classification- and event-based periods, not a blanket period measured from the evaluation date. The actual classification, disposition date, applicable holds, and version governing any earlier disposal remain necessary.[[26]](#sources-and-authorities)[[40]](#sources-and-authorities)

CrRLJ 7.2(d), (e)(3) separately requires permanent preservation of sentencing and judgment records. That does not automatically classify every psychological report as a permanent sentencing record. The court must identify whether this evaluation was an ordinary filing, probation material, exhibit, or part of the preserved sentencing record.[[41]](#sources-and-authorities)

GR 15 requires a traceable process for specified judicial actions: sealing particular records preserves docket identifiers and requires the sealing order and findings; redaction preserves the unredacted original under seal; court-ordered destruction requires supporting authority, findings, and prescribed entries. Routine destruction under an approved schedule is separately permitted by GR 15(h)(5), so a missing destruction motion alone does not prove unlawful removal. Here, the November 2025 motion sought protection of the report; the published account does not establish that an order granting that relief was entered.[[17]](#sources-and-authorities)[[26]](#sources-and-authorities)

**There is no verified blanket requirement for an electronic audit log of every addition, removal, or alteration.** Washington State Archives’ December 2024 advice sheet expressly says agencies are not required *by statute* to keep a destruction log, while strongly recommending documentation of most destruction. Where destruction or transfer records exist, CORE version 5.0, series GS50-09-06, generally retains them for 50 years after the calendar year ends. CORE separately classifies ordinary IT audit trails under GS2010-003 as retained until no longer needed for agency business, subject to applicable holds. A local policy or system requirement could add duties; Edmonds’ historical policies and system specifications have not been established here.[[42]](#sources-and-authorities)[[43]](#sources-and-authorities)

The absence of a log therefore raises a question about traceability without, by itself, proving a statutory violation. Existing docket history, filing receipts, transfer records, imaging or migration documentation, destruction authorizations and system logs should be preserved and examined. RCW 40.16.010 prohibits willful and unlawful removal, alteration, concealment or destruction of records lawfully deposited in a public office. An unsuccessful search does not establish those elements or identify a responsible person.[[44]](#sources-and-authorities)

### Discovery places duties on the prosecution; custody remains with the custodian

CrRLJ 4.7(a) requires disclosure on written demand of specified materials within the prosecution’s possession or control, including expert reports and mental-examination results connected with the particular case. Subsection (d) requires an attempt to obtain identified material held by others if it would be discoverable in the prosecution’s hands, with court subpoenas or orders available when that effort fails. Subsection (h)(7) provides court remedies for discovery violations, including compelled disclosure or a continuance; dismissal requires the rule’s additional findings. These provisions do not make the prosecutor the clerk or guarantee recovery of a record that no longer exists. The particular proceeding and the report’s connection to it matter.[[45]](#sources-and-authorities)

For a probation revocation or modification hearing, CrRLJ 7.6(f) directly requires notice of the alleged violation and discovery of supporting evidence, with opportunities to present evidence and, subject to the rule’s conditions, confront witnesses. Under (c), revocation or modification ordinarily requires a hearing or party stipulation. A prosecutor’s proposed jail term is not a judicial finding of noncompliance.[[35]](#sources-and-authorities)

Public access and litigation discovery also follow different routes. Case filings and dockets fall under GR 31; court-management records may fall under GR 31.1. That classification can affect access to custody policies or administrative logs, and privacy or security restrictions may apply. Asking for existing records is different from requiring an office to create a historical audit log it never maintained. A court-directed accounting of searches is a separate form of requested relief.[[46]](#sources-and-authorities)

### The federal constitutional question is narrower than the records-management question

*Brady v. Maryland* requires disclosure of material favorable evidence concerning guilt or punishment. For evidence already lost or destroyed, *California v. Trombetta* asks whether its exculpatory value was apparent beforehand and comparable evidence is unavailable by reasonably available means; *Arizona v. Youngblood* requires bad faith for loss of merely potentially useful evidence. Washington’s *Wittenbarger* applies that distinction. These are criminal due-process standards, not a universal federal case-file retention schedule or logging mandate. Their application to a historical report in a probation dispute requires attention to the proceeding, the record’s significance, who held it, when it became unavailable, and possible replacement evidence.[[27]](#sources-and-authorities)[[47]](#sources-and-authorities)

The immediate accountability question can be investigated without declaring a constitutional violation: what did the government receive, what did it retain, what searches occurred, and what evidence supports the proposed consequence? A missing copy does not establish intentional suppression, and obtaining another copy would not resolve the original custody gap.

### Producing the report again also raises a history of alleged harm

The prosecutor is seeking a document Mr. Nuno has long identified as a source of significant harm. His Washington DOH complaint challenges the evaluation’s statements, diagnostic reasoning, treatment of interviews, and billing; his Edmonds sealing motion challenges disclosure of sensitive family health information. Those filings document sustained objections to the report’s accuracy and handling, rather than reluctance arising only after the current request for compliance proof.[[20]](#sources-and-authorities)[[17]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno further reports that the evaluation was transmitted to Montana and used there as evidence that he “does not believe the law applies to him.” He contends that disputed psychological conclusions were thereby carried into another jurisdiction and used to portray him adversely. The Montana transmission and use should be traced to the receiving records, identifying the speaker, date, report version, and context of that characterization.[[28]](#sources-and-authorities)

**For Mr. Nuno, producing the evaluation again is therefore not a neutral paperwork exercise.** It risks renewed reliance on a document whose contents and disclosure he has repeatedly challenged. Requesting proof that an evaluation occurred should be distinguished from seeking unrestricted access to its adverse conclusions or treating those conclusions as established facts.

The City should identify the precise compliance question and explain whether a provider certification, receipt, treatment summary, or limited extract would answer it. If the report itself is necessary, the defense should have an opportunity to seek court-authorized redaction, sealing, restricted access, and limits on further disclosure and use under applicable law. The complete historical record should be preserved, with any authorized redacted copy kept separately. These safeguards should address the report’s disputed status and sensitive third-party information before renewed circulation.[[26]](#sources-and-authorities)

### Recovering completed work before imposing a repeat assessment

**There is affirmative documentary evidence that Dr. Miranda completed a report.** Her acknowledgment of the report, payment, and an amendment process is materially different from an unsupported recollection that an appointment occurred. The unresolved questions concern where the completed report went, what the court received, and which requirement it satisfied—not whether a report was ever produced.[[16]](#sources-and-authorities)

The prosecutor’s questions about producing the report and obtaining a new evaluation appear to put repetition of that process on the table. They do not establish that a judge has ordered another examination. Nevertheless, the practical concern is substantial: Mr. Nuno may face renewed cost, intrusive questioning, disclosure of sensitive information, and another disputed assessment because institutions cannot account for an evaluation already performed.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

The defense asks that recovery and reconstruction come first. A provider copy, authenticated correspondence, DOH submission, transmittal, receipt, probation note, or other reliable evidence may establish completion or recover relevant contents. Washington ER 1004 recognizes circumstances in which other evidence of a writing’s contents may be used when originals are lost or destroyed without bad faith by the proponent. ER 1101(c)(3) also permits ordinary evidence rules to be relaxed in proceedings granting or revoking probation, while privilege protections remain applicable. The hearing’s actual procedural requirements should guide the use of replacement evidence.[[29]](#sources-and-authorities)

**A new examination would not recover the missing historical evidence.** It would produce a new assessment at a different time, potentially using different information, tests, and conclusions. Requiring it cannot answer who received the original, how it was used, or why it can no longer be found. Those custody questions would remain even after Mr. Nuno paid for and underwent another assessment.

If the City believes a new assessment is independently necessary, it should identify the operative order, the unmet requirement, and the reason a current assessment is needed. It should also explain why reconstruction or narrower proof will not suffice. **A missing institutional record should not silently become a fresh personal obligation.** Establishing the completed report’s custody history and establishing a lawful basis for a new examination are separate responsibilities.

### Evaluator authority must be accompanied by access and accountability

The related article *The Civil Rights Gap: Structural Failures & Barriers to Securing Legal Representation* identifies a broader concern: a person compelled to use a court-recognized evaluator may have little practical control over the process, while the resulting report acquires substantial influence. In this matter, the combination of disputed findings, an unavailable report, and the apparent prospect of another examination makes access to the original work especially important.[[30]](#sources-and-authorities)

New York’s Blue-Ribbon Commission on Forensic Custody Evaluations, in a December 2021 report released in 2022, warned of excessive judicial reliance on evaluations and obstacles to meaningful scrutiny of defective reports. It recommended improved access, conflict disclosures, certification, training, and accountability processes. These were findings and recommendations about New York custody proceedings, not findings about Washington criminal courts or Dr. Miranda. Their relevance here is the narrower institutional lesson: expert authority needs mechanisms for review.[[31]](#sources-and-authorities)

The same accountability principle applies to the disputed evaluation’s disappearance. A person should be able to obtain and challenge a report used to assess compliance. An agency’s decision not to discipline an evaluator does not itself adjudicate every disputed statement in the report. Nor should the report’s absence prevent examination of its accuracy while simultaneously exposing the person evaluated to another demand for the same work.

## A demand for performance without a definite endpoint

The correspondence contains conditional closure language and no express acceptance of counsel’s September proposal. That provides no assurance that completing the proposed EHM and producing records will satisfy every known issue.

### The concern comes from a reported history, not an abstract possibility

Mr. Nuno describes roughly twelve years of involvement with Edmonds court in which demands and conditions repeatedly accumulated while performance he considered complete went unrecognized. That duration and pattern are his account; the precise sequence must be tied to dated orders and compliance records. The plea examined here remains the September 2019 plea. The longer history explains why he seeks finality now; it is not offered as a separate challenge to an earlier plea.[[32]](#sources-and-authorities)

His concern is therefore not merely that further conditions are theoretically possible. It is that complying again may reproduce a cycle he says he has already experienced: perform a requirement, encounter a dispute about its proof or sufficiency, and face another demand without a definite endpoint. The acknowledged evaluation followed by its reported absence is a concrete example of how completed work can become difficult to establish years later. The disputed monitoring balance presents another accounting question, although the governing orders must determine what credit or satisfaction is due.[[16]](#sources-and-authorities)[[17]](#sources-and-authorities)[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)

**A history of unresolved compliance disputes can make the lack of a final commitment materially consequential without proving that another demand will follow.** Mr. Nuno need not predict the future to ask why he should undertake new burdens before the City reconciles the old ones and specifies the result of compliance.

The City may fairly contend that particular demands enforced existing conditions, that some proof was insufficient, or that a new requirement had an independent lawful basis. A dated accounting should identify those distinctions. The answer to the reported pattern is an explanation of each requirement and disposition—not an assumption that the next round of performance will finally be enough.

*Santobello v. New York* requires fulfillment of a prosecutorial promise that materially induced a guilty plea. *Mabry v. Johnson* distinguishes a withdrawn proposal from a constitutionally defective ensuing plea. Neither decision establishes that the probation exchange is already an enforceable settlement.[[33]](#sources-and-authorities)

The appropriate protection is a written agreement defining the City’s commitments and a court order defining the operative obligations. **No agreement should purport to eliminate judicial discretion or immunize future unlawful conduct.**

For a municipal matter, RCW 3.50.330 is a more directly relevant starting point than an unexplained reliance on general probation provisions. It addresses continuing jurisdiction, modification, and tolling for specified failures to appear. Case age alone therefore does not establish that jurisdiction expired; the sentence and appearance history must be calculated.[[34]](#sources-and-authorities)

CrRLJ 7.6 supplies procedural protections for probation modification or revocation. The prosecutor’s negotiating position is not itself a new judicial condition.[[35]](#sources-and-authorities)

## His father’s safety deserves an answer

Walls’s statement that Mr. Nuno “can not use his father as his excuse” does not answer the caregiving concern raised by counsel. Mr. Nuno reports vital responsibility for an elderly father recovering from surgery. His concern is that unexpected travel or detention could interrupt necessary support and expose his father to real danger. Calling that concern an excuse substitutes a judgment about Mr. Nuno’s motives for an assessment of the consequences to a dependent person.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)[[36]](#sources-and-authorities)

Mr. Nuno reports that, over the past two years, his father has experienced **three medical emergencies requiring intensive care (ICU)**. Each arose unexpectedly. He states that, in each incident, his absence could have left his father facing serious danger or even a fatal outcome. These episodes make the caregiving concern concrete: an emergency’s timing cannot be scheduled around a court appearance, and an uncertain period of detention can prevent a caregiver from responding when urgently needed. Advance planning for known appointments therefore cannot, by itself, address the risk. A workable arrangement must also account for sudden medical crises and provide a clear procedure for seeking urgent relief from travel or monitoring restrictions.[[37]](#sources-and-authorities)

The open letter describes how unresolved Washington matters affect daily life: uncertainty about detention, travel costs, repeated searches for old records, difficulty obtaining clear answers, threats to professional stability, and an inability to plan around family responsibilities. These burdens compound one another. A caregiver cannot reliably arrange replacement care without knowing when he must travel, how long he may be absent, or whether a scheduled appearance may result in custody. Although the letter also mentions household responsibilities, including a cat, its central concern is the safety of an elderly parent who depends on assistance.[[36]](#sources-and-authorities)

**The relevant question is how compliance can be arranged without an avoidable interruption in essential care.** A redacted medical statement and a focused caregiving declaration could explain the father’s limitations, Mr. Nuno’s actual duties, available alternatives, and the time needed to arrange safe coverage. That would give the court a concrete basis for considering remote review, a scheduled appearance, reasonable preparation time, and emergency caregiving provisions in any proposed home-monitoring arrangement. Medical disclosure should be limited to what is necessary to assess those requests.

There is already a procedural starting point. In January, Walls expressed no objection to a Zoom appearance after counsel raised the father’s surgery and other travel constraints. CrRLJ 7.6(c) expressly contemplates physical or remote participation in probation modification or revocation hearings and gives the court discretion to permit appearance through counsel. These provisions support seeking a specific ruling on how this matter can be heard; an earlier lack of prosecutorial objection does not itself authorize an appearance or resolve an outside warrant.[[1]](#sources-and-authorities)[[35]](#sources-and-authorities)

The court should therefore identify the appearance required, the issues to be decided, the possible custody consequences, and any arrangements it can lawfully approve before travel occurs. Any outside warrant requires coordination with its issuing court. Caregiving does not automatically cancel an enforceable obligation, but neither does that obligation answer a request for a practicable way to comply. Dismissing the father as an “excuse” leaves the central safety question unresolved: **what procedure will address the case while allowing necessary care to continue?**

## What a definite resolution should contain

Before additional agreed performance begins, counsel should seek:

1. The cause number, operative orders, alleged violations, and accounting of accepted prior compliance.
2. Whether 60 days of EHM is an existing balance, a substitute for jail, or a proposed modification, with the calculation stated.
3. An approved provider, cost terms, permissible absences, emergency caregiving arrangements, and objective completion proof.
4. The exact treatment documents required, privacy protections, and acceptable replacement evidence for missing historical records.
5. Identification and documentation of each outside warrant, acknowledgment of prior inquiries, and specific further actions within Mr. Nuno’s control.
6. The City’s express commitment to seek or support a stated disposition resolving all enumerated and known allegations.
7. A narrowly defined reservation for genuinely new misconduct or materially fraudulent proof, with a process for disputes.
8. A proposed order stating what terminates, what remains, and when discharge or closure takes effect.

## A fair conclusion

Mr. Nuno’s sworn account describes a protective motive for both the plea and the denial of pressure: fear that disclosure could endanger a widowed mother and her only child. A response focused solely on inconsistency leaves that explanation unanswered.

The same precision should govern compliance. Mr. Nuno’s claimed efforts deserve a documented accounting; the City may require proof and identify genuine deficiencies. Both positions can be respected by specifying what was accepted, what remains disputed, and what completes the obligation.

Against Mr. Nuno’s reported history of accumulating demands and unrecognized performance, a definite endpoint matters. An accounting, an explanation of the missing completed evaluation, and a written commitment defining the outcome should precede another burden.
