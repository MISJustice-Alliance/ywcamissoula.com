# When a Complaint Closes: What Montana’s Oversight Process Decides—and What It Leaves Unclear

ODC said the Lowney grievance was too old; the Commission on Practice later affirmed its dismissal while saying the alleged facts did not show clear and convincing evidence of an ethics violation. When Elvis Nuno asked where the prior correspondence explained how those grounds applied to an alleged speech-related condition and later conduct, ODC answered that its explanation was complete, declined more information, and directed him to Supreme Court review. Its reply did not identify the requested passage. The problem documented here is not a lack of reasons or proof of a failed investigation: it is that the disclosed decisions do not connect those different reasons to the disputed allegations, leaving a person seeking review to infer what to contest.

> **About this article:** A primary-record comparison of reasons given and questions left open in two distinct Montana attorney-discipline files and a separate POST certification complaint. It evaluates whether the disclosed explanations enable an informed challenge, not whether the alleged misconduct occurred.

## Executive Summary

Montana’s oversight systems have formal points where a complaint can be screened, dismissed, reviewed, or advanced to investigation. Those are different outcomes. A closure does not, by itself, show that allegations were investigated, rejected on their merits, or ignored.

In ODC File No. 26-145, ODC dismissed Elvis R. Nuno’s grievance against prosecutor Brian C. Lowney on a limitations ground in June 2026. A Commission on Practice review panel later affirmed the dismissal, stating that the complaint’s facts did not demonstrate clear and convincing evidence of ethical violations. Those are two different stated rationales. On November 5, ODC Chief Disciplinary Counsel Pamela Bucy said that its prior correspondence explained its reasoning, declined to provide further information, and directed Nuno to Supreme Court review. The record reviewed here does not establish a later Supreme Court filing or decision in that matter.

A separate 2025 POST complaint was closed after POST cited both jurisdictional and age-related grounds, and its published policy lists an ongoing pattern as a possible exception to the general five-year limit. Follow-up correspondence in 2026 disclosed that a non-anonymous complainant could request internal review by POST’s Case Status Committee. The reviewed record does not show whether such a request was made, how the alleged conduct was classified, or what its outcome was. The rule and policy versions governing that closure were not verified, so no current procedural step is applied backward to 2025.

These records raise a focused question: can a reader identify which body considered which issue, under what ground, and what the next reviewing body decided? They do not establish that every complaint was ignored, that every closure lacked investigation, or that either system is incapable of meaningful review.

## Three paths to keep distinct

The materials describe three separate processes:

1. **Attorney discipline:** grievance intake, ODC disposition, Commission on Practice review, and a possible request for discretionary Montana Supreme Court review.
2. **POST certification oversight:** allegation intake, appointing-authority response, POST screening, and possible investigation or certification action.
3. **Public explanation:** what an institution tells a complainant and what appears in public records. This is related to, but different from, the investigative work itself.

A limited public explanation can make a disposition difficult to understand, but it does not prove that no internal investigation occurred. The existence of a review route likewise does not establish that the reviewer conducted a new investigation or addressed every underlying allegation.

Nuno’s November 4 request asked ODC to identify the document and passage stating the facts and reasons, to send a copy if the explanation was separate, and, if the existing notice sufficed, to identify the language addressing his allegation that prosecutorial leniency was conditioned on removing or refraining from protected speech. He expressly disclaimed an automatic right to every internal document, every investigative step, or a separate answer to every legal question. [The redacted exchange](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/odc-26-145-november-5-2026-bucy-response-email-address-redacted.pdf) records Bucy’s answer: “Our dismissal correspondence explained our reasoning for dismissal. You will not be receiving any additional information from us regarding our work.” She directed him to Supreme Court review and added, “Please refrain from further correspondence with us.” Her reply did not identify a passage applying the 2019 premise to the alleged later conduct or addressing the speech-related allegation. It does not establish whether such analysis exists inside the confidential file. ODC’s position is that its earlier explanation sufficed and the next challenge belongs in review, not continued correspondence.

The June notice supplies a genuine timing ground; it does not make a merits determination about the alleged speech condition. The Commission letter records that the panel considered the complaint, ODC file and report, and an appeal letter, then states its evidentiary conclusion. It does not say whether that conclusion supplements or replaces the timing ground, or how it treated the alleged later conduct. A person challenging a time bar must contest dates and the rule’s application; one challenging evidentiary insufficiency must address what facts were found insufficient. The disclosed letters leave that choice to inference. This is a practical limit on an informed request for review, not a finding that review was unavailable or that either body violated its rules.

## A separate comparison: Tipp and the “already addressed” explanation

ODC File No. 25-147 concerns defense attorney Bryan C. Tipp, not Lowney. ODC’s November 2025 determination analyzed professional-competence and communication rules, accepted the defense account of limitations advice and outside-counsel encouragement, and found no violation within Tipp’s criminal engagement. Nuno later identified a witness email and medical letter as supplemental material. ODC’s July 2026 responses said the submissions did not change its earlier analysis and described them as additional examples of legal strategy. Those are stated reasons; the later letters do not name the two exhibits or explain how each fit that category. That limits what Nuno could identify to contest in a further challenge, without proving that the materials were unread. See the [original determination](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-octo/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-odc-ruling-november-2025), [July responses](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-25-147-july-2026-closure-response-analysis) and [dated follow-up](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-time-bar-strategy-reasons-unanswered).

The Montana Supreme Court later affirmed the Tipp Commission disposition on procedural grounds, describing Nuno’s request as untimely and as seeking clarification rather than merits review. That outcome is separate from the Lowney chronology. It does not establish that every underlying allegation in the Tipp grievance was false or decided on its merits. See the [Tipp–Lowney update](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/tipp-lowney-disciplinary-decisions-october-2026) and [dated follow-up](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-time-bar-strategy-reasons-unanswered).

## How Montana attorney-discipline review is structured

The court-adopted 2021 [Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=368130) governed the 2025–26 notices described here, subject to any case-specific rule or order. The Montana Supreme Court adopted further amendments in July 2026 that are generally effective January 1, 2027. The [Court’s administrative docket](https://supremecourtdocket.mt.gov/case-info/active/11218) identifies that future effective date. The amendments should not be substituted for the rules governing earlier actions.

Under the 2021 rules, ODC may dismiss a matter, including without all listed investigative steps; Rule 5(B)(10) requires a “concise written statement of the facts and reasons” for dismissal, and Rule 10(C)(2) requires written notice stating reasons and advising of review. A grievant may request Review Panel review under Rule 10(C)(3). If the panel affirms, Rule 14(A) provides a path to request discretionary Supreme Court review. A request for review is not the same as the Court accepting or granting review. The Commission’s Lowney letter describes an addressee and timing trigger different from the court-adopted Rule 14(A); neither the actual notice-sent date nor the effect of that difference on a filing is established here. Readers should use the operative rule and qualified counsel, not this article, to determine a personal deadline. See the [process explainer](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/montana-attorney-discipline-review-process).

ODC’s June notice also cites Rule 11(D) for an exception. In the court-adopted 2021 text, Rule 11(D) concerns repose and Rule 11(E) contains the exceptions; Rule 11(A) addresses the time limit for filing a formal disciplinary Complaint, not simply lodging a grievance. That citation discrepancy does not itself invalidate the disposition. What remains unresolved in the disclosed explanation is which alleged acts ODC assigned to 2019 and why the grievance’s asserted later conduct did not change the timing conclusion. [June 9 notice](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/odc-lowney-grievance-2026/odc-26-145-rejection-brian-lowney-june-9-2026); [2021 rule text](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=368130).

These procedures do not promise that every complainant receives the confidential investigative file, a separate answer to every argument, or a public hearing. The narrower question here is whether a written disposition identifies its operative ground clearly enough for a reader to understand what it resolved and what it left open.

## POST: a separate closure and later-identified review route

POST oversees public-safety-officer certification, not lawyer discipline or every civil-rights complaint. The [POST allegation policy](https://dojmt.gov/wp-content/uploads/Allegation-Policy-and-Procedure.pdf) describes screening and possible exceptions to its general five-year limitation, including an ongoing pattern beginning more than five years before receipt. Nuno asserted an ongoing pattern in follow-up. The disclosed responses do not identify which alleged officer conduct was outside POST’s certification authority, screened by age, or considered under that exception. Neither the policy alone nor his assertion establishes that an exception applied.

In the August 2025 complaint discussed in the project record, POST’s August 13 response said the director reviewed the complaint information, that most issues were outside its purview, and that allegations more than five years old generally would not be investigated except in limited circumstances. POST expressly closed the matter in an August 27 message. A May 2026 reply pointed to the rules and policy and maintained that reasons had been supplied, but did not allocate each alleged act to one of the stated grounds. On June 2, staff identified a Case Status Committee route to review the director’s determination upon a non-anonymous request. The reviewed correspondence does not establish whether that route was available on identical terms at the August 2025 closure or whether Nuno requested it. The unresolved issue is how claimed recent officer conduct and the asserted ongoing pattern were classified; the letters do not document a case-specific explanation. See the [August correspondence and revised post-mortem](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-state-complaints/post-mortem-of-mt-doj-post-complaint-dismissal-august-2025), [May exchange](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/RE-Request-for-clarification-regarding-POST-procedures-and-complaint-closure.pdf), [June 2 response](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/POST-Response-06.02.2026.pdf), and [original complaint index](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-state-complaints/mt-doj-public-safety-officer-standards-training-post-complaint-august-2025).

The versioned historical POST rule and policy edition governing the August 2025 closure were not established for this report. The published policy is evidence of a stated exception, not proof of its operative text or application to Nuno’s complaint on that date. No claim that a later committee-vote requirement applied in 2025 is made here.

## What the decisions do—and do not—tell a reader

| Record language | What it can establish | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| “Dismissed” | An institution ended the matter at that stage. | That no investigation occurred, or that the allegation was false. |
| “Time-barred” | The stated basis is a timing rule. | Whether the alleged conduct occurred or whether the underlying ethics issue was resolved. |
| “Affirmed” | A reviewing body upheld the disposition it reviewed. | That it conducted a new investigation or resolved every underlying factual allegation. |
| “Not enough evidence” | The body stated an evidentiary-insufficiency rationale. | Which evidence it credited or how each allegation was treated, unless the record says so. |
| “No further information” | The institution declined further clarification in the cited exchange. | That no prior reasons existed or that the complete file contains no additional explanation. |

This vocabulary helps avoid two errors: treating every closure as proof of institutional failure, and treating every formal review step as proof that the central issue was substantively examined. The documents should be read for what they establish, with their limits stated alongside them.

## Why traceability matters

An oversight process can screen for jurisdiction, enforce filing limits, decide whether allegations warrant further investigation, and protect confidential materials. The public accountability question is whether its record makes those tasks distinguishable.

For a complainant or outside reader, the practical questions are:

- Which conduct and dates did the institution consider?
- Was an issue rejected as unsupported, excluded as untimely, treated as outside the agency’s authority, or considered already resolved?
- What did the next reviewer examine: the existing record, a procedural question, or the underlying allegations?
- What review route existed at that time, and what deadline or conditions applied?

The Lowney letters document both a time-bar dismissal and a later evidentiary affirmance. Neither letter says whether the panel adopted the timing ground or supplied a separate evidentiary basis, and Bucy’s November reply points to review without identifying the requested passage. Nuno therefore has to infer whether to contest a conduct date, an exception, an assessment of proof, or more than one of these. That is a demonstrated gap in the disclosed explanation, not proof of what the confidential file contains or a judicial finding of legal insufficiency.

For POST, the reviewed correspondence supplies purview and age grounds, then later identifies an internal review route. It does not say which officer-specific acts were outside certification authority, which were screened by age, or whether claimed continuing conduct was assessed under the policy exception. Nor does the reviewed chain show use of the committee route. These omissions limit the reader’s ability to assess the closure; they do not prove intentional suppression.

These issues relate to the broader questions in [The Civil Rights Gap: Structural Failures & Barriers to Securing Legal Representation](structural-failure-and-the-civil-rights-gap.md), which addresses what happens when people cannot secure counsel to challenge complex institutional decisions. This article does not attempt to prove that either complaint system caused a particular civil-rights injury.

## Conclusion

The documents establish that ODC gave a timing reason in Lowney, the Commission affirmed with an evidentiary formulation, and ODC then declined to identify a requested passage or clarify how its earlier explanation treated alleged later conduct. POST gave jurisdiction and age reasons and later identified an internal review route, but its correspondence did not allocate the alleged officer conduct among those grounds or the stated policy exception. These are shortcomings in the explanations disclosed to this complainant, not findings that the institutions ignored evidence, breached a rule, or intended to obstruct review.

A more usable Lowney explanation would identify the alleged acts assigned to 2019, address why pleaded later acts did not affect the timing ground, and state whether the panel adopted that ground or relied separately on evidentiary insufficiency. POST could identify which alleged officer-specific acts fell outside its certification remit, which were screened by age, and whether it considered the ongoing-pattern exception. Such explanations could permit a more targeted challenge without requiring disclosure of confidential files, guaranteeing a favorable result, or converting an editorial recommendation into a legal duty.

## Methodology and limitations

This report compares the ODC and POST correspondence linked above, the published Lowney and Tipp source documents, the Commission letter linked in the [evidence ledger](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-review-evidence-ledger-2026), the court-adopted 2021 disciplinary rules, POST’s published policy, and the November 4–5 exchange. The complete confidential ODC files, any later Lowney Supreme Court filing, and any POST committee-review request or outcome were unavailable. The exact August 2025 POST rule and policy editions were not independently verified, so current procedural language is not applied backward. Advocacy submissions are not independent findings, and the correspondence does not show what every investigator did internally.
