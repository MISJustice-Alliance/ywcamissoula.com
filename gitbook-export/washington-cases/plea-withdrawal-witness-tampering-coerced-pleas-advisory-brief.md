### Executive snapshot

This brief evaluates a narrow strategic issue: whether the September 2019 Washington pleas should be treated as candidates for plea withdrawal, vacation, or other collateral relief based on alleged law-enforcement witness tampering directed at a third-party witness.

The strongest record theory is not ordinary plea pressure. It is third-party coercion. The sworn declaration states that an Edmonds Police officer threatened the declarant's then-romantic partner, a widowed single mother, that she would "lose custody of her child and never see her son again" unless she cooperated or testified against him. The declaration states that the threat left no realistic constitutional path: litigate and risk harm to the witness and child, or plead guilty to protect them.

That fact pattern matters because Washington law requires plea withdrawal where necessary to correct a manifest injustice, and federal plea-voluntariness doctrine treats threats, misrepresentations, improper promises, and coercion as central to whether a guilty plea is constitutionally valid.

## Record Summary

The sworn declaration describes four linked Washington plea events in September 2019: misdemeanor no-contact order violations in Seattle Municipal Court, Lynnwood Municipal Court, and Edmonds Municipal Court, and misdemeanor stalking charges in Snohomish County Superior Court. The declaration states that these pleas were obtained after law enforcement threatened a favorable third-party witness with loss of custody of her child unless she cooperated or testified against the declarant.

The declaration gives the witness a defined litigation role. It states that the witness knew the declarant, knew the history of allegations, and could have provided favorable defense testimony. It then alleges that an Edmonds Police officer threatened that witness with permanent separation from her child if she did not cooperate. The declaration characterizes the result as an unconstitutional catch-22: raising the threat risked retaliation against the witness and child, while accepting pleas protected them at the cost of the declarant's own trial rights.

This page does not adjudicate those facts. For counsel, the significance is that the declaration is now a notarized, downloadable primary artifact and gives enough specificity to justify targeted investigation: identify the officer, interview the third-party witness with anti-retaliation safeguards, obtain communications and dispatch records, subpoena or request any child-welfare-related communications, and compare the plea colloquies against the newly asserted coercion record.

## Governing Legal Principles

Washington's plea-withdrawal rule is demanding but direct. CrR 4.2(f) provides that a court shall allow withdrawal of a guilty plea when withdrawal is necessary to correct a manifest injustice. In [State v. Osborne, 684 P.2d 683 (Wash. 1984)](https://law.justia.com/cases/washington/supreme-court/1984/50135-1-1.html), the Washington Supreme Court described CrR 4.2(f), recognized that an involuntary plea is an indicator of manifest injustice, and explained that a plea-colloquy denial of coercion is highly persuasive but not conclusive. Osborne is important because it rejects the idea that later coercion evidence is automatically defeated by the plea hearing record.

Washington courts still require evidence, not only assertion. Osborne denied relief where the record contained only a bare allegation of coercion against repeated plea-colloquy statements of voluntariness. That is the main caution for this case. The sworn declaration is a meaningful starting point, but the strongest motion should not rest on it alone. It should be supported, if possible, by a declaration from the threatened witness, officer-identification evidence, contemporaneous communications, discovery showing state knowledge, and plea records from all four courts.

Washington also applies one manifest-injustice standard rather than a relaxed pre-sentence standard. [State v. A.N.J., 225 P.3d 956 (Wash. 2010)](https://law.justia.com/cases/washington/supreme-court/2010/812365-opn.html), explains that CrR 4.2(f) uses a single, stringent manifest-injustice standard, while allowing timing to be considered with all other evidence. For these 2019 pleas, timing is a serious obstacle because ordinary direct withdrawal windows have long passed.

For post-judgment strategy, counsel must address Washington's collateral-attack rules. [RCW 10.73.090](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=10.73&full=true) generally bars collateral attacks filed more than one year after final judgment when the judgment is valid on its face and was entered by a court of competent jurisdiction. [RCW 10.73.100](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=10.73&full=true) lists exceptions, including newly discovered evidence, unconstitutional statutes as applied, jurisdictional excess, and significant retroactive changes in law. That means the strategic problem is not only proving coercion. It is also fitting the claim into a timely procedural vehicle or a recognized exception.

Federal law supplies the constitutional baseline. [Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/397/742/) states that a guilty plea made with awareness of direct consequences generally stands unless induced by threats, misrepresentation, improper promises, or comparable improper pressure. [Machibroda v. United States, 368 U.S. 487 (1962)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/368/487/) recognizes that a guilty plea induced by promises or threats that deprive it of voluntary character is void and that coercion allegations may require a hearing when the existing record does not conclusively refute them.

Third-party leverage receives special scrutiny. [United States v. Nuckols, 606 F.2d 566 (5th Cir. 1979)](https://openjurist.org/606/f2d/566/united-states-v-nuckols) explains that guilty pleas involving threatened or lenient treatment of third persons pose a greater danger of coercion and require special care in assessing voluntariness. [United States v. Marquez, 909 F.2d 738 (2d Cir. 1990)](https://openjurist.org/909/f2d/738) recognizes that third-party plea leverage is not automatically unlawful when supported by probable cause and legitimate prosecutorial interests, but it also states that defendants must have an opportunity to show a threatened third-party prosecution lacked probable cause. Those cases are not Washington Supreme Court authority, but they are useful persuasive authority because the alleged coercion here is more severe than ordinary leniency for a spouse: it concerns an alleged officer threat to separate a witness from her child to influence testimony.

Ineffective assistance may be a parallel path if counsel knew or should have known about the threat, failed to investigate it, failed to protect the witness, or failed to advise about remedies. [Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52 (1985)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/474/52/) applies the Strickland ineffective-assistance framework to guilty pleas. The prejudice question is whether, but for counsel's deficient performance, there is a reasonable probability the defendant would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on trial.

## Strategic Path Forward

The strongest practical path is a record-development-first approach. Counsel should treat the sworn declaration as a trigger for investigation, not as the entire evidentiary package. The immediate goal should be to create a corroborated coercion record that can survive the State's predictable response that each plea colloquy said the pleas were voluntary.

The first workstream is case inventory. Counsel should obtain complete dockets, plea statements, plea colloquy transcripts or audio, judgments, sentencing orders, no-contact-order history, charging documents, police reports, discovery logs, and prosecutor notes for the Seattle, Lynnwood, Edmonds, and Snohomish County matters. Each judgment must be analyzed separately because the correct vehicle may differ between superior court and courts of limited jurisdiction.

The second workstream is witness protection and corroboration. Counsel should identify "Partner A" confidentially, assess present retaliation or custody risk, and obtain a declaration only after safeguards are in place. The declaration should state what was said, who said it, when, where, whether anyone else heard it, whether child-welfare agencies were mentioned, whether any follow-up occurred, and how the threat affected her cooperation or testimony. If the witness can corroborate the custody threat, the case moves from a bare post-plea allegation toward the kind of external evidence Osborne suggests is needed to overcome plea-colloquy statements.

The third workstream is officer and agency discovery. Counsel should request or subpoena dispatch records, CAD logs, body-worn or in-car video if any exists, officer notes, phone records, email, prosecutor-police communications, victim/witness contact logs, and any contact with child protective or custody-related entities. If the alleged threat was made by an officer rather than a prosecutor, counsel should still investigate whether prosecutors knew, benefited from, failed to disclose, or failed to correct the coercion.

The fourth workstream is procedural selection. For Snohomish County Superior Court, counsel should evaluate a CrR 7.8 motion, a personal restraint petition, or both depending on custody/restraint status and the judgment's finality. For Seattle, Lynnwood, and Edmonds municipal cases, counsel should evaluate CrRLJ 7.8 relief and any local procedures for post-judgment plea withdrawal or vacation. Because more than one year has likely passed, any filing must squarely address RCW 10.73.090 and RCW 10.73.100, including whether the corroborated threat evidence qualifies as newly discovered evidence or whether another exception applies.

The fifth workstream is remedy framing. The primary remedy should be withdrawal or vacation of the affected pleas. Secondary relief may include evidentiary hearings, appointment of counsel where available, discovery, sealing or correction of collateral records, and preservation of civil-rights claims. Civil claims should be sequenced carefully because Heck/favorable-termination principles may block or complicate claims that necessarily imply the invalidity of still-standing convictions.

## Litigation Theory

The cleanest theory is involuntary plea plus witness tampering. The argument is that the government did not merely offer a harsh bargain or exploit ordinary trial risk. According to the declaration, law enforcement threatened a favorable witness with custody loss unless she cooperated or testified against the defendant. If corroborated, that threat attacked both sides of the adversarial process: it distorted witness testimony and forced the defendant to choose between trial rights and protection of a vulnerable third party.

That framing should be stronger than a generalized "plea bargaining is coercive" argument. Courts routinely tolerate substantial plea pressure. The record should therefore focus on what makes this different: a threat to a non-party parent-child relationship, alleged state action by an officer, a direct nexus to witness cooperation or testimony, and pleas entered to stop third-party harm rather than to accept a legitimate sentencing concession.

The legal theory should also be modular. If the court resists the phrase "witness tampering" in a criminal-statute sense, the same facts still support due-process coercion and plea involuntariness. If the court treats the plea colloquy as strong evidence of voluntariness, counsel can argue that the whole point of the catch-22 was that disclosing coercion in open court would risk immediate retaliation against the witness and her child.

## Principal Obstacles

The time bar is the largest procedural obstacle. RCW 10.73.090 creates a one-year limit for collateral attacks when the judgment is facially valid and entered by a court of competent jurisdiction. A filing in 2026 attacking 2019 pleas must be built around a viable exception, a facial-invalidity argument, a jurisdictional defect, or another recognized avenue for relief.

The plea colloquies are the largest evidentiary obstacle. The State will likely argue that the defendant said under oath that the pleas were voluntary. Washington law treats those statements as highly persuasive. The answer is corroboration: the threatened witness's declaration, officer identification, communications records, discovery irregularities, and evidence that the threat could not safely have been disclosed at the time.

The third obstacle is remedy fragmentation. Four pleas across different Washington courts may require separate filings, separate judges, separate records, and different local procedures. Counsel should avoid a global narrative filing that fails to ask each court for a specific remedy under that court's rules.

The fourth obstacle is civil-rights sequencing. If a civil claim would imply that the still-valid 2019 convictions are invalid, counsel should evaluate whether relief from those pleas must come first. The plea-withdrawal record can later become the factual foundation for civil claims, but the postconviction strategy should not be compromised by premature civil framing.

## Recommended Attorney Review Checklist

1. Obtain certified copies of each 2019 judgment, plea statement, charging document, police report, and no-contact order.
2. Obtain plea hearing transcripts or audio from Seattle, Lynnwood, Edmonds, and Snohomish County.
3. Interview Partner A under confidentiality safeguards and assess whether she can sign a declaration.
4. Identify the Edmonds officer alleged to have made the custody threat.
5. Request or subpoena police/prosecutor communications with Partner A and any child-welfare or custody-related agency.
6. Determine whether the State disclosed the witness threat or any pressure on Partner A before the pleas.
7. Evaluate CrR 7.8, CrRLJ 7.8, personal restraint petition, and any court-specific post-judgment remedies.
8. Address RCW 10.73.090 and RCW 10.73.100 in the first draft, not as an afterthought.
9. Frame relief separately for each conviction and court.
10. Preserve civil-rights claims while avoiding filings that create unnecessary Heck or favorable-termination problems.

## Bottom Line

The sworn declaration materially strengthens the plea-withdrawal theory because it identifies a concrete third-party coercion mechanism: an alleged law-enforcement custody threat directed at a favorable witness and her child. The strongest path forward is not to argue that all plea bargaining is coercive. It is to build a corroborated record that these specific pleas were products of unconstitutional state pressure, then use that record to seek targeted post-judgment relief in each Washington court.
