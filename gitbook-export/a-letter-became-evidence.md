# A Letter Became Evidence. His Requests for Protection Met a Wall.

> **About this article:** The June 2018 YWCA grievance, the protection-order petition that followed, the Missoula stalking case, and Nuno’s later requests for assistance. This analysis distinguishes the documents’ contents from disputed accounts and unresolved questions.

* **Published by:** [MISJustice Alliance](overview/about-this-site-publisher-and-corrections.md), an advocacy publisher associated with Elvis Nuno, independent of YWCA of Missoula and government.
* **Intended audience:** Readers examining the Nuno case record, advocates, journalists, and legal researchers.
* **Scope:** Seattle Municipal Court case 613225 as a limited comparison; the Missoula proceedings and later accounts of police intake. This article does not adjudicate allegations against Chard, YWCA employees, police, prosecutors, or counsel.
* **Research reviewed through:** November 16, 2026, for the linked public materials; this is not a complete docket or defense-file review.
* **Disclaimer:** Public-interest commentary, not legal advice. A petition or declaration establishes what its author said, not that every assertion is true. A disciplinary dismissal is not a civil judgment.

***

## The documented contrast

A June 3, 2018 letter to YWCA of Missoula leadership accused two employees of false reporting and misuse of organizational resources, sought intervention, expressed support for its mission, and enclosed a $400 cashier’s check. In her June 12 protection-order petition, employee E’Lise Chard described contact with her employer as grounds for fearing escalation and sought protection from Elvis Nuno. The letter was a grievance to leadership and, in Chard’s account, an alarming contact through her workplace. The petition shows that the grievance entered an argument for restraint. It does not establish that the court adopted her characterization or that the letter violated an order. The [June letter and petition record](elise-chards-abuse-and-manipulation-of-the-protection-filing-system-june-2018.md) permits comparison of their accounts.

The Missoula criminal matter eventually ended in dismissal with prejudice, after an earlier dismissal and a deferred-prosecution period. Defense counsel’s December 2020 letter shows that he sought a final, unambiguous disposition while reporting complaints about Nuno’s speech and difficulties caused by the open record. What became of Nuno’s own requests to report conduct directed at him is a separate question. A criminal dismissal does not answer it.

## A grievance and an account of fear

Nuno addressed substantially similar June 3 letters to YWCA leaders. He alleged that E’Lise Chard and Rebecca Pettit had made false statements to Officer Ethan Smith and misused their positions; these were his allegations, not findings established by the letter. He requested that YWCA treat the correspondence as a formal grievance and act to stop the alleged conduct. He wrote, “I support the mission of your organization and appreciate the vital contribution to the community that it provides.” The accompanying cashier’s check was for $400. But the letter also warned that, if the conduct persisted, he would consider sanctions against YWCA. That warning matters to its tone; the donation alone cannot settle how its recipient experienced it. See the [petition analysis and linked June documents](elise-chards-abuse-and-manipulation-of-the-protection-filing-system-june-2018.md).

Chard’s June 12 petition alleged that, after a June 7 no-contact restriction, Nuno contacted her employer and pressed for her firing or punishment. She called his attempt to interfere with her employment and damage her professional reputation “a new level of aggression.” She also alleged a longer history of unwanted contact and fear for her safety. Those are material parts of her account, not facts adopted by this article. The letter is dated June 3; the petition says employer contact occurred after June 7. The discovery packet includes a June 6 postmark and June 7 receipt stamp, but the available copies alone do not resolve the drafting, mailing, delivery, or alleged-contact sequence. The hearing record and service chronology matter.

Nuno later reported that Judge Sam Warren treated the letter as protected speech rather than prohibited contact with Chard. Without the hearing transcript, recording, or order, this article does not present that account as a verified judicial holding. Nor does the petition’s reference to the letter establish why prosecutors later made their charging decisions.

## The court outcomes have different scopes

The [Seattle Municipal Court order](.gitbook/assets/seattle-case-613225-dismissal.png) dismissed case 613225 with prejudice on August 23, 2016, with “Proof” checked as the reason. It does not decide the different Montana charges or establish that the Missoula allegations were false. Seattle is a procedural comparison, not a ruling on Montana evidence.

The [Montana Office of Disciplinary Counsel’s November 3, 2025 determination](.gitbook/assets/11-03-2025-ODC_25-147_Complaint-Dismissal-Bryan-Tipp-REDACTED.pdf) recounts a misdemeanor stalking charge and two felony stalking charges in two cause numbers. It lists release conditions, a petition to revoke release, defense motions, an order of dismissal entered December 13, 2019, and a motion and order for dismissal with prejudice entered January 5, 2021. These stages cannot be collapsed into one uninterrupted prosecution, nor can counsel fairly be said to have filed no substantive motions. The underlying docket and both orders are needed to identify the scope of each dismissal.

Nuno describes arrests, bail and travel restrictions, GPS monitoring, pretrial check-ins, lost work, and a detention he says lacked a proper warrant or report. A [site analysis of a letter attributed to psychiatrist William Stratford](additional-evidence-and-documentation/missoula-1983-misconduct-mpd-prosecutors-ywca-of-missoula-expanded-history/legal-malpractice-evidence-bryan-tipp/2019-dr.-stratford-psychiatric-letter-gps-monitoring-harm-missoula-wa-prosecution-and-malpractice.md) quotes an April 10, 2019 assessment describing serious anxiety and concern about travel to Washington, and reports that the letter mentioned an ankle monitor. The original clinical letter, release orders, employment records, and complete defense file were not independently reviewed for this article. They are necessary to establish the duration and grounds of particular restraints, the claimed income loss and causation, and how counsel used the medical information. This article does not repeat the site analysis’s inconsistent monitoring and dismissal dates or infer malpractice from it.

## Counsel’s advocacy and a different request

In a [December 23, 2020 letter to Deputy County Attorney Brian Lowney](.gitbook/assets/tipp-lowney-letter-december-23-2020.pdf), defense lawyer Bryan Tipp said Lowney had acknowledged Nuno’s compliance with the deferred-prosecution agreement despite complaints about Nuno’s “exercise of his constitutional right of free speech.” Tipp described negative online comments as coming from Chard “(and perhaps some of her friends)” according to information brought to his attention; the letter does not independently establish authorship. He pressed Lowney to move for dismissal with prejudice, explaining that an earlier dismissal without prejudice and a confidential agreement made it hard for Nuno to demonstrate an end to legal jeopardy. He reported withdrawn employment opportunities after background checks. The letter documents advocacy for a criminal-case disposition, not the posts’ authorship, the reason any employer withdrew an opportunity, or the agreement’s alleged speech restrictions.

Nuno separately says he asked Tipp to write the Missoula police chief seeking a referral to the state Criminal Investigation Division because he feared a local conflict. A public [analysis of representation correspondence](additional-evidence-and-documentation/missoula-1983-misconduct-mpd-prosecutors-ywca-of-missoula-expanded-history/evidence-related-to-civil-rights-violations-of-mr-nuno-2015-2025/legal-malpractice-evidence-bryan-tipp/generally-disinclined-legal-malpractice-and-first-amendment-retaliation-nuno-case.md) quotes Tipp as “generally disinclined” to send the requested letter. That phrase comes from a separate client exchange, not the letter to Lowney. The complete original client exchange was not reviewed here; its context, alternatives, and later follow-through remain open. Securing dismissal and seeking a police-to-state referral were different tasks. Declining the latter does not prove Tipp did nothing on the former.

ODC’s [determination](.gitbook/assets/11-03-2025-ODC_25-147_Complaint-Dismissal-Bryan-Tipp-REDACTED.pdf) presents Tipp’s contrary position: he was retained for criminal defense, filed substantive motions, advised Nuno of possible civil time limits and encouraged outside counsel, and obtained dismissal. ODC gave reasons for dismissing the ethics grievance and found no disciplinary violation established on its review. The [Montana Supreme Court later affirmed the Commission’s dismissal](montana-bar-complaints/tipp-lowney-disciplinary-decisions-october-2026.md), characterizing Nuno’s request for Court review as untimely and not a merits-review request. Neither decision adjudicates civil malpractice. The narrower outstanding inquiry concerns what the full correspondence and defense record show about advice and options before final dismissal.

## A reported barrier at intake

Nuno’s [sworn declaration and summary](sworn-declaration-of-elvis-nuno-detailed-summary.md) describe an attempted report at Missoula police headquarters that he says officers refused to take, including an encounter he found intimidating. He contrasts this with a Dillon officer whom he credits with contacting a person about online posts and securing their removal. These are his accounts. Department intake logs, officer reports, communications with the person contacted, and outcome records were not independently verified here. A [site chronology](montana-bar-complaints/odc-25-147-chronological-case-record-timeline-ps-email-chain-index-police-report-comparison.md) places the described Missoula/Dillon encounters in or around 2022; they should not be treated as a proven consequence of a particular 2020 email.

The [complaint-suppression analysis](institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis.md) offers a framework for asking whether an attempted report became a reviewable record. It does not independently corroborate this encounter. The useful comparison is procedural: how each agency received, documented, assessed, or declined a report, and what reason it communicated. Different evidence or jurisdiction could justify different outcomes; this account alone cannot resolve why they differed.

## What remains to be established

The June letter and petition establish competing interpretations of an employer communication. The ODC chronology and Tipp’s letter establish distinct dismissal stages and counsel’s effort to secure dismissal with prejudice. They do not establish that a court found the letter lawful, that an investigator validated or rejected Nuno’s later reports, or that anyone committed retaliation or professional misconduct.

A fuller account needs the June hearing and service records; Montana docket, dismissal orders, deferred-prosecution agreement and release orders; the complete client emails and defense file; and Missoula and Dillon intake records. Those sources could narrow or change this analysis. Readers can submit corrections through the [publisher’s corrections process](overview/about-this-site-publisher-and-corrections.md).

The supported finding is not that the two sets of complaints deserved identical outcomes. It is that one contested letter entered a protection-order narrative and later criminal-case history, while the materials reviewed here do not yet give an equally clear account of how Nuno’s own reports were received and resolved.
