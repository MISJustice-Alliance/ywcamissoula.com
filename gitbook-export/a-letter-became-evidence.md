# A Letter Became Evidence. His Requests for Protection Met a Wall.

### Months to dismissal in Seattle. Years of legal exposure, restraints, and documented psychiatric harm in Missoula. Elvis Nuno’s record asks whose complaints activated official power—and whose struggled to become a report.

*By MISJustice Alliance | Public-interest advocacy editorial*

*This article distinguishes court dispositions, contemporaneous correspondence, allegations in case materials, and editorial analysis.*

In Seattle, the prosecution began in December 2015 and ended on August 23, 2016. [Seattle Municipal Court Case No. 613225](2015-2016-seattle-case-related-civil-rights-violations.md), *City of Seattle v. Elvis Nuno*, was dismissed with prejudice for insufficient proof. The research corpus identifies the order, signed by the judge and prosecutor, as the source of that disposition.

In Missoula, resolution took more than two years—and the consequences arrived long before the final dismissal. The case materials describe a prolonged period preceding the deferred prosecution agreement marked, in Nuno’s account, by the loss of a $150,000-a-year professional engagement, repeated arrests, escalating bail demands, GPS monitoring, restrictions on movement, and recurring pretrial-services obligations. They also describe an arrest that left Nuno “lost in jail” for days while counsel challenged the absence of a warrant or formal report supporting his custody.

By April 2019, a psychiatrist had documented severe deterioration. Dr. William Stratford’s April 10 letter to defense attorney Bryan Tipp described anxiety with agoraphobic features, impaired functioning, and the risk that further interstate travel for legal proceedings could be “potentially unraveling.” The letter recorded that Nuno was wearing an ankle monitor.

At the center of the Missoula escalation was a formal grievance to YWCA leadership, accompanied by a $400 donation and an explicit statement supporting its mission. An employee’s protection-order petition characterized the employer contact as “a new level of aggression.” Felony stalking charges followed later that year.

When Nuno subsequently sought help with online accusations affecting his business and its supporters, the response was markedly less definite: skepticism, contemplated investigator contact, reluctance to seek outside review, and an alleged refusal to accept a police report. A Dillon officer’s intervention, described in Nuno’s contemporaneous October 2020 email, provides the practical counterpoint.

The question is not whether every complaint should produce charges. It is why complaints against Nuno entered an established machinery of legal consequences while his own requests for assistance encountered such uncertain follow-through.

## A grievance became an argument for restraint

The [June 3, 2018 letter](elise-chards-abuse-and-manipulation-of-the-protection-filing-system-june-2018.md) made serious allegations about employee conduct. It accused employees of false reporting and misuse of organizational resources, requested that the alleged conduct stop, and asked leadership to hold those responsible accountable. It also stated:

> “I support the mission of your organization and appreciate the vital contribution to the community that it provides.”

The donation reinforced the distinction the letter made expressly: challenging alleged employee misconduct was not opposition to the organization’s work. The contribution was described as a gesture of goodwill.

Those allegations required assessment. So did the communication itself. Its request for organizational accountability, statement of support, and accompanying donation belong in any fair evaluation of what it conveyed.

E’Lise Chard’s [June 12 petition](.gitbook/assets/elise-chard-petition-order-of-protection-2018-06-12.pdf) presented the employer contact as escalation. It also invoked a recently imposed no-contact order covering third-party contact. The disputed classification therefore mattered: was the letter a grievance directed to leadership, or prohibited contact with the employee through her employer?

Nuno’s declaration and later grievance describe Judge Sam Warren ruling that the letter was protected speech, was not a communication to Chard, and did not violate the existing order. The hearing record remains necessary to establish the ruling’s precise language and scope.

The petition itself establishes the reversal at the heart of this account: a request to investigate alleged wrongdoing became part of the argument for restricting the person reporting it.

## The investigation required scrutiny—not momentum

The Missoula prosecution involved three stalking counts, including two felonies. [ODC’s chronology](.gitbook/assets/11-03-2025-ODC_25-147_Complaint-Dismissal-Bryan-Tipp-REDACTED.pdf) records an earlier December 13, 2019 dismissal and a final dismissal with prejudice on January 5, 2021, following a deferred prosecution agreement. The underlying orders are necessary to establish each dismissal’s scope.

The challenge to its investigative foundation is specific. Nuno’s case materials identify inadequate investigation, alleged conflicts involving investigators and the complainant’s institutional relationships, and continued reliance on Officer Ethan Smith’s reports after his removal from the matter. The repository’s charging-document research also identifies disputed statements attributed to Nuno’s parents as central evidentiary concerns.

Removing an officer and reassessing his work are different actions. The relevant questions concern what happened to his assertions afterward: which remained in the charging narrative, which were independently corroborated, and how the reasons for removal affected their treatment.

Those questions belonged in the prosecution’s assessment of its evidence and the defense’s scrutiny of the case.

The Seattle disposition supplies a concrete reference point for examining delay. The Seattle case reached an insufficient-proof dismissal within approximately eight to nine months. Privately retained counsel handled the Missoula matter for substantially longer, ending through negotiated conditions despite the investigative defects Nuno identifies.

The eventual dismissal is therefore an endpoint to examine—not a substitute for examining the path to it.

## The consequences arrived before any verdict

A filing date and dismissal date do not capture the burden imposed between them.

The case materials attribute the loss of a $150,000-a-year professional engagement to the arrest and disruption of Nuno’s work. They describe repeated arrests, including a detention episode in which he remained jailed for days while Tipp established, according to the account, that no warrant or formal report supported the arrest.

Nuno also challenges repeated bail increases and efforts to revoke release as attempts to keep him incarcerated while the investigation continued. The applications, orders, and hearing records are the sources for examining the grounds advanced for those measures and the court’s response.

Release itself carried continuing restraints. The attached analysis describes GPS ankle monitoring, restrictions on leaving Missoula County without permission, and regular pretrial-services check-ins. Employment, appointments, and interstate court obligations had to be managed within that structure of monitoring and compliance.

The cumulative burden matters. Being outside a jail did not mean being free of substantial state control.

A [site analysis quoting Dr. Stratford’s April 10, 2019 letter](additional-evidence-and-documentation/missoula-1983-misconduct-mpd-prosecutors-ywca-of-missoula-expanded-history/legal-malpractice-evidence-bryan-tipp/2019-dr.-stratford-psychiatric-letter-gps-monitoring-harm-missoula-wa-prosecution-and-malpractice.md) describes the human consequences while the proceedings were underway. As excerpted in the attached analysis, it described a “remarkable anxiety disorder with agoraphobic features” and anxiety that had “precluded reasonable or adequate functioning.” It warned that travel for Washington proceedings could cause regression and worsening and indicated that the condition was not near remission.

This was contemporaneous medical evidence—not merely a retrospective account of distress after the case ended. It also put counsel on notice of serious functional impairment and the risk of further deterioration.

That creates a concrete question about the defense: how was the assessment used to seek accommodations, relief from conditions, or a timely resolution?

Taken together, the employment loss, detention, monitoring, movement restrictions, reporting obligations, and psychiatric deterioration support the editorial critique that the process operated as punishment in its practical effects before a verdict. Whether particular conditions constituted unlawful pretrial punishment is a separate legal determination.

The distinction matters when assessing the eventual agreement. Nuno was not considering restrictions on speech from a position untouched by the prosecution. He was doing so after prolonged legal exposure, economic injury, restraints on movement, and documented deterioration in his health.

## Speech became part of the price of relief

Nuno’s account of Tipp’s advice is direct: accept restrictions on expression to obtain prosecutorial leniency. He says GPS monitoring ended when he signed the deferred prosecution agreement. His declaration describes conditions requiring removal of his YWCA complaint and a period of silence. The signed agreement and related advice govern the restrictions’ exact terms and dates.

That places expression at both ends of the account. A complaint to organizational leadership became part of the case for restraint. The negotiated route out then required, in Nuno’s account, retreat from related speech.

Tipp’s [December 23, 2020 letter to Deputy County Attorney Brian Lowney](.gitbook/assets/tipp-lowney-letter-december-23-2020.pdf) captures the tension. Tipp referred to complaints about Nuno’s “exercise of his constitutional right of free speech” and recorded Lowney’s acknowledgment that Nuno had complied with the agreement.

Describing expression as constitutionally protected in correspondence is not the same as challenging restrictions a client was advised to accept. Nor should obtaining the final order implementing an agreed disposition be presented as a newly won concession.

The accountability question concerns what happened before that point: what challenges were pursued, what alternatives were evaluated, and why speech concessions became the route to relief after the process had already extracted so much.

## Pabst’s commitments supply the standard

County Attorney Kirsten Pabst had publicly articulated a demanding standard for institutional responsiveness.

In her [April 22, 2015 statement](https://www.mtpr.org/montana-news/2015-04-22/missoula-county-prosecutor-kirsten-pabsts-statement-on-jon-krakauers-book) responding to Jon Krakauer’s *Missoula*, she wrote:

> “Objectivity requires looking at all of the facts; talking to all with knowledge; insisting on complete information.”

She also promised:

> “100 percent of the time victims and witnesses will be heard and treated with compassion and respect.”

Her statement concerned sexual-assault handling and described concrete reforms, including specialized prosecutors, a victim-witness coordinator, training, and improved communication. Those changes deserve accurate representation. Her commitments to evidence and listening also provide a benchmark against which later requests for assistance can be assessed.

Nuno’s September 2020 correspondence presents that test.

Tipp acknowledged the reported online conduct, writing that it was troubling to hear that Chard and her friends were “posting negatively.” He then relayed Pabst’s response:

> “She seemed skeptical that there was much she or her office could do to assist you.”

According to the same email, Pabst wanted investigator Mike Dominick to obtain Nuno’s information. Tipp expressed hope that contact had occurred. The explanation included concern about contacting a represented person without going through counsel.

The question is what followed: assignment, contact, evidence review, assessment, and communication of an outcome.

Public commitments become meaningful through those case-level decisions. A reform narrative cannot substitute for an account of what happened to a particular request.

## The outside route remained unresolved

As recounted in the [representation-correspondence analysis](additional-evidence-and-documentation/missoula-1983-misconduct-mpd-prosecutors-ywca-of-missoula-expanded-history/evidence-related-to-civil-rights-violations-of-mr-nuno-2015-2025/legal-malpractice-evidence-bryan-tipp/generally-disinclined-legal-malpractice-and-first-amendment-retaliation-nuno-case.md), on October 15, 2020, Nuno asked Tipp for a specific intervention: write to Missoula’s police chief, attach relevant materials, and seek referral to the state Criminal Investigation Division because of an alleged local conflict.

On December 23, Tipp deferred discussion until receipt of the final dismissal order and added:

> “I am generally disinclined to send the letter you are requesting.”

Completing the agreed dismissal and seeking an investigation of reported conduct against Nuno were separate matters. Following through on the first did not supply the second.

The same day’s letter to Lowney makes the disparity particularly clear. Complaints about Nuno’s expression had reached the prosecutor overseeing his compliance. Counsel was also reporting accusations against Nuno, without identifying a resulting investigative resolution.

One set of objections reached an official with authority over his legal exposure. His requests for protection remained tied to uncertain next steps.

## Accountability can fail before a complaint exists

Nuno’s [declaration](sworn-declaration-of-elvis-nuno-detailed-summary.md) describes his attempt to report the conduct at Missoula police headquarters as an intimidating encounter. He alleges ridicule, an officer placing a hand on a holstered firearm when he reached for his phone to show evidence, and refusal to take a report. His October 2020 correspondence already described police unwillingness to accept his complaint.

This concerns access to the process itself.

MISJustice Alliance’s [complaint-suppression research](institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis.md) examines that earlier point of failure: whether a civilian can turn an encounter into a recorded, reviewable complaint. Its framework considers institution-controlled intake, opaque screening, unclear review routes, and the discouraging effects of fear or apparent futility.

That research is an analytical framework, not independent corroboration of the headquarters encounter. Its relevance is the question it asks: what happens to grievances that never enter the official record?

A report about alleged harassment and a misconduct complaint about police are distinct. The alleged encounter connects them. Nuno arrived seeking documentation of reported conduct and describes leaving with an additional grievance about how officers treated him.

An institution cannot demonstrate accessibility merely by counting complaints it accepted. A reviewed complaint can produce an explanation. A complaint stopped at intake may leave no comparable decision to examine.

## Dillon supplies the practical comparison

Nuno’s October 15, 2020 email describes a different experience with Dillon police. He credited an officer with “considerable investigation and tracking down” and resolving the immediate problem “professionally and swiftly.” His later declaration identifies Officer Ian Turnes and describes contact that secured removal of public posts. The email establishes that Nuno reported this episode by October 15, 2020; it does not independently verify the police response.

The sequence is concrete: receive the complaint, locate the person involved, make contact, and obtain a practical result.

Nuno also reported difficulty obtaining Dillon’s documentation afterward. That records problem belongs in the account, but it does not erase the contemporaneous description of intervention.

Dillon gives the Missoula inquiry a practical reference point. What options were considered? What prevented comparable action? What explanation was provided?

The comparison does not demand identical outcomes. It demands an intelligible account of different responses.

## An endpoint is not an explanation

The outstanding questions are specific:

- What did the court decide about the YWCA letter, and how was that determination addressed afterward?
- What independent verification followed Smith’s removal?
- What justified the arrests, bail requests, and restrictions on release?
- How did counsel use Stratford’s assessment to seek relief from the burdens it documented?
- What advice produced the speech concessions, and what alternatives were evaluated?
- What happened to the Dominick inquiry and Nuno’s attempted police report?

These are requests for records, explanations, and appropriate independent review—not predetermined findings or charges.

The contrast is not simply between a short case and a long one. It is between a Seattle prosecution dismissed for insufficient proof and a Missoula process that, according to the case materials, cost employment, imposed custody and surveillance, restricted movement, and coincided with severe psychiatric deterioration before reaching a negotiated resolution.

Throughout that process, complaints about Nuno’s expression reached prosecutorial oversight. His own reports of harm met skepticism, uncertain follow-through, and reluctance to seek outside review.

A letter supporting an organization’s mission and enclosing $400 became part of the argument for restraint. The resulting legal process imposed consequences long before its conclusion.

The public deserves more than the fact that it eventually ended.

It deserves an explanation of why the system was so definite about scrutinizing Nuno—and so indefinite about helping him.
