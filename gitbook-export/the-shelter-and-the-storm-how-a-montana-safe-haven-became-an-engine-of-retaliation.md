## 1.1 Author’s Note
This story is based on a comprehensive review of court records, sworn declarations, formal
complaints, internal chronologies, and public records collected by the MISJustice Alliance, a
civil-rights collective. The goal of this report is to document patterns of institutional behavior
as described by those who have experienced them, and to illuminate the structures that enable
such patterns to persist. The accounts and analyses presented here are drawn directly from that
evidentiary record.
## 1.2 The Complaint and the Cascade
To understand how institutions fail, one must sometimes trace the path of a single, routine act.
A formal complaint, a request for assistance, or a simple letter can, under the right conditions,
trigger a disproportionate and punitive response. When this happens, it cracks open the façade
of an organization, revealing the flawed wiring and compromised relationships hidden within. The
story of what happened after a polite grievance was mailed to a community shelter in Missoula,
Montana, is a case study in how such a system can turn on the very people it is meant to serve.
It began in 2017, when Elvis Nuno, an IT consultant, mailed a letter to the YWCA of Missoula.
It was not an attack but a formal grievance, accompanied by a 400 dollar donation, respectfully
requesting that harassment from a YWCA employee cease. Nuno intended to resolve a workplace
dispute through the proper channels. Instead, his letter triggered what legal analyses would later
call a “textbook example of retaliatory prosecution.” Over the next 18 months, Nuno would face
repeated arrests, felony stalking charges, invasive surveillance, and excessive bail demands—
a coordinated campaign that systematically destroyed his 20-year career and inflicted damages
estimated in the millions. All charges were eventually dismissed.
But Nuno’s letter did not land in a vacuum; it landed in a city still grappling with the ghosts of a
public scandal, and within an institution whose relationship with power had been fundamentally
reshaped by it.
## 1.3 The Shadow of the “Rape Capital”
History shapes the present, often in unintended ways. In the early 2010s, the U.S. Department of
Justice (DOJ) intervened in Missoula to reform a system that had profoundly failed victims of sexual
assault. This necessary intervention, however, also forged a new set of institutional entanglements
that would later prove disastrous for those who found themselves on the wrong side of the city’s
power structures.
According to DOJ investigation documents from 2012 to 2016, Missoula had earned the moniker
of the “rape capital” due to systemic failures within the Missoula Police Department (MPD) and
the County Attorney’s Office. The investigation uncovered documented gender bias in sexual
assault responses and a prosecution rate of only 16%, painting a picture of a justice system that
re-victimized survivors and protected abusers.
The 2014 DOJ settlement agreement mandated sweeping reforms. Central to this effort was the
creation of Memorandums of Understanding (MOUs) and a “coordinated response” model de-
signed to improve communication and collaboration between law enforcement, prosecutors, and
victim services. The YWCA of Missoula was formally designated as a key “community partner”
in these new protocols. This integration, while well-intentioned, deepened the organization’s rela-
tionship with local law enforcement. By formally embedding the YWCA with law enforcement, the
settlement blurred the lines between independent advocacy and quasi-state action, reducing the
YWCA’s role as an external watchdog and making it an integrated, and therefore less accountable,
part of the system it was supposed to be monitoring. This new, tightly woven system, designed to
protect victims, also created the perfect conditions for conflicts of interest and the weaponization
of advocacy.
## 1.4 A Man Undone: The 18-Month Prosecution of Elvis Nuno
Elvis Nuno’s case serves as a powerful case study in how this system could be turned against
an individual. What began as a formal complaint escalated into a multi-year legal battle that
dismantled a man’s life, all based on charges that were ultimately dismissed with prejudice.
The retaliation began shortly after Nuno submitted his grievance. E’Lise Chard, a YWCA employee
with whom Nuno had “no significant personal relationship,” filed stalking allegations against him.
The investigation was handled by MPD Officer Ethan Smith, who, according to legal documents,
had a documented “close personal relationship” with Chard—a clear conflict of interest that should
have disqualified him from the case.
From 2017 to 2019, Nuno was subjected to an escalating campaign of legal and personal pressure.
The tactics used transformed a civil dispute into what legal analyses describe as a malicious
prosecution.
- Fabricated Charges: Misdemeanor and felony stalking charges were filed against Nuno de-
spite what legal documents call a “complete absence of evidence” supporting the allegations.
- Evidence Suppression: According to case files, the YWCA received an exculpatory char-
acter reference for Nuno that “directly contradicted the stalking allegations.” This crucial
evidence was deliberately withheld from defense counsel, transforming the case from one of
institutional negligence to one of active evidence suppression.
- Repeated Arrests: Nuno was arrested multiple times based on the same unsubstantiated
claims, with no new evidence presented to justify the continued legal actions.
- Excessive Bail: Legal analyses concluded that prosecutors’ demands for bail amounts rang-
ing from 50,000 to 100,000 dollars served as a form of pretrial punishment.
- Invasive Surveillance: The prosecution obtained an overbroad Facebook search warrant for
“all data” in Nuno’s account and imposed an electronic ankle monitor for 18 months. This
device, which constitutes a Fourth Amendment search, severely restricted his movement
and served as a constant reminder of his status as an accused man.
The personal and professional devastation was total. The legal battle effectively destroyed Nuno’s
livelihood and well-being, with quantifiable damages that underscore the severity of the institutional
abuse.
- Career Loss: The immediate loss of his 150,000 dollars per year IT consulting contract and
the systematic destruction of his 20-year career in systems engineering.
- Total Financial Damages: The total estimated economic damages reached 3,441,000 dol-
lars, a figure that includes lost career earnings, extensive legal fees, and the cost of medical
treatment for diagnosed PTSD.
- Favorable Termination: After 18 months of this ordeal, all charges against Nuno were ulti-
mately dismissed with prejudice, a legal acknowledgment that the case should never have
been brought.
The architecture of Nuno’s ordeal was not accidental. It was built upon a structural conflict of
interest at the very heart of Missoula’s victim services and criminal justice system—a system that
was supposed to protect, not persecute.
## 1.5 The Revolving Door of Power: Analyzing the Conflict
To understand how a personal dispute escalated into a state-sponsored campaign of harassment,
it is crucial to dissect the institutional structures that enabled it. The relationship between the
YWCA, the Missoula Police Department, and the County Prosecutor’s office formed a systemic
feedback loop that laundered private grievances into official state actions, all while insulating the
involved parties from accountability.
Based on an analysis of the relationships and events, the cycle of influence operated in four distinct
steps.
- Step 1: YWCA Staff Accusations The process began with complaints from YWCA staff,
such as those made by E’Lise Chard. These accusations were passed up to the YWCA’s
leadership and board, framing an internal or civil matter as a threat requiring institutional
intervention.
- Step 2: Board Oversight & Law Enforcement Overlap Crucially, sitting on the YWCA Board
of Directors was MPD Detective Connie Brueckner. This dual role created what legal doc-
uments call an “extreme conflict of interest.” As a board member, she was involved in the
governance of the organization whose employee was the accuser. As an MPD detective,
she was in a position to direct or oversee the official police investigation into those same
accusations.
- Step 3: Tainted Police Investigations With this conflict in place, Detective Brueckner’s “dual
rol[e] shapes investigations.” As a board member, she was positioned to strategically influ-
ence the direction and framing of the MPD’s official investigation from its inception. This
process lent official weight to her own organization’s claims, transforming what might other-
wise be a civil dispute into a criminal matter backed by the authority of law enforcement.
- Step 4: Prosecutorial Action The County Prosecutor’s office received these police reports
and, based on the officially sanctioned narrative, made charging decisions. These decisions,
in turn, gave legal credibility back to the initial staff accusations, completing the circle and
validating the entire punitive process.
This feedback loop effectively weaponized the criminal justice system to settle scores and silence
critics. It created a “chilling effect” on anyone—client, employee, or citizen—who might consider
filing a complaint against the organization or its staff. The Nuno case provides a blueprint for this
pattern, but the stories of other clients and staff reveal the true breadth of its impact.
## 1.6 Echoes in the System: Other Voices, Similar Patterns
A single case, however egregious, might be dismissed as an anomaly. But to demonstrate a
systemic issue, one must look for corroborating patterns. A review of public complaints, client
testimonials, and employee reviews reveals that the themes of retaliation, discrimination, and in-
stitutional dysfunction in the Nuno case are not unique. They are echoed in the experiences of
numerous other individuals who sought help from the YWCA of Missoula.
### 1.6.1 Interference and Defamation
In a 2018 Google Review, a man named Arthur Brown alleged that the YWCA actively interfered
with his parental rights. He claimed the organization made false statements and wrote defamatory
letters to a judge, recommending a permanent restraining order and helping “separate babies from
a father they love,” all without adequate knowledge of his character or the situation.
### 1.6.2 Confidentiality Breaches and Discrimination
An anonymous client on Facebook recounted a harrowing story of a YWCA counselor allegedly
leaking confidential therapy details to her abusive ex-partner, who was friends with a high-level
staff member. This client also noted that her experience was not isolated, stating the organization
has “tons of bad reviews at not being very good with the disabled which I can attest to.”
### 1.6.3 Manufacturing Homelessness
In October 2021, a local news investigation detailed the “Meadowlark Shelter Winter Evictions,”
where the YWCA abruptly enforced a rigid 90-day limit on stays. This policy change resulted
in the eviction of multiple families as winter approached, including Jessica Waltz and her three
young children, ages 10, 9, and 7. This practice aligns with client claims of “bias-based service
allocation,” where rules are applied arbitrarily and serve to create homelessness rather than solve
it.
### 1.6.4 Internal Dysfunction and Exploitation
The organization’s internal culture has also drawn sharp criticism. Employee reviews on In-
deed.com from 2019 to 2024 paint a consistent picture of deep dysfunction. Former staff describe
an “appalling” lack of training for employees dealing with traumatized clients, “exploitative” low
wages, “racist undertones,” and management that is “ill advised” and non-supportive.
Taken together, these accounts paint a picture of a holistic institutional failure, where dysfunc-
tion impacted every core domain of the organization’s operations—from client confidentiality and
parental advocacy to housing stability and internal employee welfare.
## 1.7 Following the Money, Following the Harm
In investigative work, analyzing an organization’s finances can reveal the distance between its
stated mission and its actual priorities. For the YWCA of Missoula, a review of its funding streams
shows a powerful, well-funded institution whose operational realities, as described by clients and
staff, stand in stark contrast to its public image.
The organization receives significant funding from both public and private sources, underscoring
its central role in the region’s social services network.

This stands in sharp contrast to the organization’s public mission of “empowering women and pro-
moting peace, justice, freedom and dignity for all.” The documented allegations of manufacturing
homelessness, discriminating against disabled clients, and retaliating against complainants chal-
lenge the integrity of this mission. As one anonymous client wrote, it is “fraudulent for a non-profit
organization to claim helping multiple families when they are treated like a number. . . all just for
show to their sponsors.”
This discrepancy points to the perverse incentives that can arise within what critics call the “home-
lessness industrial complex.” Missoula’s official Needs & Gaps analysis confirms a deficit of shelter
beds and a crisis of chronic homelessness. In such an environment, performance metrics can be-
come detached from meaningful outcomes. According to an analysis from the MISJustice Alliance,
these practices are “part of a coordinated system that converts vulnerable populations into recur-
ring funding justifications while suppressing organizing and advocacy by those same populations.”
This is consistent with client allegations that “many families are repeat clients. . . that they didn’t
help properly the first go around.”
When individuals like Elvis Nuno attempted to hold this powerful and well-funded system account-
able, they were met not with reform, but with institutional silence and outright dismissal from the
very bodies designed for oversight.
## 1.8 A Wall of Silence: Accountability Denied
The true test of a system’s integrity is not whether it is perfect, but how it responds to formal
complaints and evidence of misconduct. In Missoula, the documented failure of official oversight
mechanisms to provide any accountability demonstrates a system captured by the interests it is
meant to regulate.
The most telling example is found in an email exchange in August 2025 (as written in the official
record) between Elvis Nuno and the Montana Public Safety Officer Standards and Training (POST)
Council, the state body responsible for certifying and disciplining law enforcement. Nuno submitted
a detailed, notarized complaint against Officers Ethan Smith and Connie Brueckner, alleging a
multi-year pattern of corruption, conflicts of interest, and civil rights violations.
The response was as swift as it was dismissive. Less than 24 hours after the complaint was sub-
mitted, the POST Council rejected it. The council claimed the issues were outside its purview
and that it does not investigate allegations over five years old, despite the complaint detailing on-
going harm. In his frustrated reply, Nuno argued that the complaint was not about an isolated,
long-past incident but an “established pattern of conduct that extends beyond my case” and rep-
resents an “ongoing matter.” The council’s final response was blunt: “POST will not proceed with
an investigation of the allegations and this matter is closed.”
This rapid dismissal signifies a systemic refusal to investigate. It reinforces the impunity of the
involved parties and confirms to complainants that the official channels for accountability are ef-
fectively closed. This reality is reflected in the bleak assessment of one community member on
Reddit, who noted that in Missoula, “even the attorneys will support/work for the police, and not
their client. It’s one big interconnected scheme.” This official refusal to investigate leaves behind a
trail of damaged lives and a set of urgent, unanswered questions for regulators, funders, and the
public.
## 1.9 Unanswered Questions
This investigation, based on the available public and private record, paints a disturbing picture of
institutional capture and retaliatory power. But it does not close the book. Instead, it opens a series
of urgent questions that demand answers from the institutions entrusted with public safety, justice,
and the care of vulnerable citizens. The evidence points to critical gaps where decisions were
made behind closed doors, conflicts of interest were ignored, and oversight failed to materialize.
1. The Conflict of Interest For how long was the Missoula Police Department aware that a
sitting detective, Connie Brueckner, was simultaneously serving on the board of the YWCA,
an organization frequently involved in its investigations? What steps, if any, were ever taken
to manage this clear and “extreme conflict of interest”?
2. Suppression of Evidence Did YWCA leadership or Detective Brueckner ever disclose the
exculpatory character reference for Elvis Nuno—evidence that directly contradicted the stalk-
ing allegations—to prosecutors or defense counsel? If not, why was evidence that could have
undermined a malicious prosecution deliberately withheld?
3. Grant Oversight and Federal Funding How do federal funders like the U.S. Department of
Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and private foundations like the Bezos Day 1 Fund
ensure their multi-million dollar grants are not subsidizing organizations with documented
patterns of client discrimination, confidentiality breaches, and practices that actively create
homelessness?
4. Accountability and Oversight Why did the Montana POST Council refuse to investigate a
detailed, notarized complaint alleging a multi-year pattern of officer misconduct, dismissing
it in less than 24 hours? What effective, independent mechanism exists for citizens to hold
this tightly integrated system of police, prosecutors, and service providers accountable?
When institutions designed to serve the public instead serve themselves, they erode the very
foundation of civic trust. The experiences documented here reveal the profound human cost of
that erosion—a cost measured in lost careers, fractured families, and the denial of justice. The
unanswered questions left in the wake of this systemic failure are not merely rhetorical; they are a
call for genuine, structural reform.
