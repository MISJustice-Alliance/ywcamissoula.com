# Washington bar grievance questions Edmonds prosecutor’s handling of a plea-coercion report

Elvis Nuno says he filed a grievance with the Washington State Bar Association’s Office of Disciplinary Counsel on November 18, 2026, asking it to review how Edmonds prosecutor Aaron Gregory Walls handled repeated reports that an Edmonds police officer threatened a prospective witness with the loss of custody of a child. Nuno says he pleaded guilty because he feared that contesting the charges would expose that person and the child to the threatened harm. The grievance asks what Walls did after receiving the report. It does not ask the Bar to set aside a plea, resolve a warrant, or decide a probation motion. (Filed grievance, §§ I, V.)

The full grievance packet is not attached here because it contains third-party information requiring privacy protection. Its absence limits readers’ ability to check the cited grievance sections directly.

The correspondence raises a related question about the City’s jail recommendation. A prior MISJustice Alliance analysis of author-retained correspondence reports that in a February 4, 2026 email Walls described a 120-day jail position and discussed closure in the absence of new information. The original email was not available for this review. In November, he said the City would still seek 270 days even if Nuno supplied proof of treatment and electronic home monitoring. Walls cited subsequent convictions, but did not identify which convictions, if any, were new to the City since February or explain what accounted for the additional 150 days.

The request concerned the underlying report, not the later grievance or this article.   |

| November 17, 2026 | Nuno emailed Walls about a motion packet submitted in Edmonds Municipal Court and separately directed him to Section XIV of his notarized declaration. Nuno asked whether the City had referred the report for independent investigation and, if not, whether it would do so and provide a protected way to identify the witness.   |
| November 17–18, 2026 | Walls replied about warrants, court procedure, and sanctions. Nuno renewed the referral request on both dates. The correspondence through the November 18 follow-up does not say whether the City logged, assessed, or referred the report.   |

Walls’s September reply asked: “He agreed at the time of the plea that there had been no threats made, so is he lying now or lying then?” He also asked how Nuno knew “the victim was threatened” when a no-contact order prevented contact with her. The filed grievance acknowledges that Georgiades’s phrase “complaining witness” could reasonably have been read to mean the complainant in the no-contact-order case. It does not claim Walls knew in September that Nuno was referring to someone else.

In November, Nuno removed that ambiguity. He clarified that the person he said the officer threatened was not the person covered by the no-contact order. He offered to identify the threatened person if safeguards were in place. He also explained that he was not asking Walls for legal advice or to override the court’s warrant ruling. He was asking what the City had done, or would do, with a report about an officer’s conduct. Walls’s replies addressed when the court would hear Nuno’s motions but did not answer that separate question.

## What the City was told about compliance, case dispositions, and privacy

- Treatment and monitoring. Nuno says he had already submitted treatment documentation. He pointed to a February 1, 2021 docket entry, supplied by Walls, recording that probation had received a counseling report. The entry establishes receipt of a report, not completion of every treatment requirement. Walls acknowledged completion of the 2016 monitoring; the parties continued to dispute two later monitoring terms and whether earlier monitoring could count toward them. Nuno also says Walls asserted noncompliance in open court without first checking what had already been submitted. The emails document Nuno’s objection, but do not independently establish what Walls said in court or what he checked beforehand.
- The historical sanctions list. In November, Walls reproduced the City’s 2020 sanctions request, which listed Missoula stalking matters as “pending.” The Missoula stalking prosecution was dismissed with prejudice on January 5, 2021. Nuno asked Walls to distinguish convictions from matters listed as pending in 2020, police incident entries, and other alleged violations. The historical list does not establish current case status. Its reproduction also does not establish that Walls told the court the dismissed prosecution was still pending or counted it as a conviction in the 270-day recommendation.
- Third-party health information. Nuno sought production and preservation of evaluation records that he says disclosed his father’s private health information. His father was not a party to the criminal cases. Nuno asked the City to identify any copies it held, clarify whether they were protected from public access, and guard against further disclosure. The correspondence documents the concern and the safeguards Nuno requested; it does not establish who disclosed the information or whether any disclosure violated HIPAA.

These disputes matter because the earlier analysis reports a February 120-day jail position; it does not establish a court order. In November, he said the City **“would still ask for 270 days”** even if Nuno provided proof of treatment and electronic home monitoring. Walls cited subsequent convictions and said there were more than at the time of the City’s 2020 request. But that comparison does not identify what, if anything, the City learned after February 2026.

## A reported 150-day increase without identified new information

A conviction can be subsequent to the original sentence without being new to the City. Nuno says the Washington pleas followed a failed effort to negotiate a single resolution across jurisdictions. According to Nuno, prosecutors already knew his prior conviction history during those discussions but did not seek comparable increases in penalties before the pleas were entered. He says he entered the pleas after the reported custody threat. That is Nuno’s account of the sequence; the filed grievance leaves the precise plea dates unresolved. (Filed grievance, § III.)

Walls’s November email compared the number of convictions with the City’s 2020 request, not the February 2026 position reported in the earlier analysis. It did not identify a conviction entered or newly discovered since February, or explain why any convictions already known to the City warranted another 150 days. The older sanctions list does not fill that gap: it includes matters described as pending in 2020 alongside convictions and other alleged violations. Walls gave a reason for seeking jail time; his reply did not explain the increase from the February position.

That unexplained increase raises a **retaliation concern**. Walls communicated the higher recommendation in the same exchange in which Nuno challenged the City’s compliance assertions, sought records concerning his father’s private health information, and pressed for independent review of the reported custody threat. The timing and the absence of identified new information make the increase appear potentially retaliatory. They do not establish when the City decided to seek 270 days, whether it had information it did not identify in the exchange, or whether Nuno’s requests influenced the decision. The email establishes the City’s stated position, not that it presented the recommendation in court or that a judge imposed it. Retaliation is a concern raised by the correspondence, not a separate count in the filed grievance.

## What the grievance asks the Bar to examine

The grievance asks Disciplinary Counsel to assess two distinct duties under Washington’s Rules of Professional Conduct. It does not assert that either rule was violated. (Filed grievance, §§ IV–V.)

- RPC 3.8(g) and (i): Postconviction disclosure and inquiry. When a prosecutor knows of new, credible, and material evidence creating a reasonable likelihood that a convicted person did not commit the offense, the rule requires prompt disclosure to an appropriate court or authority. If the conviction was obtained in the prosecutor’s own jurisdiction, it also requires prompt disclosure to the defendant unless a court authorizes delay, and reasonable efforts to inquire or cause an appropriate investigation. An independent, good-faith judgment that the threshold was not met is not a violation even if it later proves mistaken. The grievance asks for a conviction-by-conviction assessment: evidence that a threat influenced a plea decision does not, by itself, establish a reasonable likelihood of factual innocence. [courts.wa](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/RPC/GA_RPC_03_08_00.pdf)
- RPC 3.8(d): Mitigating information. In connection with sentencing, the rule requires disclosure of known, unprivileged mitigating information to the defense and the tribunal unless a protective order relieves the prosecutor of that responsibility. Nuno does not allege that Walls withheld from the defense a report the defense supplied. The questions are whether the City presented its recommendation in a proceeding to which that duty applied, whether the report qualified as mitigating information, and, if so, what the City disclosed to the tribunal. The November email does not resolve those questions.

The grievance also cites CrRLJ 4.2(d) and (f), which address plea voluntariness and withdrawal in courts of limited jurisdiction, and RCW 9A.72.110, Washington’s witness-intimidation statute. It cites those provisions for context, not as independent duties requiring Walls to investigate or as findings that an officer committed a crime or a plea was involuntary. [courts.wa](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/CrRLJ/CLJ_CrRLJ_04_02_00.pdf)

## Which proceedings the filed grievance identifies

The filed grievance identifies **five related Washington proceedings**. It does not say that every case ended in a plea challenged on the same basis or that the City relied on every case in its sanctions recommendation. Their case numbers and charge-by-court breakdown are omitted here to reduce the risk of identifying a third party. (Filed grievance, § III.)

A separate Edmonds municipal matter appears in the November correspondence because its number was used to submit the motion packet. The filed grievance does not list that matter among the five proceedings related to the custody-threat account.

The grievance leaves the chronology unresolved. Nuno’s declaration and the later outreach differ about the timing of the threat and pleas; docket excerpts Walls supplied identify a later plea and sentencing date in the two related Edmonds cases. The grievance also flags differences between the outreach and the sworn account about how the officer was described, the outreach’s assertion that the officer acted on behalf of the complainant, and which courts were involved. Nuno relies on the outreach to show what was sent to Walls, not to adopt its factual or legal characterizations. (Filed grievance, § III.)

## What remains unanswered

Two questions can be answered without assuming anyone’s motive. First, after Nuno clarified which person was reportedly threatened and offered a protected way to identify that person, did Walls or the City assess the report, consider that offer, or refer the matter? Second, which convictions supported the 270-day recommendation, when did the City learn of them, and what changed between February and November? The correspondence establishes what the parties wrote. It does not establish the City’s internal decisions or what it presented to the court.

Filing the grievance does not establish that the officer made the threat, that a court found a plea involuntary, or that Walls retaliated or violated an ethics rule. The materials reviewed for this article do not establish a disciplinary determination.
