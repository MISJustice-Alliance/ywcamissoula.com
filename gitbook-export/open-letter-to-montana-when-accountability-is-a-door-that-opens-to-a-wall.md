# Open Letter to Montana

## When Accountability Is a Door That Opens to a Wall

*Elvis Nuno — MISJustice Alliance | September 28, 2026*

{% include ".gitbook/includes/analysis-page-disclaimer-seo-geo-standard.md" %}

***

To Montana's public institutions, legal community, civil-rights advocates, elected officials, journalists, and the people who believe there must be a meaningful way to seek accountability:

I am writing because I have spent years trying to use the systems Montana tells people are available when they believe they have been harmed by government action, law-enforcement conduct, prosecutorial decisions, professional failures, institutional indifference, or the misuse of legal process.

I have filed complaints.

I have submitted written chronology, correspondence, records, and specific factual questions.

I have attempted to work through courts, law enforcement, oversight channels, public-defender systems, professional disciplinary processes, public-records requests, and institutional grievance procedures.

I have tried to do what people are told to do when they believe something has gone seriously wrong: document it, report it, preserve evidence, follow procedure, remain respectful, and use the proper channels.

What I have found is that the channel often exists only on paper.

A person is invited to report harm. They submit the evidence. They wait. They follow instructions. They provide more detail. They are referred elsewhere. They receive a sparse response, a conclusory closure, an unexplained delay, or no meaningful answer at all.

The door appears to open.

Behind it is a wall.

## A Process That Will Not Explain Itself

My central concern is not that every complaint must produce discipline, criminal charges, civil damages, or the outcome desired by the complainant.

A legitimate process may investigate a matter and conclude that the evidence does not support action.

But a legitimate process must be able to explain itself.

It must be able to say:

- What allegation was reviewed.
- What documents were considered.
- What witnesses or agencies were contacted.
- What governing standard was used.
- What facts were found, disputed, or left unresolved.
- What evidence was considered insufficient and why.
- Whether a conflict existed.
- What review, appeal, correction, or reconsideration process remains available.

Without those answers, an institution has not demonstrated accountability. It has demonstrated only that it can receive information and issue a conclusion.

That distinction matters.

When a person submits detailed evidence and receives only a summary dismissal, a form response, or an unexplained conclusion, the public has no way to know whether the record was seriously reviewed or merely processed.

"Not warranted" is not a reason.

"Strategy" is not a reason.

"Outside our authority" is not a reason unless the office identifies the authority that does exist and provides a meaningful referral.

A grievance system that cannot explain its decisions is not transparent. It is a black box.

## The Montana Problem

My Montana experience has involved more than one institution and more than one kind of concern.

I have raised concerns regarding alleged false or unsupported allegations, law-enforcement response, prosecutorial actions, court procedures, attorney performance, institutional conflicts, the handling of protected speech, and the inability to obtain a meaningful investigation or remedy after harm has already occurred.

I have repeatedly tried to distinguish between what I know firsthand, what the documentary record appears to show, what I allege, and what remains unresolved.

I am not asking institutions to accept my account without review.

I am asking them to conduct the review.

The public record and my own filed materials reflect that I made formal written complaints to Montana institutions concerning conduct I believed had harmed me and my family. In 2018, for example, I sent a written grievance to YWCA Missoula asking the organization to review alleged conduct by staff and to ensure that the conduct stopped. That letter reflects an attempt to use internal channels rather than bypass them.

In 2020, I also submitted a detailed written complaint to the Missoula Mayor's Office describing allegations of institutional and law-enforcement misconduct, serious personal consequences, and a request for investigation. That document reflects the scale of the concerns I was attempting to place before local government. It is my account and allegation, not a final adjudication of the matters described.

The question is not whether every fact alleged in those complaints was already proven by the act of filing them.

The question is: **what meaningful investigation, explanation, correction, or independent review followed?**

## The Cost of Fragmented Responsibility

The gravest harm need not arise from a proven coordinated conspiracy.

It can arise when every institution handles only its own narrow part, charges for its own part, and imposes its own demands and consequences without regard for the overall outcome or cumulative human cost—while no one accepts responsibility for the combined result.

In Montana, a person may encounter a police agency, a prosecutor, a court, a defense office, a nonprofit institution, a grievance office, a disciplinary authority, and a public-records custodian.

Each may see only one file.

Each may claim limited jurisdiction.

Each may say that another office must address the larger issue.

But the person at the center of the process experiences the result as one continuous reality.

They experience:

- Repeated legal demands.
- Lost work and income.
- Legal expense.
- Travel and logistical burdens.
- Public allegations.
- Fear of further escalation.
- Difficulty rebuilding professional stability.
- The strain placed on family members.
- The psychological burden of never receiving a full answer.
- The erosion of confidence that the next complaint will be treated differently.

This is how a person can lose stability without any single office acknowledging the full damage.

One office may say it only handled a report.

Another may say it only handled a charge.

Another may say it only handled a hearing.

Another may say it only handled a grievance.

Another may say it only handled a fee, a deadline, a discovery request, or a procedural decision.

But when every office handles only one narrow part, no one is responsible for asking what all of those parts have done to a human life.

## Protected Speech and Petitioning for Redress

A central concern in Montana is whether people can safely petition institutions for redress.

A person should be able to write a complaint, report misconduct, request an investigation, criticize an organization, seek records, or raise concern about public conduct without reasonably fearing that the act of speaking will itself become the basis for escalation.

That principle matters most when the complaint is directed toward powerful institutions: law enforcement, prosecutors, courts, attorneys, government-connected organizations, or public-facing nonprofits.

The question is not whether every complaint is beyond scrutiny. The question is whether institutions distinguish protected petitioning, criticism, and reporting from conduct that genuinely threatens or endangers others.

When a person believes that criticism, complaints, or requests for accountability have been mischaracterized, used against them, or met with escalating legal pressure, there must be a credible independent mechanism to review that concern.

No institution should be permitted to investigate itself in secret, close the matter without explanation, and then treat the person who complained as the problem.

## The Questions Montana Must Answer

I ask Montana institutions to answer the following questions publicly and in writing:

1. **What meaningful grievance process exists when the complaint concerns multiple interconnected institutions?**
   If law enforcement, prosecution, court procedures, legal representation, and a public-facing institution are all implicated in a person's concerns, where can that person obtain an independent whole-of-record review?

2. **Who investigates when the institution being asked to investigate may have a conflict?**
   What independent authority exists when a complaint involves an agency's own employees, close institutional partners, or people with overlapping professional relationships?

3. **What must an agency provide when it closes a complaint?**
   Will it identify the allegations reviewed, evidence considered, standard applied, findings reached, and appeal or reconsideration rights?

4. **How can a person correct an inaccurate or incomplete government record?**
   What process exists when a disputed police report, court record, allegation, or administrative entry continues to create legal, professional, or reputational harm?

5. **What protection exists against retaliation?**
   How can a person report misconduct, request records, file a complaint, or petition for redress without facing adverse treatment tied to the act of reporting?

6. **Where is the restoration process?**
   When years of fragmented procedures have damaged a person's ability to work, care for family, maintain housing, pursue opportunity, or rebuild, what institution is responsible for looking at the cumulative impact and providing a path toward correction?

## What Accountability Should Look Like

I am not asking Montana for special treatment.

I am asking for the minimum characteristics of an accountable system:

- Reasoned written decisions.
- Clear procedures and deadlines.
- Independent review when conflicts exist.
- Preservation and disclosure of relevant records.
- A meaningful opportunity to correct errors.
- Protection for those who petition for redress.
- Transparent explanations for why action was or was not taken.
- A whole-of-record process for people harmed by overlapping institutional failures.

If institutions can demand compliance, impose conditions, initiate proceedings, collect fees, restrict liberty, or create records that follow a person for years, then institutions must also be able to explain their actions and correct their mistakes.

Accountability cannot mean only that an office has an intake form.

It must mean that a person can reach a decision-maker, understand the process, see what was reviewed, challenge errors, and obtain a reasoned answer.

## Closing

I have tried to use the processes Montana provides.

I have reported concerns, documented events, provided records, asked questions, sought review, and attempted to work within the system.

I am still asking for a meaningful answer.

My question to Montana is simple:

> **When a person has followed every instruction—reported, documented, filed, waited, appealed, and asked—and every pathway ends in a conclusory response, silence, or referral to another office, where does that person go?**

A system that cannot answer that question is not yet accountable.
