# When Separate Complaints Point to One System: The Case for Collective Civil-Rights Litigation in Missoula

*A serious case for collective litigation does not begin by declaring a conspiracy. It begins by asking whether different people were subjected to the same unlawful mechanism—and then obtaining the records that can prove or disprove it.*

* **Prepared by:** MISJustice Alliance — Civil Rights Advocacy Team
* **Published:** October 12, 2026 | Legal Analysis & Advocacy Series
* **Intended audience:** Attorneys, legal researchers, journalists, and civil-rights advocates

{% hint style="warning" %}
This article distinguishes allegations, source records, legal inferences, and adjudicated findings. It does not assert that any person or institution committed a civil or criminal offense. It is public-interest legal analysis, not legal advice.
{% endhint %}

Missoula residents who say they were harmed in encounters involving the Missoula Police Department, local prosecutors, and YWCA Missoula deserve more than a collection of disconnected anecdotes. They deserve a disciplined inquiry into whether recurring relationships among public officials and a private service provider produced recurring violations of constitutional or statutory rights.

That inquiry could ultimately support coordinated civil-rights suits, a narrowly defined class action, or—if evidence of qualifying criminal predicates and economic injury emerges—a civil claim under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act. But the public record and advocacy materials reviewed for this article do not yet establish any of those institutions’ liability, much less a criminal enterprise. They establish something more preliminary and still important: a litigation-grade investigation is warranted.

That distinction matters. Civil-rights advocates lose credibility when allegations are converted into findings, institutional relationships into conspiracies, or email traffic into “wire fraud” by label alone. Attorneys considering this matter should instead build from the strongest provable events outward: allegedly deceptive warrant applications, searches that may have exceeded lawful scope, warrantless entry or detention, prosecution allegedly caused by false or omitted evidence, and retaliation for protected speech. Only then should they ask whether the same mechanism affected other people and whether it can be traced to policy, coordinated action, or racketeering predicates.

## The strongest case starts with the Fourth Amendment

The reviewed assessment identifies the most developed potential theory as “judicial deception”: the claim that an officer deliberately or recklessly used a material falsehood or omission to obtain legal process. Under *Franks v. Delaware*, a court tests such a claim by removing deliberate or reckless false statements, adding material omissions, and asking whether the corrected affidavit still establishes probable cause. The Ninth Circuit likewise requires materiality and more than negligence.[^1]

That makes the next step concrete. Counsel should assemble a line-by-line “corrected affidavit” chart showing the exact warrant language, the underlying interview or record, the alleged defect, the neutral correction, evidence of the affiant’s state of mind, and the effect on probable cause. If probable cause survives the correction, the claim weakens sharply. If it does not—and the evidence supports deliberate or reckless deception—the claim may become the anchor for a federal civil-rights action under 42 U.S.C. §1983.

Other events must be separated rather than blended into a general accusation. Execution of a warrant is legally different from a later warrantless entry, arrest, or detention. A digital search may be lawful at its inception yet unconstitutional in scope or execution. A prosecution dismissed in the accused person’s favor may support a Fourth Amendment malicious-prosecution claim, but only if the plaintiff can establish the absence of probable cause and the conduct of a nonimmune defendant who caused the prosecution. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Thompson v. Clark* clarified the favorable-termination element; it did not eliminate probable cause, causation, or immunity.[^2]

This approach is narrower than a theory of institution-wide misconduct, but it is also stronger. Certified dockets, complete warrant materials, body-camera video, dispatch logs, booking and detainer records, native emails, forensic-search logs, and disclosure indexes can be tested. General narratives cannot.

## Why police liability does not automatically become municipal liability

Even a proven constitutional violation by an officer does not automatically make a city or county liable. Under *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, a municipality is responsible only when an official policy, widespread custom, final-policymaker decision, qualifying ratification, or deliberately indifferent training or supervision failure caused the violation. Respondeat superior—the ordinary rule that an employer answers for an employee—is unavailable under §1983.[^3]

That is where other complainants may become legally important. Ten stories involving the same institutions are not necessarily evidence of one policy. Ten authenticated incidents involving the same misleading warrant language, the same disclosure protocol, the same supervisory approval, or the same known training defect might be.

The required comparison is functional, not rhetorical. Investigators should code each incident by date, actor, challenged conduct, constitutional right, policy mechanism, source quality, disposition, notice to supervisors, limitations period, and contradictory evidence. Reviews, anonymous posts, and advocacy summaries are leads, not proof. Post-event silence or failure to discipline may be relevant in context, but ordinarily cannot by itself establish the preexisting policy that caused an earlier injury. Failure-to-train liability is especially demanding: *Connick v. Thompson* generally requires a pattern of similar violations sufficient to put policymakers on notice of the specific deficiency.[^4]

## Prosecutors are not beyond scrutiny—but immunity shapes the suit

Any public article must distinguish institutional accountability from a damages claim against an individual prosecutor. Prosecutors generally receive absolute immunity for conduct intimately associated with the judicial phase of a criminal case, including initiating a prosecution and presenting the state’s case. They do not necessarily receive that protection when acting as investigators, administrators, or complaining witnesses. The boundary is functional, not occupational.[^5]

That means a viable complaint must identify conduct, not merely offices. Charging decisions, courtroom advocacy, and evaluation of evidence are commonly immune. Fabricating evidence during an investigative phase, personally attesting to facts as a witness, or performing some administrative acts may be treated differently. Claims directed generically at “the prosecutor’s office” invite early dismissal unless counsel identifies the proper governmental entity, the responsible actor, the function performed, and the causal link to a recognized injury.

## The YWCA question turns on conduct, not proximity to government

YWCA Missoula is a private nonprofit. Private organizations do not become state actors merely because they receive public funding, cooperate with police, participate in coordinated-response programs, make referrals, or have relationships with public officials.

Section 1983 liability would require a recognized state-action theory tied to the particular deprivation—for example, evidence of willful joint participation in developing materially false evidence, directing official retaliation, suppressing material information as part of a shared plan, or exercising authority delegated by the state. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the inquiry is fact-specific and that extensive regulation or public funding alone is insufficient.[^6]

The assessment reviewed for this article flags an alleged overlap between an investigator and YWCA governance. That is a legitimate records lead, not proof of joint action or conflict. The dates matter. Contemporary Forms 990, board rosters, minutes, appointment records, conflict disclosures, and communications would have to show who served when and what, if anything, the relationship had to do with a challenged act.

Even if federal state action cannot be shown, specific conduct could still implicate Montana law governing privacy, confidentiality, defamation, contract, negligence, or interference. Those claims must be analyzed independently, including duties, privileges, limitation periods, causation, and damages.

## What a viable class action would—and would not—look like

An umbrella class made up of people alleging retaliation, prosecution, confidentiality breaches, shelter-service failures, digital searches, and reputational harm would likely collapse under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. The claims would turn on different defendants, dates, communications, probable-cause determinations, privileges, immunities, injuries, and defenses.

Rule 23 does not ask only whether class members share a broad complaint. It requires common questions capable of generating common answers, representative claims typical of the class, adequate representation, and a class form suited to the remedy. A damages class also must show that common issues predominate over individual ones. The Supreme Court rejected “trial by formula” in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, explaining that a common question must resolve an issue central to every class member’s claim in one stroke.[^7]

A narrower class is more conceivable. Examples might include people whose accounts or devices were searched using materially identical warrant language during a defined period, or people whose protected information was disclosed under one documented policy. Such a case would still require a class definition grounded in records, a representative with standing, a viable underlying claim, a reliable method to identify members, and common proof. For prospective injunctive relief, the representative must also face a real and immediate threat of future injury; a past encounter alone is generally insufficient.[^8]

If no uniform mechanism emerges, collective accountability remains possible through individual suits, permissive joinder, coordinated discovery, consolidation of related actions, or use of comparable incidents as *Monell* evidence. Class certification is a procedural tool, not a measure of whether reported harm matters.

## RICO is a possibility to investigate, not a label to deploy

Civil RICO is tempting in cases alleging coordinated institutional misconduct because it offers treble damages and attorney’s fees. It is also easy to plead badly.

A civil plaintiff must prove a violation of 18 U.S.C. §1962, standing under §1964(c), and injury to business or property proximately caused by the RICO violation. A claim under §1962(c) ordinarily requires a RICO “person,” an enterprise distinct from that person, the defendant’s participation in the enterprise’s operation or management, and a pattern of related racketeering predicates showing continuity.[^9]

The present materials do not yet clear those gates. Constitutional violations, HIPAA violations, defamation, negligence, false reports, perjury, ethical breaches, and institutional nonresponse are not automatically RICO predicates. Nor does sending an email transform alleged wrongdoing into wire fraud. Mail and wire fraud require, among other things, a material scheme aimed at obtaining money or property. Recent Supreme Court decisions have insisted on that property focus.[^10]

Nor is an “institutional ecosystem” necessarily a RICO enterprise. Counsel would need evidence of a continuing unit with a common unlawful purpose and proof that each §1962(c) defendant helped direct its affairs. Ordinary employment, board service, referrals, information receipt, and parallel institutional interests are not enough.

Finally, the claimed economic loss must be directly caused by the predicate racketeering conduct. The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in *Medical Marijuana, Inc. v. Horn* held that RICO does not categorically exclude every business or property loss that follows a personal injury. But the Court did not dispense with a qualifying property interest, a proven §1962 violation, direct causation, or the bar against recovering for the personal injury itself.[^11]

The responsible “RICO stop rule” is therefore simple: do not plead the claim unless the evidence identifies at least two related, enumerated predicates; a coherent enterprise; defendant-specific operation or management; continuity; and direct injury to business or property. Grant applications, reimbursement claims, audits, certifications, native communications, and financial records may reveal a property-oriented scheme—or may show that no RICO case exists. Either answer is preferable to accusation by acronym.

## A public-interest roadmap

The case for action now is the case for disciplined evidence gathering.

First, preserve native files, email headers, attachments, recordings, metadata, public-record exports, and chain-of-custody information. Second, obtain certified court files, complete warrant materials, body-camera and dispatch records, custody and interstate-communication records, prosecutor and defense discovery indexes, contemporaneous governance documents, and relevant contracts or grant records. Third, use a standardized, nonleading intake process for every potential claimant. Fourth, calculate limitation periods claim by claim; later discovery of a broader institutional theory generally does not restart the clock for a known search, arrest, detention, prosecution, or publication. Fifth, invite each named institution to respond to specific, sourced propositions before publication or filing.

The evidentiary categories should remain visible throughout:

- An official or authenticated record can establish what the record says and that the recorded event occurred, subject to completeness and admissibility.
- A sworn firsthand account can support what the witness personally observed.
- A corroborated allegation can justify deeper merits analysis.
- A complaint, criminal referral, advocacy memorandum, public review, or anonymous post is a lead—not a finding.
- A relationship diagram, projected victim count, damages estimate, or legal inference is a hypothesis until proved.

This framework protects both potential claimants and the integrity of the investigation. It also reduces publication risk. Reports should say that a person “alleges,” a filing “states,” or records “show” only what the cited source actually supports. Fair and accurate reporting on official proceedings may receive legal protection, but it is not a license to embellish a filing or present disputed accusations as established fact.

## The real case for collective litigation

The question is not whether one can tell a broad story connecting Missoula police, prosecutors, and YWCA Missoula. One can. The question is whether admissible evidence will show that particular actors repeatedly used the same unlawful means—and whether those means caused legally cognizable injuries.

If corrected-affidavit analysis reveals deliberate and material deception; if objective records establish warrantless custody; if communications show willful joint action; if comparable incidents identify a policy known to policymakers; or if financial and communication records reveal enumerated racketeering predicates, then coordinated litigation may be not only viable but necessary. A narrow class could remedy a uniform policy. Individual or joined §1983 actions could address distinct constitutional injuries. A properly supported RICO claim could reach a durable enterprise organized around property-directed fraud.

If the evidence does not show those things, advocates should say so. Accountability is not advanced by forcing every wrong into the most dramatic cause of action.

The present record supports neither complacency nor a verdict by press release. It supports preservation demands, public-record requests, neutral claimant screening, independent legal review, and a methodical effort to determine whether separate complaints describe separate failures—or one system.
