# When 'Time-Barred' and 'Strategy' End the Inquiry: The Duty to Give Reasons

**Analysis / opinion**

> **About this article:** A document-based examination of the Lowney clarification request and ODC's November 5 response, alongside the distinct Tipp file. It tests whether the stated grounds can be traced to the disputed allegations; it does not decide whether either grievance should have succeeded.

## Executive Summary

The Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC) gave reasons for dismissing two grievances: a Rule 11 time bar in the Lowney file and, in the Tipp file, a substantive determination later followed by “already addressed” and strategy explanations. The new development is not another disposition. On November 5, 2026, Chief Disciplinary Counsel Pamela D. Bucy responded to a narrowed request about **Lowney file 26-145** by saying ODC's dismissal correspondence already explained its reasoning, declining further information and directing Elvis R. Nuno to Supreme Court review. She did not identify the passage he had requested about a particular allegation. That juxtaposition—not an absence of any response—is the issue. When an office treats the explanation as complete and ends correspondence, the complainant must decide what to contest without a disclosed account of how the contested dates and speech-related allegation fit its stated ground. [November 4–5 email exchange, address-redacted PDF](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/odc-26-145-november-5-2026-bucy-response-email-address-redacted.pdf); [public evidence ledger](../montana-bar-complaints/odc-review-evidence-ledger-2026.md).

## The request narrowed; the answer closed correspondence

The forwarded message in the new PDF bears a **November 4, 2026, 1:34 p.m.** sent header. Nuno expressly wrote: “I am not asking ODC to agree with me, provide an advisory ethics opinion, or repeat the instructions for seeking further review.” He also disclaimed an automatic entitlement to “every internal document or a separate answer to every legal question I have raised.” Instead he asked ODC to “identify the document and relevant passage that state the facts and reasons supporting dismissal of ODC File No. 26-145.” If a separate explanation existed, he asked for an electronic copy; if the existing notice sufficed, he asked for the language addressing his allegation of leniency allegedly conditioned on removing or refraining from protected speech; if there was no further explanation, he asked ODC to say so and explain how the existing one satisfied the provisions he cited. He explicitly said the clarification request did not extend any review deadline. [Email exchange, pp. 1–3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/odc-26-145-november-5-2026-bucy-response-email-address-redacted.pdf). An earlier sent export showed 1:33 p.m.; the forwarded header says 1:34 p.m. We cannot resolve the one-minute display difference from these copies and use the new PDF's header for this exchange.

Bucy's **November 5, 8:14 a.m.** reply says, in full apart from salutation and signature:

> “Our dismissal correspondence explained our reasoning for dismissal. You will not be receiving any additional information from us regarding our work. Your next available remedy is an appeal to the Supreme Court. You can raise your issues with them as Ms. Broadwater explained. Please refrain from further correspondence with us. Thank you.”

That is a response and a definite institutional position: ODC regards its prior explanation as sufficient and wants further issues raised through review, not another exchange with staff. The word “appeal” is Bucy's characterization; the applicable rule describes a request for discretionary Supreme Court review after Commission affirmance. The reply identifies no paragraph applying the time limit to the pleaded later conduct, no passage dealing with the speech-related allegation, and no separate explanation to send. It does not say whether another document exists or establish that none was created. These are distinct from a demand for the investigative file. The emailed footer's confidentiality notice does not itself settle what may be quoted from this recipient's copy; the public PDF obscures unnecessary addresses. [Email exchange, p. 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/odc-26-145-november-5-2026-bucy-response-email-address-redacted.pdf).

The table describes what is *disclosed*, not every step ODC or the panel may have taken internally. No rule cited here independently requires an answer to each question or release of an investigative report. The accountability criticism is that closing the dialogue at a general reference to prior letters leaves their application to the contested allegations untraceable.

## Reasons, not an entitlement to the file

The Montana Supreme Court's [2021 Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement and October 12, 2021 amendment order](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=368130) supply the text governing these dispositions: the underlying rules took effect January 1, 2021, and the attached 2021 revision to Rule 26A took effect immediately. Rule 10C(1)(b) allows dismissal without some or all of the enumerated investigation. The [Court's AF 06-0628 docket](https://supremecourtdocket.mt.gov/case-info/active/11218) records amendments adopted in July 2026, generally effective January 1, 2027; they are not applied retroactively here. The [Court's attorney-complaints resource](https://courts.mt.gov/forms/AttorneyComplaints) describes the offices' distinct roles, although its linked rule PDF is an older version, not the governing text quoted here. No controlling interpretation of the required specificity in these circumstances was verified. Yet Rule 5B(10) directs Disciplinary Counsel on dismissal to “provide a concise written statement of the facts and reasons a matter has been dismissed.” Rule 10C(2), “Notice of Disposition,” requires written notice “stating the reasons for the action taken, and advising the grievant of the right to request review of the dismissal.” In the reviewed notices and follow-up letters, ODC did not point Nuno to *these explanation provisions* or explain how their language applied to the questioned factual grounds. That matters editorially; it does not create a freestanding legal requirement to cite a rule number. ODC did cite Rule 11 in Lowney's time-bar notice and gave review directions in both original dismissals. An assertion that ODC “never disclosed its own governing rules” would therefore overstate this record. Rule 10C(3) permits written Review Panel review; Rule 14A describes further discretionary Court review after panel affirmance. Rule 20A makes pre-formal-Complaint disciplinary information confidential, subject to exceptions. The rules require a private explanation to the grievant, not public disclosure of the investigative file or a response to every subsequent question.

A concise answer might identify the controlling threshold and the allegation to which it applies. If timing ends the inquiry, say which alleged act was too old and why the pleaded later acts do not alter that conclusion. If an allegation was already evaluated as strategy, say whether a later document is regarded as merely another instance of the same category. Those are proposed practices, not quoted additional duties in Rule 10.

## Two files show the difference between an answer and an answer one can trace

The objection to ODC's November 3, 2025 Tipp determination is not that it was literally a single word. It is that the reasoning Nuno received did not make the resolution of his *actual* complaint intelligible. His [October 4 reply to Tipp](../montana-bar-complaints/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-octo/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-october-4th-2025.md) explicitly conceded that Tipp was retained for criminal defense, not civil litigation. Nuno alleged instead that, while representing him, Tipp discouraged civil action, did not communicate the particular filing deadlines Nuno needed to understand, and declined a requested letter recommending that police refer alleged wrongdoing to state criminal investigators. The requested *criminal-investigation referral letter* is distinct from a referral to civil counsel; the former concerns alleged conduct affecting Nuno personally and professionally, not whether Tipp had been retained to sue. Nuno argues that declining that specific request left the alleged offenses and their effects unpursued even while Tipp worked to end the existing prosecution. The letter to Lowney below cannot establish what Tipp said in the separate client email or whether the alleged conduct was criminal. The [ODC decision, pp. 2–4](../montana-bar-complaints/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-octo/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-odc-ruling-november-2025.md) *summarized* the civil-advice distinction, then accepted the defense account that Tipp had warned of potential time limits and encouraged outside counsel, and found adequate communication and no violation within the criminal engagement. That is a substantive adverse finding, not silence. But the written analysis does not identify which deadline advice was given, when, or what it said, or evaluate the distinct requested criminal-referral letter; its return to engagement scope and the favorable criminal disposition leaves those disputed questions insufficiently traceable. The original October and December 2020 client-email exchange has not been independently examined for this update, so the precise wording and any alternatives offered require source review. This criticism of the explanation is not a finding that Tipp misadvised Nuno or that ODC intentionally obscured its reasoning.

Tipp's [December 23, 2020 letter to prosecutor Brian Lowney, pp. 1–2](../.gitbook/assets/tipp-lowney-letter-december-23-2020.pdf) gives the stakes a contemporaneous anchor. Tipp reported that Lowney had heard complaints about Nuno's speech and that online comments attributed by Tipp to E'Lise “(and perhaps some of her friends)” called Nuno a “woman beater and stalker,” causing the organization Nuno worked with and Nuno “great consternation.” Tipp also reported that several employment opportunities had been withdrawn after background checks, and pressed Lowney to seek dismissal *with prejudice* because the earlier dismissal without prejudice left third parties unable to verify that legal jeopardy had ended. These are Tipp's reports and advocacy in a letter, not independent proof of who posted comments, whether statements were false, or why jobs were lost. The letter also cuts against any assertion that Tipp did nothing for his client: it documents a concrete effort to secure final criminal dismissal. Nuno argues that the eventual dismissal did not remedy the time spent under the deferred prosecution agreement, the alleged speech restrictions, missed civil opportunities, or the economic and reputational harms he reports. He further alleges that the resolution spared E'Lise Chard's statements and the YWCA from closer public scrutiny; his claim that her petition and hearing testimony contradicted each other requires the underlying records, and incidental avoidance of scrutiny would not establish Tipp's motive. The letter alone does not establish the agreement's exact speech terms, a causal link to business collapse, or that a civil claim was legally viable. What ODC's explanation does not separately evaluate is whether the criminal outcome answered those *different* asserted harms and communication duties. [November determination, pp. 3–4](../montana-bar-complaints/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-octo/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-odc-ruling-november-2025.md).

In July 2026, after Nuno raised a witness email and a medical letter, ODC said the material was already addressed and then called it additional examples of “strategy.” Strategy is a legal and factual category, not a magic word that resolves whether counsel saw a document, assessed its significance, or made an informed choice not to use it. The letters do not say which of those happened here. A categorical determination could be sufficient if ODC reasonably regarded both exhibits as immaterial to its earlier analysis; the available letters do not expose that reasoning or identify the explanation from Tipp they invoke. What is missing from the written response is a traceable account of which material the conclusion covers and how the stated strategy rationale addresses the alleged omissions. [July 10 letter](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-25-147-july-2026-closure-response-analysis); [July 30 letter](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-file-no-25-147-july-30-2026-closure-letter-technicality-defense-analysis).

The second file, 26-145 concerning Brian C. Lowney, is not a duplicate of the first. ODC's June 9, 2026 notice invoked a limitations rule: “your charges happened in 2019,” beyond the six-year period, without pleaded tolling or exception. It thus declined to reach the prosecutor-misconduct allegations on their merits. A time bar is a potentially legitimate threshold decision, not inherently a trick or an admission that the allegations were true. But the April 28 grievance describes alleged conduct from 2018 through January 2021, and Nuno's [published Commission review submission](../montana-bar-complaints/request-for-commission-on-practice-review-odc-26-145-brian-lowney.md) argues later discovery, continuing conduct, tolling and an exception. Those are his arguments, not resolved findings. The notice does not identify which acts it assigned to 2019 or why the pleaded later period and argued timing theories did not change its result. Rule 11A's text frames a six-year limit on filing a formal disciplinary Complaint, with discovery language; it does not simply say that no grievance may be submitted after six years. This distinction does not decide whether ODC's threshold application here was correct. Its citation also needs checking: it calls Rule 11D an exception, while the court-adopted 2021 text places the exceptions at Rule 11E. The Commission's October 23 affirmance says it considered the complaint, ODC file/report and a July 14 appeal letter and concluded that the facts did not demonstrate clear-and-convincing evidence of ethical violations. That is the panel's stated standard and conclusion, not an independently verified holding about the threshold appropriate to each procedural step. Its letter does not say whether it adopted the time bar, supplied an independent merits ground, or evaluated only part of the alleged conduct. Bucy's November 5 email chooses not to clarify that relationship and instead refers to prior correspondence and further review. [Lowney grievance](../montana-bar-complaints/grievance-to-montana-odc-brian-lowney-4.28.2026.md); [ODC notice](../montana-bar-complaints/odc-26-145-rejection-brian-lowney-june-9-2026.md); [Commission letter and source ledger](../montana-bar-complaints/odc-review-evidence-ledger-2026.md). The grievance's characterization of Lowney's conduct remains an allegation.

This is why the distinction between **a reason exists** and **its application is intelligible** matters. Asking the first question alone yields an easy but incomplete answer; calling every unsatisfying answer silence erases the reasons that really were given. The better question is which disputed issue the existing ground disposes of, and which issue remains outside its stated scope.

## Review depends on knowing what to contest

A request for review can challenge a timing premise, a factual description, or a professional-duty judgment. Those call for different arguments. Rule 10C(3) provides panel review on a record, not an automatic fresh investigation; Rule 14 addresses discretionary Court review after panel affirmance. If the June ODC letter is dispositive, Nuno could contest its 2019 premise and the application of Rule 11 to pleaded later conduct. If the panel's evidentiary formulation is an independent ground, he would need to address that premise too. Bucy's refusal to identify the requested passage leaves him to infer the relationship from the two notices. This is a practical burden, not proof that legal review was denied or a deadline suspended. The Tipp matter reached the Court, which affirmed the Commission's disposition on its account of an untimely request. The Lowney matter reached the Commission; the new email identifies a next step but does not establish a later Court filing or decision. [Rules 10C(3), 14](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=368130); [October outcomes](../montana-bar-complaints/tipp-lowney-disciplinary-decisions-october-2026.md).

## The strongest objection

ODC's strongest answer is now on the record: it considers its dismissal correspondence adequate and sees review—not continuing correspondence—as the way forward. The reasons rule calls for concision, not a miniature judicial opinion; confidentiality protects participants; and disciplinary counsel may dismiss without every investigative step. The Tipp determination recites potential-limitations warnings and encouragement to seek outside counsel; Tipp's 2020 letter documents advocacy for dismissal with prejudice. The Lowney notice gives a genuine time-based ground, and the panel letter states an evidentiary conclusion. Bucy did not say “we have no reasons.” None of this, however, supplies the requested bridge between “charges happened in 2019” and the grievance's later pleaded acts, or explains whether the panel's conclusion replaces or supplements ODC's threshold ruling. Nor does a referral to review identify which passage disposes of the alleged speech-related condition. Whether the minimum legal obligation was satisfied remains unresolved; the narrower editorial finding is that **ODC declined further clarification at the point the complainant asked for that bridge.** [Email exchange](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MISJustice-Alliance/ywcamissoula.com/main/gitbook-export/.gitbook/assets/odc-26-145-november-5-2026-bucy-response-email-address-redacted.pdf); [court-adopted rules, Rules 5, 10, 11, 14 and 20](https://juddocumentservice.mt.gov/getDocByCTrackId?DocId=368130).

## A proportionate accountability standard

The existing rules require concise facts and reasons at ODC dismissal and notice of review rights; they do not mandate the following editorial proposals. A closure notice could identify the dispositive conduct date and why pleaded later acts do not matter; a panel affirmance could say whether it adopts or replaces an earlier ground; and a reply referring a complainant back to a prior notice could identify the controlling passage without opening the investigative file. These measures would not guarantee a different result or unlimited correspondence. The question is not whether a complainant deserves endless correspondence or a favorable decision. It is whether the disclosed explanation lets that person identify and contest the actual basis of dismissal. That is an editorial accountability standard, not a judicial holding.
