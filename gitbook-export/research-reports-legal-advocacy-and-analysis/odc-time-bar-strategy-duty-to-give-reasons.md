# When 'Time-Barred' and 'Strategy' End the Inquiry: The Duty to Give Reasons

**Analysis / opinion**

> **About this article:** A procedural analysis of what two Montana lawyer-discipline files show about stated reasons, later submissions and review. It evaluates the intelligibility of decisions, not whether either grievance should have succeeded; it does not treat other agencies' practices as proof about ODC.

## Executive Summary

An agency can close a grievance without deciding its merits. Montana ODC's June 9, 2026 letter did just that in the Lowney file: it invoked a time bar. In the Tipp file, the later closure letters described particular alleged failures to investigate evidence as “strategy” or as already addressed. Those explanations can be legally consequential and still tell the complainant too little about how the specific alleged conduct was classified. That is the accountability issue: a review pathway means less in practice if a reader cannot trace the decision from the allegation to the stated ground. This is an editorial assessment of the available letters, not a finding of bad faith or a legal determination that the notices violated a rule. [Tipp and Lowney decision report](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/tipp-lowney-disciplinary-decisions-october-2026); [public evidence ledger](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-review-evidence-ledger-2026).

## Reasons, not an entitlement to the file

The Montana Supreme Court's [2021 Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/0dfa798d-443a-4159-b66e-3a404e41e7a3/downloads/RLDE%202021%20(with%20Order).pdf?ver=1791651978503), effective January 1, 2021, give ODC discretion over investigative steps: Rule 10C(1)(b) allows dismissal without some or all of the enumerated investigation. Yet Rule 5B(10) directs Disciplinary Counsel on dismissal to “provide a concise written statement of the facts and reasons a matter has been dismissed.” Rule 10C(2), “Notice of Disposition,” requires written notice “stating the reasons for the action taken, and advising the grievant of the right to request review of the dismissal.” In the reviewed notices and follow-up letters, ODC did not point Nuno to *these explanation provisions* or explain how their language applied to the questioned factual grounds. That matters editorially; it does not create a freestanding legal requirement to cite a rule number. ODC did cite Rule 11 in Lowney's time-bar notice and gave review directions in both original dismissals. An assertion that ODC “never disclosed its own governing rules” would therefore overstate this record. Rule 10C(3) permits written Review Panel review; Rule 14A describes further discretionary Court review after panel affirmance. Rule 20A makes pre-formal-Complaint disciplinary information confidential, subject to exceptions. The rules require a private explanation to the grievant, not public disclosure of the investigative file or a response to every subsequent question.

A concise answer might identify the controlling threshold and the allegation to which it applies. If timing ends the inquiry, say which alleged act was too old and why the pleaded later acts do not alter that conclusion. If an allegation was already evaluated as strategy, say whether a later document is regarded as merely another instance of the same category. Those are proposed practices, not quoted additional duties in Rule 10.

## Two files show the difference between an answer and an answer one can trace

ODC's November 3, 2025 decision in file 25-147 concerning Bryan C. Tipp was not a one-word dismissal. It discussed professional-conduct rules, the scope of representation, the criminal case's dismissal and counsel's strategic discretion; it advised of review. That makes the strongest objection to a “no explanation” claim decisive. But the later question is whether that earlier discussion answered the *later*, document-specific allegations. In July 2026, after Nuno raised a witness email and a medical letter, ODC said the material was already addressed and then called it additional examples of “strategy.” Strategy is a legal and factual category, not a magic word that resolves whether counsel saw a document, assessed its significance, or made an informed choice not to use it. The letters do not say which of those happened here. A categorical determination could be sufficient if ODC reasonably regarded both exhibits as immaterial to its earlier analysis; the available letters do not expose that reasoning or identify the explanation from Tipp they invoke. What is missing from the written response is a traceable account of which material the conclusion covers and how the stated strategy rationale addresses the alleged omissions. [Original November decision reproduced on site](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-response-to-bryan-tipp-bar-complaint-answer-octo/mt-bar-complaint-odc-no.-25-147-odc-ruling-november-2025); [July 10 letter](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-25-147-july-2026-closure-response-analysis); [July 30 letter](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-file-no-25-147-july-30-2026-closure-letter-technicality-defense-analysis).

The second file, 26-145 concerning Brian C. Lowney, is not a duplicate of the first. ODC's June 9, 2026 notice invoked a limitations rule: “your charges happened in 2019,” beyond the six-year period, without pleaded tolling or exception. It thus declined to reach the prosecutor-misconduct allegations on their merits. A time bar is a potentially legitimate threshold decision, not inherently a trick or an admission that the allegations were true. But the grievance describes conduct through January 2021, and the notice does not identify which alleged acts it placed in 2019 or why the later pleaded period makes no difference. Even its exception citation needs checking: the notice names Rule 11D, while the operative 2021 text places the exceptions at Rule 11E. The Commission's October 23 affirmance then said the facts did not demonstrate clear-and-convincing evidence of ethical violations. That broad evidentiary conclusion supplies no allegation-specific account and does not say whether the panel adopted the time bar, added an independent ground or considered only some conduct. The reason may be legally sufficient; the letters alone do not permit a reader to see how the two grounds relate. Nuno's November 4, 1:33 p.m. email asks ODC to identify the passage dealing with his allegation of a speech-related condition; the provided packet ends at that sent email, and a reply may exist later. [Lowney grievance](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/odc-lowney-grievance-2026/grievance-to-montana-odc-brian-lowney-4.28.2026); [ODC notice](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/odc-lowney-grievance-2026/odc-26-145-rejection-brian-lowney-june-9-2026); [Commission letter](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/odc-review-evidence-ledger-2026); [2021 rules](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/0dfa798d-443a-4159-b66e-3a404e41e7a3/downloads/RLDE%202021%20(with%20Order).pdf?ver=1791651978503). The grievance's characterization of Lowney's conduct remains an allegation.

This is why the distinction between **a reason exists** and **its application is intelligible** matters. Asking the first question alone yields an easy but incomplete answer; calling every unsatisfying answer silence erases the reasons that really were given. The better question is which disputed issue the existing ground disposes of, and which issue remains outside its stated scope.

## Review depends on knowing what to contest

A meaningful request for Review Panel consideration may challenge a timing premise, a factual description, or a professional-duty judgment. Those are different responses to different grounds. Rule 10C(3) provides review on the record before the panel, not an automatic fresh investigation; Rule 14B makes Supreme Court review discretionary. A brief but clear decision helps the grievant identify the premise in dispute and helps reviewers distinguish what the agency actually decided from what the complainant wished it had decided. That is a practical argument for clarity, not proof that an ambiguous letter legally denied review. The Tipp matter reached the Court, which affirmed the Commission's disposition on its account of an untimely request. The Lowney matter reached the Commission; no later Court ruling was verified in the supplied materials. [Rules 10C(3), 14](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/0dfa798d-443a-4159-b66e-3a404e41e7a3/downloads/RLDE%202021%20(with%20Order).pdf?ver=1791651978503); [October outcomes](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/montana-bar-complaints/tipp-lowney-disciplinary-decisions-october-2026).

## Comparison without collapsing institutions

The site's [Structural Failure of Police Accountability in Montana](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/research-reports-legal-advocacy-and-analysis/montana-state-institutional-failures/structural_failure_of_police_accountability_in_montana_from_mpd_to_post) discusses police internal complaint handling and state POST certification triage. Its shared question with this article is whether a threshold explanation tells the complainant which allegations fell outside a body's remit. Its institutions, powers, records and duties are not ODC's. A POST time-screening statement cannot establish how ODC handled a lawyer grievance, and police complaint rates do not measure ODC performance.

The site's [Institutional Willful Blindness analysis](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/research-reports-legal-advocacy-and-analysis/montana-state-institutional-failures/institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis) proposes a cross-channel pattern of narrow responses. Its shared mechanism is category-level closure. But parts of its ODC account say the Lowney grievance was dismissed on a scope-of-representation ground; the [June 9 primary notice](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/odc-lowney-grievance-2026/odc-26-145-rejection-brian-lowney-june-9-2026) says limitations. That discrepancy is material. Its stronger claims about deliberate suppression and the absence of substantive ODC review cannot be carried into this analysis as findings; the Tipp decision actually contains substantive discussion. The older piece is a record of an advocacy argument, not corroboration of intent or a demonstrated institution-wide pattern.

The [anonymized transparency report](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/research-reports-legal-advocacy-and-analysis/montana-state-institutional-failures/transparency-and-public-understanding-attorney-discipline) shares this article's question about what a closure notice lets a person understand. Unlike this analysis, it avoids named case findings. These pieces can sit together only if their different scope and evidentiary status stay visible. Two linked ODC grievances by one complainant, even alongside a separate police-oversight account, do not establish a prevalence rate or coordinated practice across agencies.

## The strongest objection

ODC may say—reasonably—that its rule is a *concise* reasons requirement, not a demand for a miniature judicial opinion; that confidentiality protects lawyers, witnesses and complainants; that it can dismiss without all investigative steps; and that a dissatisfied person may seek the review the rules provide. The Tipp November determination is substantial evidence for the first point. The Lowney June notice gives a genuine time-based ground, and the later panel gives an evidentiary one. Nothing reviewed establishes a legal duty to answer Nuno's questions about national ABA standards or to disclose the file. Rule 5C also says Disciplinary Counsel cannot render advisory ethics opinions. The editorial position survives these objections only in narrower form: even a concise reason should make the decision's operative premise understandable where the complaint itself presents an apparent date or category mismatch. Whether a particular notice meets the legal minimum is a question requiring fuller records and legal assessment, not a conclusion made here. [Court-adopted rules, Rules 5C, 10 and 20](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/0dfa798d-443a-4159-b66e-3a404e41e7a3/downloads/RLDE%202021%20(with%20Order).pdf?ver=1791651978503).

## A proportionate accountability standard

The existing rules require concise facts and reasons at dismissal and notice of review rights; they do not mandate the following editorial proposals. A closure notice could name the dispositive issue and the factual premise used to resolve it; a response to materially new documents could say whether they change the prior rationale, without releasing confidential contents; a panel affirmance could clarify whether it adopts or replaces an earlier ground; and a review notice could reproduce the operative rule's recipient and trigger accurately. These measures would not guarantee a different result. They would make the result easier to evaluate, correct and, where available, challenge. That is a defensible standard of accountability precisely because it asks for traceable reasons rather than a predetermined verdict.
