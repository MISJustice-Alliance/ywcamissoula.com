# A Plea for Justice: The Injustice of the Plea Deal System

**How detention, financial exhaustion, and the price of trial can make a guilty plea a rational choice for an innocent person**

> **About this article:** This research and commentary examines how detention, trial-penalty exposure, defense costs, and collateral consequences can shape a defendant’s decision to plead guilty. It compares official statistics, exoneration records, empirical studies, and legal authorities without treating plea rates as measures of innocence. Elvis Nuno’s accounts are attributed, unadjudicated examples, not independently established findings in this article.

## Executive Summary

The constitutional right to trial promises an opportunity to make the government prove its accusation. Plea bargaining can make exercising that right extraordinarily expensive. A defendant may face continued detention, disappearing income, family disruption, and a substantially longer sentence if convicted after rejecting an offer. Under those conditions, admitting guilt can become a strategy for survival—even when the admission is false.

This article reviews official court statistics, documented exonerations, controlled experiments, detention research, and Supreme Court precedent. It distinguishes lawful bargaining pressure from legally invalid coercion, and evidence that innocent people plead guilty from estimates of how often they do so. It also discusses Elvis Nuno’s attributed accounts of bail pressure and an alleged threat involving a third party; the underlying conversational source is not publicly available here, and the case-specific claims have not been independently corroborated for this article.

Negotiated resolutions can reduce punishment and spare victims and witnesses a trial. Those benefits do not establish factual accuracy. A meaningful choice requires more than a signed agreement: it requires adequate defense, timely evidence, proportionate sentencing, protection against improper threats, and a practical ability to refuse.

## Part I: How Plea Bargaining Changes the Right to Trial

### When winning still means losing

What does a right to trial mean when accepting a conviction offers immediate relief, while contesting the accusation threatens everything that makes freedom useful?

That question does not require an invented defendant. In Harris County, Texas, a review of drug convictions exposed guilty pleas entered before laboratory results were available. Subsequent testing found no controlled substances. The National Registry of Exonerations documented 73 such drug-crime exonerations arising from the county’s review program during 2014–2015. In 41, field tests had indicated drugs that later laboratory testing did not confirm.[[1]](#note-1)

The Registry reported two possible explanations from the prosecutor overseeing the review: some defendants apparently believed the substances were illegal; others accepted favorable offers rather than remain detained and risk substantial imprisonment. These explanations need not fit every case. What the laboratory results demonstrate is narrower and devastating: a guilty plea had not reliably established the charged drug offense.[[1]](#note-1)

The national evidence nevertheless makes the central concern unavoidable. A person can make an informed, deliberate decision to accept a plea while denying the accusation’s truth. The question is whether the system gives that person a tolerable alternative.

### A system organized around surrendering trial

Plea bargaining did not arrive through one comprehensive legislative design. Historical scholarship traces its growth through changing prosecutorial practices and the institutional demands of processing criminal cases. Supreme Court decisions later supplied a constitutional framework for negotiated convictions. By 2012, *Missouri v. Frye* treated effective representation during bargaining as indispensable to a system in which pleas dominate.[[3]](#note-3)

The arrangement offers obvious administrative advantages. Prosecutors exchange charging or sentencing concessions for resolution; defendants obtain reduced exposure or greater certainty; courts avoid the time required for contested trials. But when institutions budget around that arrangement, trial becomes an exceptional demand on a system designed for agreement.

The familiar assertion that “95–98% of cases end in plea deals” conceals several different questions. For the fiscal year ending September 30, 2025, the federal judiciary’s Table D-7 reports the following:[[4]](#note-4)

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guilty pleas among convicted-and-sentenced defendants | 75,151 ÷ 76,514 | 98.2% |
| Guilty pleas among all defendants disposed of in the table | 75,151 ÷ 82,042 | 91.6% |
| Convictions by any route among defendants disposed of | 76,514 ÷ 82,042 | 93.3% |

These are federal disposition-year figures, not a cohort following everyone charged that year. The table covers cases filed as felonies or Class A misdemeanors, plus petty offenses assigned to district judges. It does not capture every federal magistrate proceeding, much less every state prosecution. Nor does its guilty-plea category independently identify negotiated agreements: a defendant can plead without a bargain.

State evidence must retain its own date and population. A Bureau of Justice Statistics study of felony filings in May 2009, sampled to represent the 75 largest counties, reported convictions in 66% of cases adjudicated within its one-year window; the published table’s rounded plea components total approximately 64% of adjudicated cases. That produces an approximate 97% plea share among convictions—not among everyone charged. Murder cases received longer follow-up, and the report’s table notes must be retained when using its figures.[[5]](#note-5)

The often-repeated 95% state figure is also historical: the ABA’s 2023 policy report cites *Frye*, a 2012 decision, for it. It should not be repackaged as a fresh national census of state felony and misdemeanor dispositions.[[6]](#note-6)

None of these numbers measures factual accuracy. A plea rate tells us how convictions were obtained. It cannot tell us how often police identified the right person, whether the original charge was correct, or how many defendants admitted guilt to escape the process.

### The price attached to a trial

The “trial penalty” describes the sentencing disadvantage associated with rejecting a plea and proceeding to trial. Three propositions must remain separate: a concession offered for pleading, increased exposure after negotiations fail, and unconstitutional retaliation for exercising a right. Their practical effects can overlap even when courts classify them differently.

In *Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, a prosecutor offered to recommend five years and warned that rejection would trigger a habitual-offender charge carrying mandatory life imprisonment. Hayes refused, was tried, and received life. The Supreme Court upheld the bargaining conduct because the additional charge was supported and Hayes was legally subject to it. The decision permits formidable leverage; it does not authorize invented charges or every threat involving another person.[[7]](#note-7)

The legal baseline matters. Is the longer sentence independently proportionate, with the plea rewarding cooperation? Or was the higher exposure selected principally to make refusal intolerable? Calling the difference a discount does not answer that question.

Charging choices can control access to mandatory minimums, repeat-offender enhancements, and multiple counts carrying additional punishment. Where consecutive sentencing is authorized, charge stacking can magnify exposure. A defendant may therefore depend on prosecutorial concessions for relief a sentencing judge cannot otherwise provide. An expiring offer can add urgency before investigation is complete.[[6]](#note-6)

Nuno’s supplied account describes prosecutors conditioning maximum-sentence requests on rejection of offers. That is his reported experience, not evidence that every prosecutor follows that practice. A warning that the maximum is legally possible differs from announcing an intention to request it if bargaining fails. A prosecutorial request also differs from the sentence a judge ultimately imposes.[[2]](#note-2)

Measuring the penalty nationally is difficult. Trial defendants may face different evidence, charges, criminal histories, cooperation opportunities, or aggravating facts. Comparing average sentences does not isolate punishment attributable to exercising trial rights. Recording successive offers, charging changes, and sentencing recommendations would permit a more meaningful comparison—although even within-case comparisons must account for newly discovered facts.[[6]](#note-6)

## Part II: Innocence, Detention, and the Cost of Defense

### Why innocence does not remove the incentive

Knowing that an accusation is false does not mean knowing a jury will reject it. An innocent defendant may distrust a witness, fear that records will be misunderstood, or doubt that counsel can present the defense effectively. A small perceived chance of catastrophe can outweigh a substantial chance of acquittal when the threatened loss is unbearable.

This is an explanation of decision-making under asymmetric risk, not a calculation of any defendant’s actual conviction probability. The person may be protecting children, preserving employment, or avoiding further confinement. Rational loss avoidance and accurate adjudication are different objectives.

Controlled research tests that distinction. In Lucian Dervan and Vanessa Edkins’s 2013 study, students faced a staged academic-misconduct accusation and an admission-based alternative to a disciplinary proceeding. Researchers knew whether the alleged misconduct occurred. Of 39 innocent participants retained in the analysis, 22—56.4%—accepted the offer; 33 of 37 guilty participants—89.2%—did so.[[8]](#note-8)

Those are results from a small university experiment, not criminal-court error rates. Its importance is methodological: innocence was independently known, yet it did not prevent false admissions. The study did not establish that every increase in threatened punishment increases plea acceptance; its sentencing manipulation did not produce a significant effect.[[8]](#note-8)

Edkins and Dervan’s 2018 experiments separately varied guilt, detention scenarios, and information about collateral consequences. Detention increased willingness to plead, including among participants assigned innocence. Warnings about future consequences generally did not overcome the attraction of immediate liberty. Present bias and temporal discounting offer explanations: immediate relief receives greater weight than damage arriving later. Imagined detention, however, is not actual incarceration.[[9]](#note-9)

Advice matters too. Kelsey Henderson and Lora Levett’s 2018 academic-misconduct experiment found that advocate recommendations changed plea decisions, especially among innocent participants. This demonstrates susceptibility to advice under experimental conditions, not the prevalence of ineffective criminal lawyers.[[10]](#note-10)

Stress, disrupted sleep, isolation, fear, and cognitive overload are additional plausible pathways through which confinement can impair deliberation. They should not be presented as separately measured causes in studies that manipulate only detention status. Nor should distrust or apparent submission be mistaken for proof of guilt. Interviews can reveal how people describe pressure; experiments isolate selected mechanisms; administrative records show outcomes. None substitutes for all the others.

### Detention changes the bargain before guilt is decided

Pretrial custody imposes costs that an eventual acquittal cannot automatically repair. Employment can disappear, rent go unpaid, childcare arrangements collapse, and treatment be interrupted. Confinement can also obstruct confidential consultation and practical assistance with records, witnesses, and investigation. These are possible mechanisms and documented concerns, not outcomes experienced identically by every detained person.[[11]](#note-11)

The strongest evidence goes beyond comparing detained defendants with released defendants. Those groups differ in ways that could themselves explain case outcomes.

Will Dobbie, Jacob Goldin, and Crystal Yang used the differing release tendencies of quasi-randomly assigned bail judges in Philadelphia and Miami-Dade County. Their 2018 study linked court and tax records for more than 420,000 defendants. It estimated that initial pretrial release reduced guilty pleas by 10.8 percentage points.[[12]](#note-12)

The design supports a causal interpretation under its identifying assumptions, especially that judge assignment affects later outcomes through release rather than some separate channel. The estimate concerns defendants whose release status changed with the assigned judge’s tendencies; it is not necessarily the effect for every defendant. The study also found improved formal employment with release, but increased failure to appear and pre-disposition rearrest, with no detectable net effect on rearrest over two years. Release policy involves real tradeoffs.[[12]](#note-12)

The records did not identify factual innocence. Detention’s effect on pleas cannot simply be relabeled its effect on false pleas.

For a detained person offered time served, however, the mechanism is clear: continuing the defense carries a present cost even if the eventual outcome is dismissal. The offer competes with the cost of waiting to win, not just the risk of losing. Experimental and field evidence converge on that concern without establishing a nationwide wrongful-plea percentage.

### The financial cost of maintaining innocence

Release reduces one source of pressure; it does not make defense free. Retained counsel, investigators, expert analysis, and document review require money. Repeated appearances require transportation, childcare, and time away from work. Bail may immobilize savings or impose nonrefundable commercial-bond costs where that system operates. A household can exhaust its resources before the merits are resolved.[[11]](#note-11)

The burden differs by representation and custody status. A detained defendant may lose income while needing help arranging a defense. A released defendant may keep working but face recurring absences and mounting expenses. A person with appointed counsel avoids some direct legal bills yet still needs an attorney with sufficient time and investigative support. Someone proceeding without effective representation must assess unfamiliar legal risks with fewer safeguards.

The 2023 *National Public Defense Workload Study* reviewed 17 state-level studies and used a panel of 33 experts to estimate the time competent representation requires. It rejected undifferentiated, decades-old caseload limits as inadequate. These are research-informed workload standards, not a finding that every overburdened lawyer performs ineffectively in every case.[[13]](#note-13)

The relevant inequality is not simply that government can outspend defendants. Prosecutors also operate under budgets, and some defendants possess considerable wealth. It is that the government can continue a prosecution while the accused must finance daily survival and the practical work of contesting it. That asymmetry can change what a person is willing to concede without changing the underlying facts.

## Part III: Voluntariness and the Limits of Plea Safeguards

### What the Constitution calls voluntary

Constitutional validity requires a knowing, intelligent, voluntary plea. Yet *Brady v. United States* holds that choosing a plea to avoid a greater possible punishment does not, by itself, make the choice involuntary. The law distinguishes substantial pressure from coercion sufficient to invalidate consent.[[14]](#note-14)

That distinction is consequential, not semantic. A defendant can understand the agreement, rationally prefer it, and still confront circumstances that raise grave questions about justice and factual reliability.

Counsel is constitutionally important during bargaining. *Frye* addresses failures to communicate formal favorable offers; *Lafler v. Cooper* addresses deficient advice causing rejection of an offer. *Hill v. Lockhart* governs the familiar claim that deficient advice caused acceptance of a plea: relief requires prejudice as well as deficient performance, ordinarily a reasonable probability that competent advice would have led the defendant to insist on trial. These standards do not make every disappointing bargain unconstitutional.[[15]](#note-15)

Disclosure creates another limit. *United States v. Ruiz* held that the Constitution does not require material impeachment information before a plea agreement. Its agreement preserved disclosure of known factual-innocence information; the opinion should not be converted into blanket permission to hide all exculpatory evidence.[[16]](#note-16)

Lower courts differ on the broader question. The Fifth Circuit’s en banc *Alvarez v. City of Brownsville* rejected a constitutional right to exculpatory material before a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit’s majority opinion in *Parker v. County of Riverside* recognized existing circuit precedent allowing plea-related *Brady* claims. A concurrence urged reconsideration, but did not change the majority’s rule. State discovery rules and ethical duties may provide additional protections. Jurisdiction therefore matters.[[17]](#note-17)

Federal Rule 11 requires personal judicial inquiry into understanding and voluntariness, and a factual basis before judgment on a guilty plea. It also bars judicial participation in plea discussions. These federal procedures are not automatically the governing rules in a Montana or Washington state proceeding.[[18]](#note-18)

An *Alford* plea permits conviction despite a protestation of innocence under the circumstances the Supreme Court approved, including strong evidence of guilt and an intelligent decision to plead. A no-contest plea declines to contest the charge rather than expressly admitting it; availability and consequences vary. Neither is an acquittal, and neither should be conflated with an ordinary factual admission.[[19]](#note-19)

### When the threat also requires silence

The supplied account raises a sharper problem than ordinary sentencing pressure. Nuno describes an alleged threat affecting a third party and a child and says he believed disclosing it could expose them to serious harm. He presents concealment as an attempt to protect third parties.[[2]](#note-2)

That account is not independently established by the conversation. But the procedural problem it identifies is real as a matter of logic: asking whether someone was threatened cannot reliably exclude a threat that makes disclosure seem dangerous.

The Supreme Court has not treated courtroom answers as infallible. In *Blackledge v. Allison*, it emphasized their substantial evidentiary weight while rejecting an absolute barrier to sufficiently specific allegations outside the plea record. *Machibroda v. United States* required an opportunity to substantiate detailed allegations of off-the-record promises and threats. Neither decision found that every later coercion allegation must be believed or that the alleged coercion in those proceedings had already been proved.[[20]](#note-20)

Even *Bordenkircher* expressly reserved bargaining involving adverse or lenient treatment of another person, recognizing the additional risk of false pleas. Its permissive rule about supported charges against the accused does not settle the alleged custody threat described here.[[7]](#note-7)

A meaningful inquiry must therefore examine who communicated what, to whom, when, under what asserted authority, and with what connection to the plea. It must also consider whether counsel knew and whether disclosure could realistically be made safely. A scripted denial supplies evidence; it does not investigate those circumstances.

## Part IV: The Consequences and Tradeoffs of Negotiated Convictions

### A conviction can outlast the bargain

“No additional jail” does not mean “no serious consequences.” Depending on the offense, jurisdiction, and person, a conviction can affect employment, professional licensing, housing, immigration status, public benefits, voting or other civil rights, and family proceedings. The National Inventory of Collateral Consequences catalogs legal restrictions; it does not imply that all apply to every conviction.[[21]](#note-21)

Immigration illustrates why advice matters. Under *Padilla v. Kentucky*, counsel must advise a noncitizen about deportation risk, with correct advice required when the consequence is clear. A short criminal sentence may accompany a consequence the defendant considers much worse.[[22]](#note-22)

Reputation, family strain, and psychological distress are additional human costs, although their extent cannot be quantified from the supplied narrative. A conviction can also affect future criminal-history calculations or enhancements under applicable law. Today's concession may alter tomorrow’s exposure.

Reopening the case is difficult. *Tollett v. Henderson* generally limits independent attacks on earlier constitutional defects after a counseled, valid plea. Exceptions matter: *Class v. United States* preserved a particular challenge to the constitutionality of the statute of conviction. Appeal waivers can further restrict review, but their reach depends on the agreement and governing law.[[23]](#note-23)

Federal plea withdrawal becomes harder after acceptance and is unavailable by ordinary withdrawal motion after sentencing. Collateral proceedings bring additional procedural barriers. *Bousley v. United States* recognizes an actual-innocence gateway for a procedurally defaulted plea challenge, but demands factual innocence, not merely insufficient legal proof; it is not automatic relief.[[24]](#note-24)

Factual innocence, legal insufficiency, dismissal, procedural reversal, and exoneration are consequently different categories. Exonerations establish discovered errors under specified criteria. Their selected character prevents using their plea share as a national false-plea rate.

### Efficiency is a justification, not an accuracy test

The strongest defense of bargaining deserves a serious answer. Negotiation can spare victims and witnesses repeated testimony, provide certainty, reward useful cooperation, and reduce defendants’ exposure. Eliminating every concession without reducing charging volume or punishment could leave defendants worse off. Court capacity cannot expand instantly.[[6]](#note-6)

An attorney recommending acceptance may be doing conscientious work under dangerous conditions. An innocent client may reasonably decide that the risk of losing a home, caregiving role, or years of freedom is unacceptable. Recognizing that rationality should increase concern about the system’s design, not invite contempt for the defendant.

The difficulty is that these benefits answer a different question from whether a conviction is true. Efficiency counts completed cases. Accuracy asks whether the person committed the offense. A process that induces both guilty and innocent people to accept convictions cannot certify its reliability merely by recording their agreement.

Trials also create public records in which witnesses, police conduct, and disputed evidence can be tested. Bargaining may preserve resources for those functions, but too little meaningful litigation can weaken them. The appropriate response is not to romanticize every trial; it is to preserve a realistic capacity to demand one.

## Part V: Making Refusal a Real Option

Reform should address the circumstances producing consent, rather than merely obtain more emphatic declarations of voluntariness.

Reducing unnecessary detention is one starting point, coupled with individualized safety assessments, court reminders, transportation help, and minimally burdensome conditions. These supports should be evaluated rather than presumed effective. Replacing money bail with intrusive supervision can reproduce pressure in another form.

Early counsel has concrete evidence behind it. A 2023 field experiment in Allegheny County found that providing defenders at initial bail hearings reduced monetary bail and short-term detention without increasing failure to appear at the preliminary hearing. It also found an increase in theft rearrests. That tradeoff, and the study’s limited setting, belong in any honest account of the reform.[[25]](#note-25)

Defense funding should follow realistic workload assessments, with investigation, experts, interpreters, and private consultation available before advice becomes irreversible. Earlier discovery and adequate review time would reduce decisions made in avoidable ignorance. Critical forensic testing should ordinarily precede conviction; exceptions require scrutiny rather than treating immediate-release offers as inherently harmless.

Written offers, recorded deadlines, and preserved charging histories would make leverage visible. Legislatures and prosecutors should constrain escalation undertaken merely to obtain a plea, and require independent justification for sentencing recommendations. Limits on rushed offers must allow necessary investigation while accommodating genuine witness-safety concerns. These measures require resources and enforceable remedies, not just aspirational language.

The ABA adopted fourteen reform principles in August 2023 addressing these concerns, including substantial trial penalties, detention-induced pleas, discovery, counsel, and oversight. They are professional policy recommendations, not automatically enforceable law or demonstrated nationwide reforms.[[6]](#note-6)

Judicial resistance is also emerging. In *United States v. Tavberidze*, Judge Jed Rakoff challenged the additional Guidelines reduction tied to timely pleading under § 3E1.1(b). That district-court position is not a nationwide rule. A separate 2026 Second Circuit summary order, *United States v. Patterson*, rejected an unpreserved trial-penalty challenge under plain-error review; it was nonprecedential and was not the disposition of Tavberidze’s own appeal.[[26]](#note-26)

Finally, courts need practical channels for investigating alleged threats, particularly those involving third parties. Confidential consultation, time to investigate, and carefully authorized protective procedures can improve inquiry; secrecy and safety cannot simply be promised. Post-conviction testing and credible innocence review should remain available after pleas. Data systems should measure offers, detention, representation, charging changes, and later corrections—not just disposal speed.

When exercising trial rights threatens livelihood, family stability, financial survival, and freedom, the right remains formally available while its practical value can collapse. A guilty plea may then express what a person can endure rather than what that person did.

Making the choice meaningful requires the ability to refuse without manufactured catastrophe: competent defense, accessible evidence, proportionate punishment, protection from improper threats, and a workable path to correct error. The government should have to earn a conviction through a reliable process. A defendant’s inability to survive that process should never be mistaken for proof.

This is the plea-voluntariness decision, distinct from *Brady v. Maryland*’s disclosure doctrine. Later treatment concerning breached promises does not displace the limited sentencing-pressure proposition used here.

### Note 15

[Missouri v. Frye, 566 U.S. 134 (2012)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/missouri-v-frye-625832?utm_source=mcp); [Lafler v. Cooper, 566 U.S. 156 (2012)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/lafler-v-cooper-625833?utm_source=mcp&lines=63-65) (both decided Mar. 21, 2012); [Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52, 58–59 (1985)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/hill-v-lockhart-111539?utm_source=mcp&lines=69-72) (decided Nov. 18, 1985). Rejected/lapsed-offer claims require the distinct prejudice showings specified in *Frye* and *Lafler*; *Hill* is not the sole test for every bargaining injury.

### Note 16

[United States v. Ruiz, 536 U.S. 622, 633 (2002)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-ruiz-121166?utm_source=mcp&lines=115-115); [United States v. Ruiz, 536 U.S. 622, 625 (2002)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-ruiz-121166?utm_source=mcp&lines=12-13) (decided June 24, 2002). The Court also addressed affirmative-defense information in the particular fast-track agreement; the article does not claim the decision concerned impeachment alone in every respect.

### Note 17

[Alvarez v. City of Brownsville, 904 F.3d 382 (5th Cir. 2018)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/george-alvarez-v-city-of-4313442?utm_source=mcp&lines=173-180) (en banc, decided Sept. 18, 2018); [Parker v. County of Riverside, 78 F.4th 1109 (9th Cir. 2023)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/roger-parker-v-county-of-9555903?utm_source=mcp&lines=82-92) (decided Aug. 15, 2023). *Parker* rejected the plaintiff’s particular prejudice theory; its discussion of existing plea precedents is not a finding that his own plea was invalid. The concurrence questioning that precedent is separate from the majority.

### Note 18

Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(a)–(c), [current official rules compilation](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf) (rules as amended Dec. 1, 2025; GPO print 2026). Rule 11(b)(1)(N) addresses appeal/collateral-attack waivers; Rule 11(b)(2) voluntariness; Rule 11(b)(3) factual basis; Rule 11(c)(1) judicial nonparticipation. Recommendation-only agreements differ from agreements binding the court upon acceptance under Rule 11(c)(1)(C).

### Note 19

[North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 37–38 (1970)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/north-carolina-v-alford-108215?utm_source=mcp&lines=100-109) (decided Nov. 23, 1970); Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(a)(3), [rule text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcrmp/rule_11). *Alford* does not create an absolute constitutional right to have such a plea accepted.

### Note 20

[Blackledge v. Allison, 431 U.S. 63, 73–75 (1977)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/blackledge-v-allison-109648?utm_source=mcp&lines=82-85) (decided May 2, 1977); [Machibroda v. United States, 368 U.S. 487, 494–95 (1962)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/machibroda-v-united-states-106335?utm_source=mcp&lines=36-47) (decided Feb. 19, 1962). The first affirmed further processing of the claim, not automatic entitlement to a full evidentiary hearing in every case; the second required a hearing on the specific disputed allegations before it.

### Note 21

Council of State Governments Justice Center, *National Inventory of Collateral Consequences of Conviction*, [inventory](https://niccc.nationalreentryresourcecenter.org/) (undated, accessed Oct. 30, 2026). Search jurisdiction and offense-specific provisions before asserting a particular disability. Legal restrictions differ from discretionary decisions and informal stigma.

### Note 22

[Padilla v. Kentucky, 559 U.S. 356 (2010)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/padilla-v-kentucky-1723?utm_source=mcp&lines=117-120) (decided Mar. 31, 2010). The Court left prejudice for further proceedings; this citation is not a determination that any particular person qualifies for relief.

### Note 23

[Tollett v. Henderson, 411 U.S. 258, 266–67 (1973)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/tollett-v-henderson-108762?utm_source=mcp&lines=55-61) (decided Apr. 17, 1973); [Class v. United States, 583 U.S. 174 (2018)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/class-v-united-states-4246856?utm_source=mcp&lines=114-116) (decided Feb. 21, 2018). *Class* concerned claims consistent with the plea’s admissions and resolvable on the existing record; it does not preserve every constitutional claim. Conditional pleas and state preservation rules can also matter.

### Note 24

Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(d)–(e), [rule text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcrmp/rule_11); [Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 623–24 (1998)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/bousley-v-united-states-118205?utm_source=mcp&lines=106-116) (decided May 18, 1998). *Bousley* requires the demanding showing that, considering all evidence, it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would convict; it remanded for an attempt to make that showing. Applicable custody requirements, filing deadlines, procedural defaults, and successive-petition restrictions must be analyzed individually.

### Note 25

Shamena Anwar, Shawn Bushway & John Engberg, *The Impact of Defense Counsel at Bail Hearings*, 9 Sci. Advances eade3909 (May 5, 2023), [original study](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.ade3909). The intervention used an experimentally designed defender schedule across hearing shifts. A measured increase in rearrest is not itself proof of guilt or a nationally generalizable effect.

[United States v. Tavberidze, 769 F.Supp.3d 264 (S.D.N.Y. 2025)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-sd-new-yor/117038738.html) (Mar. 10, 2025 pre-sentence opinion; § 3E1.1(a)’s constitutionality left undecided). [United States v. Patterson, No. 24-2320 (2d Cir. Jul 13, 2026)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/united-states-v-patterson--fd06b52d-8f1f-437b-a675-f859589f51f6?utm_source=mcp&lines=36-67) (summary order; nonprecedential; plain-error review). NACDL’s [Feb. 9, 2026 amicus filing page](https://www.nacdl.org/brief/United-States-of-America-v-Tavberidze) identifies Tavberidze’s separate appeal as No. 25-1294. No final disposition of that appeal was verified for this manuscript; neither affirmance nor reversal is asserted.
