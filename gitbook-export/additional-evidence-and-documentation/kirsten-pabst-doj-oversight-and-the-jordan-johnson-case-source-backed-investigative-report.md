# Kirsten Pabst, DOJ Oversight, and the Jordan Johnson Case

## 1. Executive Summary

This report isolates the source-verified record connecting the Missoula County Attorney's Office DOJ investigation, Kirsten H. Pabst's public posture during and after that oversight period, and the Jordan Johnson case. It stays narrower than the broader Missoula accountability pages already in this book and avoids repeating the fuller ecosystem histories already documented in [Remembering When: Missoula rape-capital era](../remembering-when-mpd,-county-prosecutors,-and-the-ywca-allowed-missoula-to-becom.md) and [Montana Police Accountability: Complaint Suppression and Civil Rights Advocacy Brief](../montana-police-accountability-complaint-suppression-and-civil-rights-advocacy-brief.md).

Three points are well supported by the record:

1. The DOJ's February 2014 findings letter concluded there was substantial evidence of gender bias in the Missoula County Attorney's Office's response to sexual assault reports and documented that only 14 of 85 referred adult-woman sexual-assault cases were prosecuted. (Source: U.S. Department of Justice, Findings Letter, Feb. 14, 2014, https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/crt/legacy/2014/02/19/missoula_ltr_2-14-14.pdf)
2. The DOJ's June 10, 2014 MOU/settlement PDF and contemporaneous press release announced a comprehensive agreement with the Missoula County Attorney's Office, Missoula County, and the Montana Attorney General's Office requiring policy, training, victim-services, communication, and data-tracking reforms. (Source: U.S. Department of Justice, MOU/settlement PDF, June 10, 2014, https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/crt/legacy/2014/06/10/missoula_settle_6-10-14.pdf; U.S. Department of Justice, Press Release, June 10, 2014, https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/department-justice-reaches-landmark-agreement-improve-missoula-county-attorney-s-office-s)
3. Contemporaneous coverage and motion paperwork identify Kirsten Pabst as one of Johnson's defense attorneys and show the defense invoking the DOJ investigation in the motion to dismiss. (Source: NBC Montana, "Motion to Dismiss claims no probable cause in Jordan Johnson case"; motion filing noted in contemporaneous coverage)

The record also shows that Pabst, after winning election as county attorney, publicly defended the office's record and rejected Krakauer's criticism as inaccurate or exaggerated. Those statements are documented, but they are not the same thing as an adjudicated finding of misconduct. (Source: Montana Public Radio, Apr. 22, 2015; Montana Kaimin, Nov. 2014; Montana Standard, Apr. 2015)

Two additional threads deserve to be said plainly:

1. The federal record says MCAO refused the DOJ's request for documents, information, and staff interviews during the investigation. Pabst's later public posture, by contrast, emphasized "many factual errors," said the DOJ agreement was "not about prosecution rates," and recast the office as already improving and "starting to make ripples." That is not a neutral description of oversight; it is a minimizing narrative that helps convert a serious civil-rights investigation into a managed public-relations reset.
2. Read with the June 2014 MOU and the broader oversight architecture, that posture fits a bureaucratic black-box pattern: the office controls much of the underlying record, the city/state/federal actors formalize a cooperative compliance story, and the public is left with a process that can discourage complaints, narrow the record, and favor early closure over independent scrutiny. See also [[institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis]] and [[montana-police-accountability-complaint-suppression-and-civil-rights-advocacy-brief]].

## 3. DOJ Baseline: What the Federal Record Actually Said

The DOJ findings letter is the anchor document. It did not merely criticize public messaging; it described a structural problem in how the Missoula County Attorney's Office handled sexual-assault referrals and stated that the office had prosecuted only 14 of 85 referred adult-woman sexual-assault cases in the covered period. (Source: DOJ Findings Letter, Feb. 14, 2014, https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/crt/legacy/2014/02/19/missoula_ltr_2-14-14.pdf)

The June 10, 2014 DOJ MOU/settlement PDF sets the baseline even more precisely. It states that the purpose of the agreement was to ensure the MCAO responded to sexual assault in a nondiscriminatory manner, improved the safety and security of victims, and increased community confidence; it also requires the Attorney General to consult with DOJ on policy review, training, and technical-advisor selection, and to provide DOJ semi-annual reports on policies, training materials, written recommendations, and case data. (Source: DOJ MOU / settlement PDF, June 10, 2014, https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/crt/legacy/2014/06/10/missoula_settle_6-10-14.pdf)

The June 10, 2014 agreement therefore required the County Attorney's Office to:

- develop and implement sexual-assault policies and training,
- improve treatment of sexual-assault reporters,
- improve prosecution techniques and internal supervision,
- hire an in-house victim coordinator,
- improve communication and coordination with other Missoula stakeholders, and
- improve tracking and sharing of sexual-assault prosecution data. (Source: DOJ MOU / settlement PDF, June 10, 2014)

That federal baseline matters because later public disputes were often framed as ordinary disagreement about rhetoric. The federal record shows the issue was larger: the DOJ was addressing an institutional response problem, not merely a public-relations problem. (Source: DOJ Findings Letter, Feb. 14, 2014; DOJ MOU / settlement PDF, June 10, 2014)

## 4. Kirsten Pabst's Role in the Transition Period

Public reporting from the period placed Kirsten Pabst inside the office culture the DOJ later criticized, and later reporting described her as part of the office's leadership continuity. This report uses the source-backed minimum: Pabst was not a detached outside observer when the controversy escalated. (Source: Montana Kaimin, Nov. 2014; Montana Public Radio, Apr. 2015; Montana Standard, Apr. 2015)

The strongest directly quoted public statement available in this pass is the April 22, 2015 Montana Public Radio transcript. In it, Pabst said the office was proud of its work, said the community had been improving its response to sexual assault, and stated that criticism of how cases were charged could be harmful to victims. She also said the DOJ agreement "is not about prosecution rates" and asserted that Missoula's prosecution rate had been "much higher than the national average." (Source: Montana Public Radio, Apr. 22, 2015, https://www.mtpr.org/montana-news/2015-04-22/missoula-county-prosecutor-kirsten-pabsts-statement-on-jon-krakauers-book)

### Why the rhetoric matters

The public statement matters because the DOJ record was not about a mere disagreement over optics. The federal findings letter is explicit: "You declined, and throughout the investigation you have refused to provide requested documents, information, or access to staff for interviews." That is the opposite of a transparent accounting process. Against that backdrop, Pabst's public framing — "many factual errors," "not about prosecution rates," and a story of Missoula having already "changed" and begun to "make ripples" — is the kind of claim a transparent system and transparent operations would dispel, not the absolute refusal to share records with the public and with federal DOJ investigators. It reads as a minimizing narrative that helps launder a civil-rights investigation into a reform success story. (Source: DOJ Findings Letter, Feb. 14, 2014; Montana Public Radio, Apr. 22, 2015; DOJ MOU / settlement PDF, June 10, 2014)

In this project's broader analytical frame, that pattern looks like a bureaucratic black box: the office controls much of the underlying record, the city/county/state oversight structure turns reform into a cooperative compliance story, and the public is left with a process that can deter complaints, narrow the evidentiary record, and favor early closure over independent scrutiny. See also [[institutional-willful-blindness-pattern-analysis]] and [[montana-police-accountability-complaint-suppression-and-civil-rights-advocacy-brief]].

## 5. The Jordan Johnson Case

The Jordan Johnson case matters here for a narrower reason: it is one of the public records that sat inside the DOJ-era Missoula sexual-assault debate, and it shows Pabst moving between institutional roles in the same ecosystem.

The underlying court materials establish the baseline. The charging affidavit and information filed in 2012 charged Johnson with sexual intercourse without consent, and the case proceeded through the ordinary criminal process. NBC Montana's report on the motion to dismiss then identified Kirsten Pabst as one of Johnson's defense attorneys and quoted the filing arguing that prosecutors lacked probable cause and that the DOJ investigation into Missoula's handling of sexual-assault reports explained why the case was brought. Those statements are defense arguments, not court findings. (Source: NBC Montana, "Motion to Dismiss claims no probable cause in Jordan Johnson case"; contemporaneous court filing noted in the report)

Later trial reporting should be used cautiously. A 406 MT Sports / Missoulian verdict report states that Pabst was on the defense team, that the defense continued to reference the DOJ investigation, and that the jury returned a not-guilty verdict. That reporting verifies role and outcome; it does not prove the defense's factual theory about probable cause. (Source: 406 MT Sports / Missoulian, "Verdict reached in Jordan Johnson case," Feb. 7, 2017)

That timeline still creates an appearance problem even if it does not, by itself, prove a formal violation. Pabst had been part of the county attorney ecosystem, then moved onto the defense side in one of the highest-profile sexual-assault prosecutions in the state, and later ran for county attorney. Contemporary reporting is precise on the timing: in 2012 the Missoulian described her as the defense lawyer who "until this spring was chief deputy county attorney," NBC Montana later identified her as a "former Chief Deputy County Attorney" who had served as a deputy prosecutor in the Missoula County Attorney's Office for 15 years, and the Missoulian reported that she was sworn in as county attorney in December 2014. In plain terms, that sequence can read less like a clean break with a troubled institution and more like a career transition through the same contested system. At minimum, that posture sits uneasily beside the public-duty and conflict principles that govern public officers and former government lawyers. (See Mont. Code Ann. § 2-2-105; Mont. Code Ann. § 2-2-131; Montana Rules of Professional Conduct Rule 1.11.)

Because this section relies on trial reporting rather than a complete docket transcript, the safest use of the source remains limited: it confirms Pabst's role on the defense side and the existence of the defense's DOJ-based argument, but not the truth of that argument itself.

## 6. Krakauer, the Public Narrative, and the Office's Response

The public dispute over Jon Krakauer's book is a major part of the accountability record because it shows how the office responded when outside scrutiny turned detailed and specific.

Montana Kaimin reported in November 2014 that Pabst called Krakauer's accusations "inaccurate and exaggerated." (Source: Montana Kaimin, Nov. 2014, https://www.montanakaimin.com/news/county-attorney-krakauers-accusations-inaccurate-exaggerated/article_96d2ec06-e929-11e4-bb7e-e76ed8d15c40.html)

Montana Public Radio then captured Pabst's own April 22, 2015 statement, in which she said the office had "changed," that criticism could be harmful to victims, and that the DOJ agreement was not about prosecution rates. (Source: Montana Public Radio, Apr. 22, 2015)

Montana Standard published Krakauer's reply, reporting that he called Pabst's actions "egregious" and predicted that rape victims would be "served poorly" under her leadership. (Source: Montana Standard, Apr. 2015, https://mtstandard.com/news/local/jon-krakauer-missoula-county-attorney-s-actions-egregious/article_ebcb75f4-9319-5d2a-97b6-89e1cacdf0fb.html)

Taken together, those reports do not prove a specific later prosecution failure on their own. They do, however, establish a documented public conflict between the prosecutor, the author who criticized the office, and the broader reform narrative.

## 7. Official Political-Practices Record: Complaint, Decision, and Settlement Now Transcribed

The Montana Commissioner of Political Practices case Fletcher v. Montanans for Veracity, Diversity and Work is a separate campaign-practices record from the 2014 election cycle. The commissioner’s decision found sufficient evidence of campaign-practices violations tied to alleged coordination and reporting issues involving Montanans for Veracity, Diversity and Work and Pabst’s candidacy, and the matter was later resolved by settlement for a $1,485 cash fine plus in-kind CLE value. That makes the case relevant here not because it proves the DOJ allegations, but because it adds a second official controversy from the same period in which Pabst was moving from office insider to elected prosecutor. (Source: COPP decision PDF; COPP settlement PDF)

Contemporaneous reporting helps pin down the timing: NBC Montana described Pabst in 2014 as the former chief deputy county attorney, said she had served as a deputy prosecutor in the Missoula County Attorney’s Office for 15 years, and reported she was running for the office; the Missoulian later reported she was sworn in as county attorney in December 2014. That chronology matters because it places the Fletcher complaint inside the transition from office insider to elected county attorney. (Source: NBC Montana, "Pabst announces run for county attorney, talks changes"; Missoulian, "Krakauer: Missoula County attorney's actions 'egregious'")
