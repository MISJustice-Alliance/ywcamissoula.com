## Executive Summary

The record assembled here demonstrates a straightforward proposition: the civil-rights remedy exists as a matter of law, but in too many cases it is not meaningfully available as a matter of practice. That is no accident. It is the predictable result of recurring structural barriers, especially in rural states like Montana.

Five recurring mechanisms account for that failure. Operating together, they convert formal constitutional protections into remedies that are often unavailable as a practical matter:

1. **The economics of § 1983 representation** — qualified immunity, damages ceilings, and fee-shifting risk make many meritorious claims economically irrational to file
2. **Rural and small-market legal deserts** — attorney shortages, social overlap, and institutional dependence make independent civil-rights counsel structurally unavailable in many communities
3. **The criminal-to-civil knowledge gap** — criminal defense counsel frequently do not preserve or transmit parallel civil-rights claims before limitations periods expire
4. **Institutional conflict architecture** — nonprofit-law-enforcement overlap, undisclosed relationships, and monopolized expert systems compromise investigations before they are tested adversarially
5. **Inter-jurisdictional intelligence failure** — the absence of cross-jurisdictional pattern recognition allows repeat misconduct to persist unchecked across state lines

Each of these failures is independently documented in peer-reviewed research and federal oversight findings. In combination, they produce a recurring and predictable outcome: a person suffers a constitutional injury, but cannot secure representation, develop the record, or obtain a practical remedy.

## Part I: The § 1983 Representation Market — Why the Economics Break Against Most Victims

### The Statute and Its Promise

Congress enacted 42 U.S.C. § 1983 after the Civil War as the primary mechanism to hold state and local officials accountable for constitutional violations. The companion statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1988, was added in 1976 specifically to make it economically viable for attorneys to bring these cases by allowing fee recovery from the defendant when a plaintiff prevails.[^1][^2]

Together, the statutes were supposed to function as a self-funding enforcement mechanism: attorneys could take cases without charging clients because the defendant would pay fees if the case succeeded. In practice, a series of subsequent doctrinal developments — qualified immunity, tightened pleading standards, and the *Farrar v. Hobby* damages-fee asymmetry — have largely dismantled the economic logic that made the system work.

### The Contingency Fee Math

A typical § 1983 case requires between 500 and 2,000 attorney hours to litigate from complaint through trial — representing between $150,000 and $600,000 in professional time invested at modest billing rates. The attorney receives nothing if the case is lost at any stage. The attorney may receive reduced fees even if the case is won but damages are low. Under *Farrar v. Hobby* (1992), a plaintiff who proves a constitutional violation but recovers only nominal damages may receive zero fee award — meaning the attorney who prevailed on the constitutional merits is still not compensated for years of work.[^2]

This creates a market in which civil-rights attorneys are not simply making moral judgments about whether a violation occurred; they are performing probability-adjusted expected-value calculations. The calculation is unforgiving: the probability of surviving a motion to dismiss, multiplied by the probability of surviving summary judgment, multiplied by the probability of overcoming qualified immunity, multiplied by the probability of winning at trial, multiplied by expected damages, multiplied by the probability of collecting fees afterward. If any single factor is small, the case may become economically irrational to accept regardless of how serious the constitutional violation was.[^2]

### The Qualified Immunity Filter

Qualified immunity — a judicially created doctrine that shields government officials from liability unless they violated a "clearly established" constitutional right — operates as an additional economic filter that further reduces the set of viable § 1983 cases. Under the doctrine as currently applied, plaintiffs must generally identify a prior appellate court or Supreme Court decision with "nearly identical" facts establishing that the specific conduct was unconstitutional.[^3][^4]

In practical terms, this means that an officer can engage in conduct that is clearly unconstitutional by any general principle but still win qualified immunity because the exact fact pattern has never been decided by an appellate court. A 2017 Yale Law Journal study found that qualified immunity drove case outcomes in a significant portion of civil rights appeals and that the doctrine's primary practical effect was to prevent cases from ever reaching merits review — not to protect officers from the consequences of genuinely reasonable mistakes. The Cato Institute's analysis concluded that the doctrine "has no valid legal basis, regularly denies justice to victims whose rights were violated, and severely undermines official accountability."[^4][^3]

Critically, qualified immunity is expensive and time-consuming to litigate even when it ultimately fails. Cities' attorneys — who are paid whether the case is won or lost — routinely invoke qualified immunity even in weak cases to increase litigation costs and push the expected-value calculation below viability for plaintiffs' attorneys working on contingency.[^5][^2]

### What the Research Shows About Representation and Outcomes

A 2026 Florida International University Law Review study — the largest systematic empirical examination of § 1983 case outcomes to date — coded the dockets of 463 civil rights cases initiated in 2021 and 2022 across five federal district courts. Its central finding: attorney identity and prior experience are significantly associated with plaintiff success outcomes in § 1983 litigation, with prior attorney experience showing the strongest correlation with favorable outcomes. This means that not only does representation matter — the *type* of representation matters. Victims represented by inexperienced attorneys or solo practitioners unfamiliar with § 1983 doctrine face systematically worse outcomes than those represented by experienced civil rights firms.[^6]

The Cornell Law School longitudinal study of four decades of federal civil rights litigation found consistently low plaintiff win rates in § 1983 cases, with the gap in success rates between counseled and pro se plaintiffs being especially pronounced. Pro se plaintiffs — those representing themselves because no attorney would take the case — win at dramatically lower rates than those with experienced counsel, yet pro se filings constitute a substantial portion of all § 1983 complaints.[^7][^8]

### Which Cases Attorneys Actually Take

Because the economics of § 1983 representation require high damages and beatable qualified immunity to function, the cases that attract capable civil rights attorneys cluster in a narrow category: death, serious physical injury, clear video evidence, or cases involving patterns and practices of documented misconduct against multiple victims.[^2]

The cases most commonly experienced by civil rights victims — wrongful arrests, constitutional violations in plea proceedings, First Amendment retaliation, unreasonable searches causing economic harm rather than physical injury — fall disproportionately into the gap: genuine violations that are economically unviable to litigate under current doctrine. Cities and municipalities know this and calibrate their exposure management accordingly, fighting damages numbers and fee requests in part because making cases uneconomical is itself an effective defense strategy.[^5][^2]

## Part II: Rural and Small-Market Legal Deserts

### The Structural Attorney Shortage

The access-to-justice crisis is measurably more acute in rural areas. A 2025 Iowa State University study — among the most comprehensive rural legal desert analyses to date — found that more than **half of all rural counties in the contiguous United States** qualify as legal deserts, with insufficient practicing private attorneys relative to population and legal need. The crisis is especially concentrated in western states.[^9][^10]

Montana's rural legal landscape is particularly stark. According to filings by the Montana Legal Services Association (MLSA), **30 of Montana's easternmost counties have just 79 combined active attorneys** — approximately one attorney per every 2,386 residents. The Montana Supreme Court recognized this crisis in March 2026 when it authorized a Community Justice Worker program — certified lay volunteers, not attorneys — to fill basic legal service needs because attorney capacity has failed to meet civil legal demand.[^11]

A 2026 American Bar Foundation research brief confirmed that rurality compounds multiple other disadvantages: poverty, disability, age, and race all intersect with rural residence to increase exposure to civil justice problems while simultaneously reducing the likelihood those problems are resolved. The brief found that **nearly half of all low-income Montanans have at least one civil legal problem that goes unaddressed** each year.[^12][^11]

### The Social-Overlap Problem

Attorney shortages in rural communities are not simply a matter of quantity. Even where attorneys exist, structural barriers make them frequently inaccessible to civil rights claimants against local institutions.

The American Bar Foundation's rural access research identifies what it calls the "dense social ties" problem: in small communities, overlapping social, professional, and institutional relationships create conflicts — formal, informal, or relational — that impede access to legal assistance. An attorney whose firm also represents the county, whose partners golf with police leadership, whose clients include city-affiliated contractors, and who relies on referrals from local judges is structurally unable to take an aggressive civil rights case against those same institutions — regardless of whether the case has merit.[^13][^12]

Harvard Law School's Legal Deserts study — examining rural access to justice across multiple states — found that rural attorneys' reliance on community relationships for referrals and repeat clients creates "implicit dependencies that operate as soft conflicts of interest, reducing the effective availability of representation in cases that implicate local institutional relationships." In a county with three attorneys, all of whom have existing relationships with the county jail, the police department, or the courthouse, a person seeking to sue law enforcement faces effectively zero locally available representation.[^14]

The problem is amplified when the potential defendant is a high-profile local institution — a hospital, a major employer, a well-regarded nonprofit — whose board members, donors, and staff are deeply embedded in the community's social and professional fabric. The more locally prominent the institution, the more complete the representation exclusion tends to be.[^12][^13]

### The Out-of-State Representation Barrier

The logical response to local representation exclusion is to seek counsel from outside the jurisdiction. But this faces its own structural barriers. Out-of-state attorneys practicing in Montana courts must associate with a local Montana-licensed attorney as required by pro hac vice rules — creating a dependency on the very local bar whose institutional relationships may already create soft conflicts. This requirement adds cost, creates coordination friction, and can expose the out-of-state attorney to reputational concerns if local counsel declines to associate or associates reluctantly.[^14]

More broadly, complex multi-jurisdictional cases — involving conduct spanning multiple states with different statutes of limitations, different procedural rules, and defendants in multiple forums — are among the most expensive and resource-intensive categories of civil rights litigation. Contingency-fee firms that might otherwise take a strong single-jurisdiction case frequently decline multi-state matters because the coordination costs degrade the expected-value calculation across both jurisdictions.[^6][^2]

## Part III: The Criminal-to-Civil Knowledge Gap

### The Parallel Proceeding Problem

When a person is criminally charged, the facts of that charge frequently support parallel civil rights claims against the officers, prosecutors, or other institutional actors involved. The criminal defense attorney who knows those facts is often the only professional with both the knowledge and the attorney-client relationship to advise the client of those civil claims and the approaching deadlines to file them.

This structural position creates a professional obligation that is imperfectly understood and inconsistently fulfilled. The Florida Bar Ethics Committee concluded in 1974 that criminal defense counsel has "not only the right to discuss the availability of a civil remedy but a duty to advise his client of such rights." The American Bar Association Model Rules, Rule 1.4, require attorneys to communicate information reasonably necessary for clients to make informed decisions about their representation.[^15][^16]

Yet the standard of practice in criminal defense — particularly in small-market jurisdictions where criminal defense is primarily conducted by generalists rather than specialists — rarely includes systematic civil rights analysis of the underlying facts. The criminal defense attorney's attention is entirely focused on the criminal matter: plea negotiations, evidentiary suppression, trial preparation. The civil rights claims arising from the same facts — and their statute-of-limitations deadlines — are treated as someone else's problem, typically assumed to be addressed by the client's hypothetical civil attorney who, as Part II established, often does not exist in the relevant market.

### The Statute-of-Limitations Mechanics

Federal civil rights claims under § 1983 are governed by the personal injury statute of limitations of the state where the violation occurred. In Montana, this is three years from the date the claim accrues. The claim generally accrues when the plaintiff knows or should know of the injury — which in the context of police misconduct often means the date of the arrest, the search, or the retaliatory act.[^15]

A criminal case may take eighteen months to two years to resolve through investigation, charging, motions practice, trial, or plea. By the time it concludes, statutes of limitations on civil claims arising from the same facts may be expired or nearly expired. If the criminal defense attorney never raised the civil claims, and the client only learned of them after the case concluded, the most valuable civil rights remedies may already be lost.

This is not a rare edge case. It is a predictable, systematic failure that occurs whenever a criminal defense attorney treats their representation as bounded by the criminal docket rather than by the full scope of the client's legal interests. Legal malpractice claims for failure to advise civil rights remedies are among the most consistently documented forms of civil legal malpractice in the professional responsibility literature, yet they remain widespread in practice.[^17][^18]

### The Compounding Effect of the Discovery Rule

Montana recognizes a **discovery rule** for legal malpractice claims: the statute of limitations begins when the client discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the malpractice — not when it occurred. This means that if a client only learns years later that their criminal defense attorney allowed civil rights claims worth millions of dollars to expire without advising them, the malpractice clock may not have started until that discovery.[^15]

In practice, assembling the evidence to recognize that malpractice occurred requires: (a) knowing that civil rights claims existed and what they were worth; (b) identifying the specific deadlines that expired; (c) connecting those expirations to the attorney's representation period; and (d) documenting that the attorney failed to provide the required advice. Few non-attorney clients can assemble this analysis without independent legal help — which, in a legal desert, may itself be unavailable. The result is a recursive trap: the attorney's failure to advise produces harm that the client cannot diagnose without legal assistance that does not exist.

## Part IV: Institutional Conflict Architecture

### Nonprofit-Law Enforcement Overlap

In many American communities — particularly smaller cities — victim-services organizations, domestic-violence shelters, and social-services nonprofits maintain close operational relationships with local law enforcement. These relationships have genuine value: coordinated responses to domestic violence and sexual assault, information sharing, co-located services, joint grant applications. The relationships are common, encouraged by federal funding requirements, and generally understood as beneficial.[^19]

But these cooperative relationships also create structural conflicts of interest when employees of those organizations become parties to criminal complaints investigated by officers who simultaneously hold board positions, advisory roles, or other formal affiliations with the organization. A board member who simultaneously serves as a detective investigating a complaint against their organization's staff has a direct, undisclosed conflict of interest. This conflict does not require subjective bad faith to affect outcomes — the mere presence of the undisclosed relationship means the investigation is structurally compromised regardless of the investigator's subjective intent.[^20]

The legal framework for this problem is well-established. *Brady v. Maryland* (1963) requires prosecutors to disclose material evidence that is favorable to the defense — including information about investigator conflicts of interest that could be used to challenge the investigation's independence. Suppression of a material conflict — even through omission rather than active concealment — constitutes a *Brady* violation when it deprives the defendant of evidence they could have used to challenge the investigation.[^21][^22]

The *Monell* doctrine extends institutional liability to the municipality itself when an officer's conduct reflects a municipal policy, custom, or practice — or when supervisory failure to screen for conflicts reflects deliberate indifference to constitutional rights. A municipality that places no systematic requirement on investigators to disclose board memberships or organizational affiliations before being assigned to cases involving those organizations has, through omission, established a policy that allows conflict-tainted investigations to proceed unchecked.[^23][^24]

### The Conflict Disclosure Gap

No Montana statute currently requires law enforcement officers to disclose formal nonprofit board affiliations before being assigned to cases involving those organizations or their employees. No statewide policy requires recusal. The result is a structural gap: the conflict may exist, be material, and be discoverable through public nonprofit records — yet never be known to the defense attorney, the court, or the prosecutor unless someone independently researches the investigator's institutional affiliations.

This gap has national dimensions. A 2023 report by the DOJ Civil Rights Division's pattern-and-practice investigation unit documented that failure to establish adequate conflict-of-interest screening in investigative assignments is among the most common systemic deficiencies found in municipal police departments subject to pattern-and-practice review. The DOJ may investigate law enforcement agencies when evidence suggests a pattern or practice of unconstitutional conduct — but a pattern of unconstitutional conduct that is systematically concealed through undisclosed conflicts is, by definition, harder to identify as a pattern.[^25][^26]

### Court-Ordered Expert Monopolies

A related structural problem occurs in the court-appointed professional evaluator context — most commonly in forensic psychology assessments, but also in child custody evaluations, substance abuse assessments, and competency examinations ordered as conditions of criminal proceedings.

When a court orders a defendant to undergo a psychological evaluation as a condition of a plea or proceeding, several structural conditions often converge to remove the normal accountability mechanisms that govern professional conduct:

- **Market monopoly**: In smaller jurisdictions, one or very few practitioners may hold the specific credentials recognized by local courts for a particular type of evaluation, creating a de facto monopoly with no competitive accountability[^27][^28]
- **One-way adversarialism**: The evaluator is appointed by the court but produces findings that are operationally adversarial to the defendant, without the subject having independent representation during the evaluation process
- **Evidentiary authority**: The evaluator's conclusions carry the weight of expert testimony in subsequent proceedings, including bail determinations, sentencing, and parole — making errors or misconduct particularly consequential
- **Regulatory passivity**: State licensing boards for psychologists and mental health professionals are composed largely of professional peers and rarely take disciplinary action against forensic evaluators, even in the face of documented complaints[^29]

A 2022 New York Blue Ribbon Commission study of forensic evaluators found that the quality of court-ordered psychological assessments was highly variable, that credentialing standards were inconsistently applied, and that no systematic audit or review process existed in most jurisdictions to identify evaluators whose reports showed patterns of bias or error. The Commission found particular concern with what it described as the "judicial legitimacy amplification" problem: once a court relies on an evaluator's report, the official imprimatur makes subsequent challenges to the report's accuracy substantially more difficult, regardless of evidence.[^27]

When a licensing board denies a complaint against a court-ordered evaluator — even without a thorough investigation — that denial functions as an additional layer of official endorsement. Each subsequent court's reliance on the report further entrenches the report's apparent authority, compounding the original harm at each iteration.

## Part V: Inter-Jurisdictional Intelligence Failure

### The Multi-State Pattern Problem

American law enforcement operates within a highly fragmented jurisdictional structure. Local police departments, county sheriff's offices, state agencies, and federal authorities each maintain separate databases, separate case management systems, and separate information-sharing protocols. There is no national system that reliably flags when a complainant in one jurisdiction has had prior complaints dismissed, disbelieved, or found to lack evidentiary basis in another jurisdiction — or when an evaluator, officer, or prosecutor subject to documented misconduct in one state continues to practice in another.[^30][^31]

This fragmentation creates systematic opportunities for what researchers have termed "jurisdictional forum shopping" — the pattern by which a complainant whose allegations are not credited in one jurisdiction moves to a new jurisdiction to file similar allegations against the same target, with no notification to the new jurisdiction about the prior dismissal. Each new jurisdiction processes the allegations in isolation, without access to the prior investigation, prior dismissal, or prior credibility findings. The prior dismissal that would be highly material to evaluating the new allegations is structurally invisible to the new investigators.[^31][^30]

The problem is documented in the literature on police report data quality: researchers relying on police reports across jurisdictions have found systematic gaps in inter-jurisdictional data linkage that prevent pattern recognition, inflate apparent rates of credible allegations, and create artificial differences in case outcomes that reflect fragmentation rather than genuine evidentiary differences. As one study noted, the absence of cross-jurisdictional data linkage means that "a pattern which would be clearly apparent if viewed across jurisdictions remains invisible when viewed from within any single jurisdiction."[^30][^31]

### Sequential Economic Exhaustion

Multi-jurisdictional targeting — whether intentional or a product of fragmented systems — has a predictable economic effect on the target: each successive prosecution requires independent legal defense in a new jurisdiction, with new attorneys, new courts, new costs, new bail, and new limitations on employment. A person subject to simultaneous or sequential prosecutions in two or three states may face total legal defense costs that exceed their life savings regardless of whether any of the charges results in conviction.[^8][^2]

This economic exhaustion effect compounds the civil rights representation gap. By the time a multi-jurisdictional target has spent resources defending criminal charges across multiple states, they may have nothing left to fund civil rights litigation in the states where the violations occurred. Contingency-fee representation may be the only viable model, but the contingency-fee market's reluctance to take complex, multi-state, lower-damages cases means the very fact pattern that most warrants civil rights relief may be the one least likely to attract representation.

## Part VI: The Compounding Structure — How Failures Stack

Each of the failure mechanisms described in Parts I through V is individually documented and individually harmful. Their compounding interaction, however, produces a class of outcome more severe than any single failure would generate: a person whose constitutional rights were genuinely violated, who has no economic path to civil remedy, no available local counsel, no legal advice from the only professional who held the relevant information, no notification of conflicts in the investigation, and no means to demonstrate the cross-jurisdictional pattern that would be most persuasive to a court.

The structural sequence, in its most complete form:

1. False or exaggerated allegations are made in Jurisdiction A. Law enforcement processes them without independent credibility investigation.
2. Charges are dismissed — but the dismissal record does not follow the complainant to Jurisdiction B.
3. The same pattern of allegations arises in Jurisdiction B, processed again in isolation. The investigation is conducted by an officer with an undisclosed institutional relationship to the complainant's employer.
4. A search warrant is executed, seizing the target's professional tools and disrupting their livelihood regardless of the case's outcome.
5. A court-ordered professional evaluation produces findings adverse to the defendant, carrying official authority that subsequent proceedings rely upon.
6. A criminal defense attorney is retained who successfully resolves the criminal matter but never advises the client of the parallel civil rights claims — or of the approaching deadlines to file them.
7. By the time the client becomes aware that civil rights claims exist and have value, the statutes of limitations have expired. The criminal defense attorney faces a malpractice claim — but one that requires its own civil representation to prosecute, in the same small legal market where civil rights representation was already unavailable.

This sequence is not a hypothetical construction. It reflects a documented pattern that advocacy organizations across the country have identified in civil rights case materials — a pattern in which the structural features of the civil rights enforcement system combine to produce systematic impunity for institutional actors.[^32][^22][^2]

## Part VII: The Legal Framework That Should Work — and Why It Often Doesn't

### Section 1983 and Its Structural Limits

Congress designed § 1983 specifically to address situations where state-level institutions systematically failed to protect constitutional rights — the precise scenario this report describes. The statute's broad scope covers any person acting "under color of state law" who deprives another of rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution. This encompasses police officers, prosecutors exercising administrative functions, court-appointed experts operating under judicial authority, and municipal entities whose policies produce constitutional violations.[^33]

The three doctrinal barriers most frequently used to defeat viable § 1983 claims are qualified immunity (discussed in Part I), the *Iqbal/Twombly* heightened pleading standard (which requires plaintiffs to plead specific, plausible facts at the complaint stage — before discovery has produced those facts), and *Monell*'s demanding requirements for municipal liability. Each of these doctrines individually raises the cost and difficulty of civil rights litigation. Collectively, they filter out many valid claims at stages long before a jury ever evaluates the evidence.[^34][^23]

### The First Amendment Retaliation Doctrine

One of the more consistently viable § 1983 theories — when the facts support it — is First Amendment retaliation: a claim that government actors took adverse action against an individual specifically because they engaged in constitutionally protected speech or petitioning. The right to file formal complaints, post public reviews, send letters to elected officials, and petition government agencies for redress are all clearly established First Amendment rights.[^35]

In retaliation claims, temporal proximity between protected speech and adverse government action is powerful evidence of causation. Courts have held that when adverse action follows protected speech within days, the inference of retaliatory motive is strong enough to survive motions to dismiss and summary judgment. The *Mt. Healthy City School Dist. Bd. of Ed. v. Doyle* (1977) burden-shifting framework then places on the government the burden of proving it would have taken the same action regardless of the protected speech — a burden that becomes difficult when the adverse action closely follows the protected activity and when no other clear trigger exists.[^35]

### The *Brady* Doctrine and Conflict Disclosure

*Brady v. Maryland* (1963) and its progeny require that prosecutors turn over any evidence material to guilt or punishment — including evidence that would be useful in challenging the credibility or independence of an investigating officer. An undisclosed conflict of interest — a board membership, a personal relationship, a financial interest in the outcome — is Brady material if its disclosure could reasonably affect the defense strategy or outcome.[^21]

Brady violations are an area where pattern evidence is particularly powerful. A single undisclosed conflict might be characterized as an oversight. Recurring failures to disclose investigator conflicts, across multiple cases and multiple officers, constitute the kind of systemic pattern that supports a *Monell* municipal liability claim — establishing that the city's policies and customs were deliberately indifferent to the constitutional right to an untainted investigation.[^22][^23]

### The Pattern-or-Practice Enforcement Framework

The most powerful available tool for addressing systemic civil rights failures at the institutional level is the DOJ Civil Rights Division's pattern-or-practice investigation authority under 34 U.S.C. § 12601 (formerly 42 U.S.C. § 14141). When the DOJ finds that a law enforcement agency has engaged in a pattern or practice of unconstitutional conduct, it can seek court-ordered reforms, consent decrees, independent monitors, and mandatory policy changes.[^26][^25]

The statute requires finding a "pattern or practice" — not a single incident, however severe. This is where the inter-jurisdictional intelligence failure described in Part V becomes most consequential: patterns that are clear when viewed across multiple incidents and multiple victims may be invisible to DOJ investigators who rely on local agency records and local complaint histories. Community advocacy organizations that aggregate complaints, document cross-case patterns, and present compiled evidence to DOJ investigators play a critical function that no other institutional actor currently performs.[^36][^25]

## Part IX: Targeted Reforms

The reforms below are narrow, administrable, and directed at the failure points identified above. None requires a wholesale redesign of the civil-rights system. Each targets a documented mechanism that prevents meritorious claims from being investigated, preserved, or pursued.

### Reform 1: Mandatory Conflict Disclosure for Investigative Assignments

State legislatures should require law enforcement agencies to maintain current, comprehensive records of officers' formal organizational affiliations — board memberships, employment of immediate family members, financial interests, and prior professional relationships with individuals or organizations connected to a case — and to require affirmative written disclosure when an officer is assigned to a case in which any such affiliation exists. Failure to disclose should constitute a per se *Brady* violation, creating civil liability and grounds for case dismissal.

This reform costs nothing beyond basic administrative implementation. It directly addresses the most consistently documented source of conflict-tainted investigations in the reviewed literature.

### Reform 2: Mandatory Audio Recording of Court-Ordered Evaluations

All court-ordered psychological, psychiatric, and forensic evaluations — regardless of jurisdiction — should be audio or video recorded, with recordings preserved as part of the official case record and available to the subject of the evaluation upon request. The recording requirement removes the evaluator's monopoly on the account of what was said and provides an objective check on the accuracy of the written report.

New York's Blue Ribbon Commission on Forensic Evaluators recommended a similar recording requirement in 2022. No state has adopted this reform comprehensively. Given that fabricated or inaccurate evaluator statements can result in unconstitutional detention, coerced pleas, and decade-long reputational harm, the cost of the reform — essentially zero — is trivially outweighed by its protective value.[^27]

### Reform 3: Criminal Defense Notification Requirement

State bar associations and rules of professional conduct should explicitly codify the obligation — recognized but underenforced in existing ethics opinions — that criminal defense attorneys must advise clients of civil rights claims arising from the same facts as the criminal matter, including applicable statutes of limitations, before those deadlines expire. A standard-of-practice requirement for a signed client acknowledgment that the conversation occurred would create both an accountability mechanism and protection for attorneys who do provide the advice.[^16]

### Reform 4: Cross-Jurisdictional Complaint Flagging

National Crime Information Center (NCIC) protocols and state-level law enforcement data systems should be amended to include complainant-history fields that flag prior complaints found to lack evidentiary basis across jurisdictions. This does not mean recording "false" allegations in a punitive database — it means ensuring that when a complainant files allegations in Jurisdiction B, investigators in that jurisdiction are aware that Jurisdiction A previously found the same or similar allegations unsupported. This is precisely the kind of material contextual information that *Brady* obligations theoretically require be turned over but that currently never reaches investigators.

### Reform 5: Qualified Immunity Reform at the State Level

Several states — including Colorado, New Mexico, and New York City — have enacted state-level civil rights statutes that provide civil rights claims independent of federal § 1983 doctrine and expressly eliminate qualified immunity as a defense. Montana has not done so. State-level civil rights legislation that eliminates qualified immunity, lowers the pleading threshold, and establishes fee-shifting independent of federal doctrine would allow viable cases currently barred by federal doctrine to proceed in state court.[^37]

Colorado's 2020 Law Enforcement Integrity Act, which eliminated qualified immunity for state civil rights claims, provides a model. Research following its enactment found no evidence of the "flood of frivolous lawsuits" predicted by opponents, while producing measurable increases in accountability settlements.[^37]

### Reform 6: Statute of Limitations Tolling During Criminal Representation

State legislatures should codify that the civil rights statute of limitations is tolled — paused — during the period of criminal defense representation when the same facts give rise to both criminal charges and civil rights claims. This addresses the structural inevitability of the criminal-to-civil knowledge gap: even if the attorney fails to advise, the client's deadline does not expire while they are dependent on that attorney and unable to seek alternative advice.

## Part X: Evidence Aggregation and Public Oversight

### Pattern Aggregation and Documentation

The pattern-and-practice enforcement framework depends on pattern evidence that local attorneys, isolated victims, and DOJ investigators cannot independently assemble. Community advocacy organizations — particularly those focused on civil rights, police accountability, and institutional misconduct — occupy a unique structural position: they receive complaints from multiple individuals, across multiple cases, involving overlapping institutional actors, over extended time periods. Aggregated, anonymized complaint data that reveals recurring actors, recurring conflict structures, and recurring investigative failures provides the evidentiary foundation for DOJ pattern-or-practice investigations.[^25][^36]

The most effective advocacy organizations in this space approach their function as active evidence aggregators, not passive referral services. This means maintaining standardized intake documentation, preserving records across cases for cross-case analysis, and actively presenting compiled pattern evidence to regulatory and oversight bodies that have authority to investigate.

### Journalist Engagement

Investigative journalism has historically been among the most effective mechanisms for surfacing the kinds of institutional conflicts that pattern-and-practice enforcement depends on finding. Nonprofit board records are publicly available. Court dismissal records are public. Police internal affairs complaint outcomes are often accessible through public records requests. The connection between a detective's board membership and their assignment to a case involving that organization's employee is verifiable through public documents — it requires only the research to link them.

Journalists who understand the structural patterns described in this report can use them as diagnostic frameworks: when a civil rights case is dismissed on qualified immunity grounds, when a criminal case is dismissed with prejudice for lack of evidence after 18 months of prosecution, when a court-appointed evaluator's licensing complaint is denied without apparent investigation — each of these outcomes warrants the question: what institutional relationships, undisclosed conflicts, or systemic failures explain this result?

***

## Part XI: Montana's Evidentiary Access Barrier and Counsel-Supply Problem
### Records that never reach the attorney
In Missoula, the practical barrier is not only whether a victim can file a complaint; it is whether the underlying evidence package ever becomes visible enough for counsel to evaluate. Incident reports, internal complaints, body-camera files, dispatch logs, emails, and prosecutorial correspondence are often locked inside the same institutions whose conduct is under challenge. If those materials are withheld, delayed, or released only after narrow redactions, a civil-rights lawyer cannot tell whether the case is viable, cannot price the litigation, and cannot plead the facts with the confidence needed to take the matter on contingency.

That evidentiary bottleneck matters because the legal market for civil-rights cases is already thin. A technically capable victim can draft complaints, preserve timelines, and understand the theory of liability, but they still cannot manufacture the record. When the police department and prosecutor's office control the evidence, they also control the complainant's ability to show a lawyer enough to cross the intake threshold before the statute of limitations runs.

### The complaint black box in Montana
Montana's complaint process compounds the problem. Civil complaints can be blocked at intake, closed early on technical purview grounds, or routed into procedures that never respond to the full substance of the allegation. POST's narrow jurisdictional and temporal rules are especially damaging because they let institutions answer the form of the complaint while ignoring its substance. The result is a bureaucratic black box: complaints disappear into layers of filing requirements, redactions, and early closure rules without ever reaching a full merits review.

### Why this hits Montana harder than large states
Montana also lacks the deep civil-rights and legal-aid ecosystem found in larger states. The ACLU of Montana operates statewide through only three offices, and Montana Legal Services Association relies heavily on external funding and already serves far more need than it can meet.[^38][^39][^40] That matters because civil-rights representation is not just about legal theory; it is about where a victim can go with a paper trail, who has staff to review it, and whether anyone has the capacity to move before the deadline expires.

For most members of the public — especially the people most likely to suffer civil-rights abuse — that means the challenge is not merely "finding a lawyer." It is finding a lawyer who can get enough of the underlying record, fast enough, to decide whether representation is even economically possible. In Montana, that threshold is often the real gatekeeper. And once it closes, even the strongest case can become functionally unrepresentable.

***

## Conclusion

The practical question is not whether constitutional rights exist on paper. The question is whether the people most likely to suffer government abuse can actually invoke those rights before time, cost, isolation, and institutional conflict foreclose relief. On the record assembled here, the answer is too often no.

The barriers documented in this report fall hardest on rural residents in legal deserts, people entangled in criminal proceedings, defendants dependent on conflict-adjacent counsel, and individuals whose claims depend on records held by the very institutions they challenge. The failure is not episodic. It is structural.

The five mechanisms identified above — economic unviability of § 1983 litigation, rural legal deserts, the criminal-to-civil knowledge gap, institutional conflict architecture, and inter-jurisdictional intelligence failure — operate cumulatively. No single reform can solve a problem that is produced by several reinforcing failures. Meaningful reform therefore requires simultaneous pressure on each point of blockage.

Accordingly, the legal tools already exist: § 1983, *Brady*, *Monell*, the First Amendment retaliation doctrine, and the legal-malpractice discovery rule. What is missing is not doctrine. What is missing is an enforcement environment in which doctrine can actually be reached by the people it was designed to protect.
