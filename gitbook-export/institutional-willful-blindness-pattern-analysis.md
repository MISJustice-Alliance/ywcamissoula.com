## Executive Summary

This report documents a systemic, multi-layered failure of police accountability in Missoula, Montana — one that operates not through active conspiracy but through structural design. At every level of the oversight hierarchy, complaint systems are engineered to favor early closure, shield institutional actors, and suppress civilian accountability: at the local level via the Missoula Police Department's self-investigated Office of Professional Standards (OPS); at the municipal oversight level via an advisory-only Police Commission with no enforcement authority; and at the state level via the Montana Public Safety Officer Standards and Training (POST) Council, whose complaint triage policies effectively block civilian-origin investigations before they can begin.

Across multiple complaint channels — the Montana POST Council, the Montana Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC), and the Missoula Police Department's own Office of Professional Standards — detailed submissions supported by primary records have been met with responses so narrow that the actual content of each complaint was never acknowledged. Not disputed. Not addressed. Not even mentioned. Each oversight body found a specific procedural technicality precise enough to close the matter before engaging with a single substantive allegation: a date, a jurisdictional framing, a scope-of-representation characterization. The complaints might as well not have existed.

This is not a story about agencies that reviewed the evidence and reached the wrong conclusion. It is a story about agencies that appear to have deliberately structured their responses to ensure they would never have to reach any conclusion at all.

The pattern has the character of practiced, institutional willful blindness — a cynical inversion of "see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil" applied not just to misconduct and abuse of power, but to the baseline acknowledgment that institutions and the people inside them are capable of error. A system designed to evaluate professional and official conduct that cannot acknowledge even the possibility of a mistake is not an oversight system. It is a shield.

## I. Context: The Underlying Allegations That No Agency Would Address

Before analyzing the pattern of complaint closure, it is essential to establish what was actually submitted — and what was systematically not addressed.

The core factual record documents an alleged multi-phase pattern of retaliation, investigative misconduct, and institutional corruption spanning 2017 through 2025. The primary allegation is that after the complainant, Elvis Ryland Nuno, submitted a formal written complaint to YWCA Missoula leadership on June 3, 2018 — a communication later recognized by the court as protected First Amendment speech — law enforcement, prosecutorial actors, and YWCA-affiliated personnel coordinated to retaliate against him through the criminal process.

**The specific, documented substantive allegations that no oversight body addressed include:**

- **Officer Ethan Smith** initiated a stalking prosecution in 2018 while allegedly maintaining an undisclosed personal relationship with the complaining witness, E'Lise Chard, an Operations Manager at YWCA Missoula. Smith was subsequently removed from the case — a finding described in the record as arising from "an obvious appearance of impropriety."
- **Detective Connie Brueckner**, who assumed the lead investigative role after Smith's removal, simultaneously served on the Board of Directors of YWCA Missoula while investigating allegations that arose directly from Nuno's formal complaint to that same organization — an undisclosed conflict that bore directly on warrant integrity, investigative bias, and Brady-disclosure obligations.
- The September 2018 charging documents relied on statements attributed to Nuno's parents that both parents categorically deny making — fabricated or materially inaccurate police report content that was never investigated, challenged, or disclosed to the defense.
- A court-ordered forensic psychological evaluation by Dr. Marta Miranda — later alleged to contain fabricated interview summaries, an unsupported personality disorder diagnosis, and material HIPAA violations under 42 C.F.R. Part 2 — was obtained by Missoula County prosecutors and used to portray Nuno as a flight risk, fueling a serial charge-file-dismiss-refile strategy and an attempt to raise his bail from $50,000 to $75,000 and ultimately to revoke bail entirely.
- Defense counsel Bryan Tipp — representing Nuno in the criminal matter from July 2018 through January 2021 — allegedly advised Nuno to "give it time" regarding related civil rights claims while federal statutes of limitations expired, explicitly warned that filing civil litigation "would appear retaliatory and harm your criminal defense" (a statement the record characterizes as legally false and designed to deter constitutional accountability), and ultimately negotiated a deferred non-prosecution agreement containing a condition requiring removal of Nuno's protected online speech.
- All criminal charges were ultimately dismissed with prejudice in January 2021, producing no conviction and no corroborating evidence for any of the charged conduct — after eighteen months of prosecution, device seizure, bail conditions, and GPS monitoring.

Each oversight complaint submitted in the years that followed provided primary documentation in support of these specific allegations. Each oversight body closed the matter without addressing any of them.

## II. The Three-Tiered Architecture of Non-Review

### Layer One — Local Self-Investigation: The Missoula Police Department's Office of Professional Standards

The Missoula Police Department's complaint process routes all civilian complaints through the Department's own supervisory chain, with referral to the Office of Professional Standards (OPS) reserved for matters involving use of force, perceived ethics violations, or alleged criminal acts. The OPS is staffed by the Professional Standards Lieutenant and the Department's Training Officer — both MPD employees — and operates without external review authority, civilian oversight, or a mandatory disclosure obligation when complaints are closed.

This structure ensures that any complaint against MPD personnel is evaluated exclusively by MPD personnel. The institutional consequences are empirically measurable. Police Scorecard data for the Missoula Police Department covering 2016 through 2022 documents **349 civilian complaints** during that period, of which only **16% were ruled in favor of civilians** — a figure that ranks MPD's accountability score at **27% overall**, placing it among the lower tier of comparable departments nationally. Complaints specifically alleging **use of force** resulted in a **0% sustained rate** over that period, and complaints alleging **police discrimination** produced an identical **0% sustained rate**.

These are not aberrant outcomes in an otherwise functional system. They are the predictable output of a structure in which the institution that generates the misconduct is solely responsible for evaluating allegations of that misconduct, with no mechanism for independent review at the departmental level.

In the context of the allegations documented here, the MPD's self-investigation architecture meant that any complaint naming Officer Smith or Detective Brueckner — both MPD personnel — would be reviewed internally by an institution that had already demonstrated institutional alignment with their conduct by allowing a structurally conflicted investigator (Brueckner) to obtain a search warrant and drive a stalking prosecution while serving on the board of the organization at the center of the underlying dispute.

### Layer Two — Municipal Review With No Enforcement Power: The Missoula Police Commission

Montana law requires cities of a certain size to appoint a three-person Police Commission. The Missoula Police Commission hears appeals by citizens who have used the MPD complaint process and remain unsatisfied with its outcome. It also hears officer appeals of discipline under Title 7 of the Montana Code Annotated.

The Commission's structural limitation is categorical: it is an advisory body with no independent enforcement power. Commissioners are volunteers, unpaid, and without authority to compel production of records, impose sanctions, or override departmental disciplinary decisions. Its review function operates as a second reading of the same record produced by the same departmental process, without access to independent investigators, subpoena authority, or binding resolution mechanisms.

The Commission's existence satisfies the statutory requirement for civilian "review" while conferring none of the functional attributes that would make such review meaningful. For complainants who have experienced investigative misconduct, fabricated police reports, or undisclosed officer conflicts — allegations that are, by their nature, suppressed in the departmental record — a Commission review of that same departmental record cannot surface what the record was designed to exclude.

### Layer Three — State-Level Triage: The Montana POST Council's Five-Year Bar

The Montana Public Safety Officer Standards and Training (POST) Council holds statutory authority to investigate allegations of officer misconduct and, upon sustained findings, take action on officer certifications — including decertification. POST's process formally requires notification to the employing authority, a 30-day response period, presentation to the Case Status Committee, and a determination whether to close or open an independent administrative investigation.

In practice, however, a categorical triage rule effectively removes civilian-origin complaints from substantive review before that process can begin. POST's stated policy is that it "does not investigate allegations that are over 5 years old."

The application of this rule to the Nuno complaint is the paradigmatic example of the pattern this report documents. The complaint was dismissed in less than 24 hours. The stated ground was the five-year temporal bar. The actual content of the complaint — which documented not isolated historical incidents but an **ongoing pattern of harassment and institutional misconduct that was continuing into 2025** — was never acknowledged, assessed, or described in the dismissal.

The five-year bar, applied mechanically, created an outcome in which POST could avoid reviewing: the fabricated parental statements in 2018 charging documents; the undisclosed Brueckner-YWCA board overlap; the search warrant obtained by a structurally conflicted detective; and the serial charge-dismiss-refile strategy — all alleged to have produced continuing harm that remained active at the time the complaint was filed. The response made none of these distinctions. It invoked the date and stopped.

## III. The Attorney Oversight Channel: ODC-25-147 and ODC-26-145

### ODC-25-147: The November 2025 Dismissal of the Tipp Grievance

The grievance filed with the Montana Office of Disciplinary Counsel (ODC) as Case No. 25-147 addressed attorney Bryan Tipp's representation of Nuno from July 2018 through January 2021. The complaint alleged multiple, documented violations of the Montana Rules of Professional Conduct, supported by:

- Email correspondence from October 15, 2020, in which Nuno explicitly requested Tipp's assistance documenting an institutional conflict of interest involving law enforcement — a request to which Tipp responded that he was "generally disinclined" to provide the requested advocacy;
- Email correspondence from December 23, 2020, in which Tipp deferred discussion of civil remedies until receipt of the final dismissal order — by which time the 42 U.S.C. § 1983 retaliation claim (accruing from the November 2017 YWCA complaint) had already expired;
- Supplemental submissions documenting Tipp's failure to investigate Brueckner's undisclosed YWCA board membership — a publicly available conflict that bore directly on the integrity of the investigation, the warrant, and the charging theory;
- Documentation that Tipp negotiated a deferred non-prosecution agreement requiring removal of Nuno's Google review criticizing YWCA Missoula — a condition that, if accurately characterized, constituted an unconstitutional waiver of First Amendment rights as a term of criminal resolution;
- A Request for Review filed November 18, 2025, asserting that ODC's initial dismissal fundamentally mischaracterized the complaint as a "scope of representation" dispute when the actual claims concerned active misinformation about civil deadlines, affirmative discouragement of constitutional accountability litigation, and the trading of First Amendment rights in case resolution.

**ODC's stated ground for dismissal:** That Tipp fulfilled his obligations by achieving dismissal of all criminal charges and that the fee agreement established the representation was limited to criminal defense. Concluding there was no clear evidence of ethical violations under Rules 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, or 1.4, the ODC dismissed the grievance.

**What ODC did not address:** The specific allegation that Tipp provided legally false advice — that civil rights litigation "would appear retaliatory and harm your criminal defense" — advice the Request for Review described as "affirmative misrepresentation designed to deter constitutional accountability litigation." ODC's dismissal response did not quote, describe, dispute, or acknowledge this allegation. It did not address the documented email in which Tipp declined the October 15, 2020 advocacy request as "generally disinclined." It did not acknowledge the documented expiration of the November 2020 § 1983 statute of limitations during the period Tipp was actively representing Nuno. It did not address the First Amendment waiver allegation. It reframed the entire complaint as a question of whether Tipp was retained for civil representation — a question the complainant had explicitly stated, multiple times, was not the point.

The ODC's response is textbook procedural foreclosure: take the narrowest possible characterization of the complaint, resolve that narrow question in the attorney's favor, and close the file without engaging any of the specific allegations that gave the complaint its substance.

### ODC-25-147 July 2026 Closure Response: the evidentiary gap

The July 10, 2026 closure letter adds an important nuance to the record. Supplemental Submission #4 did not merely restate the earlier grievance; it identified two exhibit-specific allegations: the alleged failure to investigate an August 14, 2018 third-party email and an April 10, 2019 psychiatric letter concerning monitoring conditions. ODC's July response said the newly provided material contained no new or changed information or allegations not already addressed in the November determination, and therefore the matter would remain closed.

That language strengthens the procedural critique in one sense: the public letter does not show where the November determination addressed either exhibit. But the source record also supports a narrower and more careful conclusion. Montana's disciplinary rules give Disciplinary Counsel discretion over investigative scope and require a concise written statement of reasons, not an exhibit-by-exhibit findings order at the intake stage. So the public record supports saying the July response shows a continued resistance to responding to the substance of the complaint: it does not map the closure back to the two exhibits, and it preserves the same habit of answering at the level of process rather than substance.

That is the pattern's key feature: not just silence, but carefully bounded silence that leaves no public trace of substantive engagement.

### ODC-26-145: The June 9, 2026 Rejection of the Lowney Grievance

A separate grievance — ODC Case No. 26-145 — was filed on April 28, 2026, against Brian C. Lowney, another attorney whose conduct in relation to the Nuno matter is documented in the complaint record. The grievance was dismissed by ODC on June 9, 2026.

**Dismissal date context:** The dismissal was issued approximately six weeks after the grievance was filed. A Commission on Practice review request was being prepared before the July 9, 2026 deadline.

The ODC-26-145 dismissal — filed against the second attorney in the sequence — follows the same structural pattern as ODC-25-147: a brief response keyed to a framing narrow enough to close the file, without reaching the substantive allegations. The ODC's stated grounds in this instance relied on scope-of-representation framing — the same categorical resolution applied to the Tipp grievance — without engaging the specific record of Lowney's conduct as documented in the grievance and its exhibits.

The replication of an identical procedural deflection across two separate attorney grievances, involving two different respondents and two different complaint narratives, is itself evidence of a standardized institutional response pattern — not a case-specific assessment of the merits.

## V. The Anatomy of Practiced Willful Blindness

The pattern across these complaint channels is not random. It is structured. Each dismissal response reflects what might be called the **minimum-surface contact method**: the response acknowledges the existence of the complaint, identifies the narrowest possible procedural, jurisdictional, or scope-of-representation ground on which to close the file, and terminates the response at that point.

### The Review Loop: How a Complaint Becomes a Closed File

A companion visual analysis, *Institutional Willful Blindness in Montana: How Complaint Systems Can Convert Evidence Into Inaction* (MISJustice Alliance, June 2026), traces the minimum-surface contact method through five recurring stages, each labeled by evidentiary basis — a documented record directly supported by a cited letter, policy, complaint, agency response, court record, or official report; a reported allegation asserted in a complaint, declaration, or advocacy document but not independently adjudicated; an advocacy analysis interpreting a recurring pattern visible across the cited record; or a question the cited material raises but does not conclusively resolve, marked for independent review:

1. **Complaint or evidence submitted** *(documented record).* A complainant or advocate files records, correspondence, agency responses, and constitutional concerns with an oversight body — for example, the August 2025 POST complaint or the ODC 25-147 and ODC 26-145 attorney grievances, each supported by exhibits and supplemental submissions.
2. **Warning signs appear in the record** *(reported allegation; independent review needed).* The submitted materials raise questions that, on their face, would call for substantive review: contradictory witness accounts, an undisclosed institutional overlap between an investigator and a party to the dispute, historical context from the DOJ's 2014 gender-bias findings, and closure letters that do not quote, describe, or dispute any document submitted.
3. **Procedural deflection** *(documented record; advocacy analysis).* The response relies on a threshold category rather than an assessment of the submitted evidence — a temporal bar, a narrow scope-of-representation framing, a limited-authority commission, a self-referred review, a closure that asserts an unseen explanation, or a response speed inconsistent with substantive review.
4. **A closed accountability loop** *(advocacy analysis).* Each body — local self-review, the advisory municipal commission, the state certification council — defines the matter as belonging elsewhere and returns the complainant to the pathway rather than to a decision, leaving the central question unanswered: who has responsibility to evaluate the substance of the evidence?
5. **Downstream impact** *(advocacy analysis; independent review needed).* Running limitations periods, eroded public trust in oversight, unresolved and continuing harm, a record that states the matter was not reviewed rather than that the claims were false, and practical barriers to appeal or judicial review in the absence of a reasoned rationale.

The same source document sets out seven accountability checkpoints against which any closure can be tested: preserve the complete record as submitted; identify and test material contradictions rather than leaving them unmentioned; review relevant prior dispositions naming the same actors; explain jurisdictional and temporal limits in writing, applied to the facts alleged; refer matters onward with the complete evidentiary file attached; provide a documented, merits-based response that cites what was reviewed; and maintain an independent escalation path outside the responding institution. None of the closures analyzed in this report satisfies more than the first of these seven checkpoints.

This method has three structural consequences, each of which functions to protect institutional actors:

**First, it prevents the creation of an adverse record.** A response that says "we reviewed your allegations of fabricated police reports and found them unsubstantiated" creates a documented position that can be challenged, appealed, and used in subsequent litigation. A response that says "the complaint falls outside our temporal jurisdiction" creates nothing of substance — it cannot be litigated on its merits because no merits determination was made.

**Second, it guarantees replicability.** Because no agency reviewed the allegations on their substance, no agency produced findings that could bind a successor or complicate a future proceeding. Each complaint is treated as if it arrives in a vacuum. The pattern of the underlying conduct — the same actors, the same institutional relationships, the same sequence of events — is invisible to an oversight architecture designed to evaluate each complaint in isolation, resolved on procedural grounds before any cross-case analysis can occur.

**Third, it is self-reinforcing.** A complainant who has been procedurally deflected at the POST level, the ODC level, and the MPD internal level has been told repeatedly, in formal written communications from official bodies, that no investigation was warranted. Those communications do not say the allegations are false. They say the allegations were not reviewed. But the effect — in future proceedings, in reputational terms, in the absence of any adverse findings — is functionally equivalent.

### The ODC July 30 Letter as an Evidentiary Black Box

The July 30, 2026 ODC closure letter makes the pattern unusually visible. The agency does not simply reject the complaint; it attributes the closure to an explanation allegedly supplied by counsel and then withholds the contents of that explanation. The complainant is left with a conclusion unsupported by a cited document, a named witness note, or a reproduced statement. That is a textbook black-box maneuver: the agency asserts that it has a reason, but it does not disclose enough of that reason for a reader to assess whether the complaint was actually evaluated.

Two features make that especially useful for this analysis. First, the omitted allegations are not abstract disagreements about litigation style; they are concrete claims about conflicts, witness handling, and the treatment of exculpatory evidence. Second, the agency's own closure language turns on a factual premise — that counsel "was able to provide explanation" — which ought to be the easiest part of the file to disclose if it truly supports the closure. The refusal to do so is not neutral. It is a choice to preserve opacity.

The July 30 letter therefore belongs in the same family as POST's five-year rule and MPD's internal closure practices: each uses a formal category to terminate review while denying the public and the complainant access to the factual basis for the decision.

### The Scope-of-Representation Inversion

The ODC's handling of both the Tipp and Lowney grievances reflects a specific variant of this pattern. In each case, the complaint was reframed as a question of scope of representation — did the attorney undertake to perform the challenged conduct as part of the engagement? — and resolved on that framing without reaching the actual allegation, which was that the attorney performed conduct he did undertake in a manner that violated specific, identified rules.

The Request for Review in ODC-25-147 names this inversion directly: "ODC's framing fundamentally mischaracterizes this complaint as a scope of representation dispute about whether Mr. Tipp was retained to pursue civil claims. This complaint has never been about scope of representation — a point I made repeatedly in both my initial complaint and my response." The actual claims — active misinformation, rule violations, constitutional rights trading — required engagement with the specific conduct. The ODC's dismissal refused that engagement by treating a framing the complainant had repeatedly rejected as if it were the only framing available.

The July 2026 closure letter sharpens the same point. By saying the new material contained nothing new or changed, while not mapping that conclusion to the two exhibits identified in Supplemental Submission #4, ODC created a record that is procedurally defensible but substantively opaque. That is not the same as a merits finding on the exhibits; it is a controlled refusal to reveal whether the exhibits were actually weighed or merely absorbed into the prior closure label.

The July 30, 2026 closure letter adds a second layer of opacity. ODC says counsel "was able to provide explanation" for how he pursued the client's objectives, but the agency never discloses what that explanation actually was. No document, email, interview note, or other anchor is identified; the complainant is simply told the explanation exists and that it was enough to convert the disputed conduct into "strategy." In practical terms, this is an evidentiary black box: a final closure justified by an unseen record that cannot be tested or rebutted.

That matters because the allegations in the ODC-25-147 sequence are concrete and falsifiable — conflict screening, witness interviews, allegedly intercepted communications, and the handling of mitigation evidence. An institution that refuses to identify the factual basis for its own conclusion is not merely making a legal judgment; it is engineering a non-reviewable conclusion.

This is not review. It is procedural substitution.

### The Temporal-Bar Inversion

POST's five-year rule, as applied to the August 2025 complaint, reflects a parallel inversion. The temporal bar is presumably designed to protect officers from stale allegations unsupported by available evidence. Applied to a complaint documenting an ongoing pattern of institutional harassment with contemporaneous records extending through 2025, the bar functioned instead to exclude precisely the complaint with the most current and well-documented factual basis.

The POST response did not assess whether the complaint fell within or outside its scope — it invoked the five-year ground without examining the complaint's characterization of the conduct as a continuing pattern. Under established civil rights doctrine, a continuing violation is not measured from the first predicate act; it is measured from the last. POST's response applied a bright-line rule without engaging that question — and produced its answer in less than 24 hours, a timeline that forecloses any possibility of substantive review of the submitted documentation.

## VI. The Structural Roots: What Missoula's Oversight Architecture Was Designed to Prevent

The individual complaint dismissals analyzed above do not exist in isolation. They are consistent outputs of institutional structures that, across multiple analyses, have been shown to systematically produce non-accountability at each tier of the oversight hierarchy.

### The DOJ Precedent

In 2012, Missoula attracted national attention for what was publicly described as a systemic pattern of discounting and discrediting sexual assault victims. The United States Department of Justice launched an exhaustive investigation and, between 2013 and 2016, published findings and secured formal agreements with Missoula law enforcement, prosecutorial agencies, and local community victim advocacy institutions — including the YWCA of Missoula.

What followed the DOJ's closure of that investigation in 2016 is documented in the analytical record: the institutions subject to that oversight process responded by implementing what the record characterizes as "extreme overcorrections — policies that superficially signaled 'change'... that ultimately have served only as mechanisms to avoid future accountability, prevent independent oversight, and enable these institutions to quickly and severely stifle any dissent or public criticism." The DOJ oversight ended. The structural conditions that produced the original misconduct remained and, in some respects, were formalized through the new policy frameworks.

### The Prosecutorial Continuity: Pabst and the Post-DOJ Defensive Posture

The public record of the County Attorney's Office during and after the DOJ investigation documents a consistent posture of institutional minimization that paralleled the police-side accountability failures. During the 2014 campaign — conducted simultaneously with the DOJ investigation she was inheriting — Kirsten Pabst, then chief deputy under Fred Van Valkenburg, defended Jordan Johnson, a University of Montana quarterback charged with rape, arguing that his accuser had fabricated the allegation because Johnson "didn't offer her a relationship." National reporting from the period documented Pabst's role in this defense and her broader public minimization of sexual-assault reporting controversies. For a narrower source-led analysis of that role-switch and the appearance-of-conflict issue, see [Kirsten Pabst, DOJ Oversight, and the Jordan Johnson Case: Public-Record Investigative Report](https://www.ywcaofmissoula.com/additional-evidence-and-documentation/kirsten-pabst-doj-oversight-and-the-jordan-johnson-case-source-backed-investigative-report).

After winning election as County Attorney in November 2014, Pabst publicly positioned herself as part of the office's corrective direction. However, when Jon Krakauer's 2015 book *Missoula: Rape and the Justice System in a College Town* documented specific charging failures in cases her office had handled, Pabst issued a formal public statement characterizing Krakauer's findings as "inaccurate, exaggerated and unnecessarily personal," while simultaneously insisting Missoula's prosecution rates were "much higher than the national average" — a claim directly contradicted by the DOJ's own February 2014 findings letter, which documented only 14 prosecutions from 85 referred cases. The MTPR coverage from April 2015 — the same month as the DOJ settlement agreement — showed the office asserting that reforms had already been implemented while simultaneously disputing the factual basis of outside criticism. The Montana Kaimin headline from the same period — "County Attorney: Krakauer's Accusations Inaccurate, Exaggerated" — captured the core posture: the sitting County Attorney of the city at the center of a federal civil rights investigation publicly characterizing documented criticism of prosecutorial failures as overblown.

These are not private views. They are the documented public record of the person who led the institutional reform process. They are also the strongest available evidence that the reforms that followed were shaped by an institutional disposition to contest accountability rather than pursue it. The pattern documented in this report — procedural foreclosure, narrative management, and the systematic deflection of substantive complaints — is not a police-specific phenomenon. It is the prosecutorial counterpart of the same structural impulse.

#### Article Summaries and Significance

**Article 1 — Jezebel: "Not-So-Secret Rape Apologist Runs for Missoula County Attorney" (~2014, pre-election)**

Published during Pabst's 2014 campaign for County Attorney — after the DOJ findings letter but before she won the election. This placed it at the precise moment Missoula was supposedly turning a corner on accountability. The article documented Pabst's direct role in defending accused rapists while simultaneously campaigning on a "reform" platform — specifically her work as defense attorney on the Jordan Johnson case, during which she argued the victim had fabricated the allegation out of romantic disappointment. The article framed her candidacy as a direct threat to the DOJ reform process: a person who had spent the prior two years as a private defense attorney undermining sexual assault prosecutions was now positioning herself as the person who would fix the office's sexual assault prosecution problem.

**Article 2 — MTPR: Pabst's Statement on Krakauer's Book (April 22, 2015)**

Published the day after Jon Krakauer's book *Missoula: Rape and the Justice System in a College Town* was released — Pabst's first public, official response as sitting County Attorney to detailed documented criticism of her office and her predecessor's. The statement is a masterclass in institutional deflection. Pabst acknowledged "things that we missed" and conceded there were "deficiencies in communication" — but spent the bulk of the statement: (1) disputing the "rape capital" characterization, asserting Missoula's prosecution rate was actually "much higher than the national average"; (2) framing Krakauer's charges as "inaccurate, exaggerated and unnecessarily personal"; (3) insisting that "oversimplifying the many factors properly considered in deciding if a person should be formally charged...is disingenuous, destructive and, in the end, harmful to victims"; and (4) repeatedly pivoting to the SVU reforms her office had just created. Critically, she said the DOJ agreement "is not about prosecution rates" — directly contradicting the plain text of the DOJ's February 2014 findings letter, which was centrally about the charging decisions that produced only 14 prosecutions from 85 referrals. The statement's framing — that critics were "oversimplifying," that prosecutorial discretion required putting "emotion aside," and that Missoula was being unfairly maligned — mirrors precisely the posture described in this briefing: an institutional refusal to engage the substance of documented complaints in favor of procedural and rhetorical deflection.

**Article 3 — Montana Kaimin: "County Attorney: Krakauer's Accusations Inaccurate, Exaggerated" (April 2015)**

Published simultaneously with the MTPR statement — a student newspaper at the University of Montana, ground zero for the scandal, reporting on Pabst's public response to the book. The headline itself — drawn directly from Pabst's own words in the MTPR statement — captures the core posture: the sitting County Attorney of the city at the center of a federal civil rights investigation publicly characterizing documented criticism of prosecutorial failures as "inaccurate" and "exaggerated." This was not an off-the-record sentiment but a formal public statement from the chief law enforcement officer of Missoula County, delivered while still under the monitoring period of the DOJ agreement and directed at a nationally published investigative account of cases her own office had handled. The Kaimin audience — University of Montana students, many of whom knew victims and witnesses in the cases Krakauer documented — received from their county's top prosecutor the message that the national criticism was overblown.

### The Board-Police Overlap

The presence of Missoula Police Department Detective Connie Brueckner as a Board Member at-Large of YWCA Missoula while simultaneously serving as the lead investigator in a stalking prosecution that arose from a complainant's formal letter to YWCA leadership is the structural embodiment of the accountability failure this report documents. It represents the collapse of the institutional boundary between a law enforcement agency and a victim advocacy organization — a collapse that creates a structural conflict in both directions: the victim advocacy organization cannot serve as an adversarial check on police conduct when police leadership sits on its board, and the law enforcement investigation cannot be independent when the investigating officer holds fiduciary duties to the organization at the center of the dispute.

This overlap was never disclosed to the defense, the court, or the prosecution. It was never investigated by any oversight body. It is documented in publicly available records — YWCA board rosters, IRS Form 990 filings. No complaint channel reviewing the allegations against the criminal investigation has reached the question of whether that overlap was material. Each complaint was closed before the question could be asked.

### The Police Scorecard Data

Independent accountability data corroborates the structural analysis. Over the six-year period from 2016 to 2022, Missoula Police Department received 349 civilian complaints. Of those, **only 16% were ruled in any way in favor of civilians**. Use of force complaints: **0% sustained**. Discrimination complaints: **0% sustained**. The MPD's overall accountability score ranks at **27%** on the Police Scorecard's comparative index.

These numbers do not represent a string of reviewed complaints that were assessed and found lacking. They represent the systematic output of a process that begins with the institution under scrutiny, proceeds through an advisory body with no enforcement power, and terminates at a state oversight council that applies temporal bars without reviewing the substance of the allegations. The data and the process produce identical outcomes: complaints that do not register as misconduct not because they were assessed and rejected, but because the systems were never required to assess them.

## VII. The Complaint-Suppression Mechanism in Legal Theory

The pattern described in this report intersects with established civil rights doctrine in several respects that bear on both advocacy strategy and future litigation.

### Monell Liability and Policy or Custom

Under *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978), a municipality may be held liable under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for constitutional deprivations that result from an official policy, a widespread custom, or deliberate indifference to known constitutional violations. The MPD's complaint closure patterns — a 0% sustained rate on use of force and discrimination complaints over six years, combined with structural self-investigation and advisory-only external review — present a potential basis for a policy-or-custom claim that does not require proof of a specific policy directing misconduct, but rather evidence that the municipality was deliberately indifferent to a known pattern of constitutional violations.

The documented failure to disclose Brueckner's YWCA board membership to the court, the defense, or the prosecution — a disclosure that would have been required under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) and its progeny — combined with the institutional failure to investigate or act on that conflict when it was raised through the complaint process, creates a documented record of deliberate indifference that could support Monell analysis.

### The Unconstitutional-Conditions Doctrine

The alleged term in the deferred non-prosecution agreement requiring removal of Nuno's Google review — a public critique of YWCA Missoula — implicates the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine, established in *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593 (1972) and its successors, which holds that the government cannot condition a benefit on the waiver of a constitutionally protected right. If the non-prosecution agreement contained such a term, and if defense counsel negotiated or accepted it without adequate advisement of its constitutional implications, that conduct falls squarely within the territory addressed by the ABA's 2018 Resolution 113E condemning the practice of conditioning criminal resolution on civil rights waivers.

ODC's dismissal of the Tipp grievance on scope-of-representation grounds did not reach this claim. No oversight body has reached it.

### First Amendment Retaliation

The temporal sequence — protected complaint filed June 3, 2018; protection-order petition filed June 12, 2018; stalking charges filed September 2018; charging documents relying on conduct a court had already recognized as protected First Amendment activity — presents a substantial First Amendment retaliation claim under *Mt. Healthy City School District v. Doyle*, 429 U.S. 274 (1977). The nine-day proximity between the protected petition and the retaliatory filing, combined with documented evidence that the charging theory relied on the same YWCA complaint letter Judge Warren had already found to be protected speech, satisfies the threshold causation requirements for this theory.

No oversight body that received a complaint in this matter addressed this claim on its merits. Each response terminated before reaching it.

## VIII. The Broader Pattern: Institutional Capture and the YWCA Alignment

The institutional failures documented here are not reducible to individual acts of incompetence or isolated procedural missteps. The record reflects what multiple analytical memoranda in the case file characterize as **institutional capture** — the structural alignment of a victim advocacy organization with law enforcement in a manner that systematically compromises both.

The YWCA of Missoula's governance structure includes a sitting MPD detective as a Board member at-large, alongside other government-linked directors. This arrangement creates the structural conditions for confidentiality failures, biased information flows, and the conversion of an advocacy organization's institutional resources into tools of retaliation against individuals who file formal complaints about organizational conduct — precisely the sequence alleged here.

Multiple analytical sources in the case record document this pattern as broader than the Nuno matter: employee reviews describing organizational dysfunction, untrained staff engaging with traumatized clients, and management described as inflexible and self-protective; client accounts of confidentiality breaches; allegations of disability discrimination; and the broader documented history of the DOJ investigation and its institutional aftermath. These materials, taken in aggregate, describe not an organization with isolated failings but a governance structure that has systematically resisted external accountability while maintaining institutional relationships that foreclose effective oversight.

When the YWCA is understood as an actor in this configuration — alongside MPD, the County Prosecutors' Office, and defense counsel who allegedly aligned with institutional interests over client interests — the complaint dismissal pattern is not a series of unrelated procedural outcomes. It is the consistent behavior of a network of institutions that have collectively ensured that no oversight channel reaches the substance of allegations concerning their conduct.

## IX. Comparative Analysis: What Functional Oversight Would Look Like

A genuinely functional oversight response to the documentation submitted across these complaint channels would have exhibited at minimum the following features:

| Feature | What Occurred | What Functional Review Requires |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal scope analysis | POST invoked five-year bar without assessing continuing-pattern allegation | Assessment of whether conduct is isolated or part of a continuing violation before applying temporal bar |
| Conflict investigation | No body investigated or acknowledged Brueckner-YWCA dual role | Independent verification of investigator's institutional affiliations at time of investigation |
| Scope-of-representation parsing | ODC reframed grievance as scope dispute; dismissed without reaching actual claims | Distinction between passive scope limitation and affirmative misinformation; engagement with specific rule allegations |
| Evidence acknowledgment | No dismissal response quoted, described, or disputed any document submitted | Written summary of evidence reviewed and specific reasons for finding it insufficient |
| Continuing jurisdiction assessment | No body assessed whether ongoing harm extended complaint timeliness | Analysis of whether harm is continuing; tolling assessment |
| Cross-complaint pattern analysis | No body identified that multiple complaints concerned the same actors and institutional relationships | Pattern identification when multiple complaints name the same individuals or entities |
| Written rationale | Dismissal responses were conclusory: "does not warrant disciplinary action"; "over 5 years old" | Reasoned explanation sufficient to support meaningful appeal or judicial review |

The gap between what occurred and what functional oversight requires is not marginal. It is categorical. Every procedural feature that would give a complainant the ability to meaningfully challenge a determination was absent from every determination.

## X. Advocacy Recommendations and Escalation Pathways

### Immediate — Commission on Practice Review (ODC-26-145)

The July 9, 2026 deadline for requesting Commission on Practice review of the ODC-26-145 dismissal is the most immediate procedural threshold. The request should:

1. Distinguish, as the ODC-25-147 Request for Review did, between scope of representation and active misinformation — documenting the specific conduct alleged and demonstrating that ODC's framing resolved a question different from the one presented;
2. Incorporate the ODC-25-147 pattern analysis as evidence that identical procedural deflection has been applied across two separate attorney grievances, raising the inference of a standardized non-review response;
3. Preserve all arguments for potential Montana Supreme Court review if the Review Panel affirms dismissal, within the 35-day window under Montana Rules for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement Rule 10(C)(3).

### Federal Oversight — DOJ Civil Rights Division

The structural failure of civilian complaint mechanisms in Missoula presents grounds for referral to the Department of Justice Civil Rights Division's Special Litigation Section under 42 U.S.C. § 14141 (now codified at 34 U.S.C. § 12601), which authorizes the DOJ to investigate and seek equitable relief against law enforcement agencies engaged in patterns or practices of constitutional violations. The documented data — 349 complaints, 16% civilian-favorable rate, 0% sustained use-of-force rate, structural self-investigation — combined with the specific documented conduct in the Nuno matter, presents a factual basis for such a referral.

### Montana POST — Continuing-Violation Resubmission

A resubmission to POST framed explicitly as a continuing-violation complaint — documenting specific predicate acts within the five-year window, citing civil rights doctrine on continuing violations, and anticipating the temporal-bar response — is warranted. The submission should include a legal analysis of why the five-year bar, as applied to a continuing-pattern allegation, constitutes an abuse of the POST triage process that itself warrants review by the Law and Justice Interim Committee of the Montana Legislature, which has previously received POST process presentations.

### Legislative Advocacy

The structural gaps documented in this report — the absence of enforcement authority in the Police Commission, the self-investigation model at OPS, and the categorical triage policy at POST — require legislative remedy. Recommended reforms include:

- **Mandatory written rationale** for all civilian complaint closures at the MPD level, sufficient to support meaningful Commission review;
- **Independent investigative authority** for the Police Commission, including subpoena power and access to officer personnel records;
- **POST triage reform** requiring assessment of the continuing-violation question before applying any temporal bar to civilian-origin complaints;
- **Conflict screening requirement** prohibiting law enforcement officers from conducting investigations involving organizations to which they hold board-level fiduciary duties;
- **Mandatory disclosure obligation** requiring investigating officers to disclose any organizational affiliation with parties to the investigation to the court, the defense, and the prosecution.

### Civil Litigation

The malpractice claim against Bryan Tipp remains the most document-intensive and potentially most immediately viable civil pathway. The discovery that would flow from that litigation — fee agreements, email chronology, criminal docket, criminal motion filings, and the text of the deferred non-prosecution agreement — would also produce the primary records needed to establish the broader civil rights case against institutional actors. The malpractice filing deadline under Montana's three-year discovery-based limitations period, with the representation ending in January 2021, requires immediate attention from retained counsel.

## XII. Conclusion: The Shield Beneath the System

An oversight architecture that systematically closes complaints through procedural foreclosure — without assessing the evidence, without describing the allegations, without engaging the substance of any claim — is not performing oversight. It is performing the function of a shield, interposed between institutional actors and the accountability mechanisms nominally designed to reach them.

The pattern documented here is not anomalous. It is the predictable result of a system designed with accountability as a secondary priority: local self-investigation that requires the accused institution to assess its own conduct; municipal review without enforcement power; state-level triage that terminates the complaint before the review process begins; and a professional disciplinary framework that resolves grievances on the narrowest procedural ground available.

The individual complaint responses, taken in isolation, appear defensible on technical grounds. POST has a five-year rule. ODC found that Tipp's fee agreement limited his scope. Each response can be explained. What cannot be explained — if each response is taken at face value — is that across multiple oversight channels, across multiple attorneys and multiple agencies, across complaints submitted with primary record documentation over multiple years, not a single oversight body identified a single substantive allegation sufficient to warrant investigation.

The probability of that outcome arising by chance, across independent processes applying independent standards to independent factual records, is vanishingly small. The probability that it arises by design — through institutional structures engineered to produce exactly this result — is, as the data and the pattern both demonstrate, very high indeed.
