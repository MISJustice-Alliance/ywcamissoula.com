# Montana Police Accountability: Complaint Suppression and Civil Rights Advocacy Brief

**MISJustice Alliance | Accountability Research Division**

**Prepared:** June 2026 | *For Public Education, Advocacy, and Civil Intake Use — Not Legal Advice*

---

### Executive snapshot

This page is a public-facing companion to the longer structural-failure analysis. Its narrower claim is that Montana's police-accountability design does not fail only at the moment of discipline. It fails earlier, at intake, visibility, and review. In Missoula, the gap between documented use-of-force incidents and filed civilian complaints strongly suggests a complaint pathway that ordinary civilians do not experience as real, safe, or legible. At the state level, the documented handling of the 2025 POST complaint shows the same pattern in a different form: formal channels exist, but they are screened, closed, or explained through internal rules with little meaningful transparency.

For potential civil counsel and civil-rights advocates, the significance is twofold. First, the factual record supports an argument that accountability barriers are structural rather than anecdotal. Second, those barriers matter legally because Montana law gives the public unusually strong rights to know and participate, while federal civil-rights doctrine recognizes that a municipality can face liability when a culture of impunity is maintained through failure to investigate or discipline repeated constitutional violations.

## Core Thesis

The strongest public-accountability reading of the present record is not simply that some complaints fail. It is that the system is organized to make civilian complaints unusually hard to convert into reviewable accountability events.

At the local level, complaints are routed through the same department being accused, investigated by internal mechanisms, and then reviewed by a commission with no operational power to compel discipline or public disclosure. At the state level, the documented POST process shows rapid triage, self-referential explanations, and a review path that was not clearly disclosed at the moment it mattered most. These are not isolated paperwork defects. They are the practical features that determine whether civilians can use the process at all.

The data point that best captures the local problem is the disparity between MPD's own reported use-of-force activity and the number of civilian use-of-force complaints that entered the complaint system. The pages already published in this project document 211 use-of-force incidents in 2021 and 294 in 2022, while only four use-of-force complaints were filed across 2016–2022. Even allowing for imperfect benchmarks and differences in reporting methods, that gap is large enough to justify public concern that the complaint process is suppressive in practice rather than merely imperfect in design.

## Why the Complaint Gap Matters

For public audiences, the critical point is simple: an accountability system cannot be judged only by how many complaints it sustains. It must also be judged by whether people can realistically file, persist with, and understand a complaint in the first place.

The Missoula record supports at least four structural explanations for underreporting:

1. Complaint intake is controlled by the accused institution.
2. Complaint files and underlying investigative materials are largely shielded from public audit.
3. External review bodies appear advisory or internal rather than independent.
4. Complainants who describe pattern-based misconduct face screening rules designed around single incidents rather than continuing conduct.

That combination predictably produces low visibility, low trust, and low participation. A near-absence of complaints in categories where a department itself reports frequent force events is therefore not exculpatory. It can instead be evidence that the path from incident to complaint is blocked by fear, opacity, futility, or all three.

## Montana Law Makes This More Than a Policy Concern

Montana's Constitution is unusually protective of public oversight. In [Great Falls Tribune Co. v. Cascade County Sheriff, 775 P.2d 1267 (Mont. 1989)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/great-falls-tribune-co-v-1257485?utm_source=mcp), the Montana Supreme Court held that the public has a strong right to know when law-enforcement officers are disciplined for misconduct in the line of duty, explaining that public health and safety are closely tied to an honest police force. The case does not open every personnel file, but it squarely rejects the idea that police misconduct may be categorically hidden behind an undifferentiated privacy claim.

That point matters because the public record described across these pages reflects a different operational reality: blanket or near-blanket opacity around complaint files, limited visibility into how allegations are screened, and difficulty obtaining even the process information necessary to evaluate whether the system functioned as represented. Montana law does not require total disclosure of every internal record. But it does require an actual balancing of privacy and public disclosure interests, not a reflexive collapse of police-accountability information into a generic personnel-records category. [Billings Gazette v. City of Billings, 313 P.3d 129 (Mont. 2013)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/billings-gazette-v-city-of-2641776?utm_source=mcp) reinforces that point in the opposite direction: the balancing is fact-specific, and courts distinguish police officers and other public-trust actors from lower-trust administrative employees.

Montana also protects public participation in agency decisionmaking. In [Citizens for a Better Flathead v. Board of County Commissioners, 381 P.3d 555 (Mont. 2016)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/citizens-for-a-better-flathead-4088646?utm_source=mcp), the Court explained that the essential elements of participation are notice and an opportunity to be heard, and that the right requires more than an uninformed opportunity to speak. That principle is directly relevant to police-accountability systems. If a complainant is never clearly told how a complaint is being screened, what materials were reviewed, what review path exists, or how to invoke it, then the public has not been afforded meaningful participation in any ordinary sense.

## The POST Record as a State-Level Case Study

The 2025 POST complaint and the 2025–2026 follow-up correspondence matter because they show the state version of the same structural problem. The documented complaint described a multi-year pattern involving named officers, conflicts of interest, and civil-rights concerns. The documented response was rapid closure, reliance on internal purview and time-limit rules, and no substantive parsing of what parts of the complaint did or did not fall within POST's authority. Later correspondence, as described in the post-mortem page, surfaced an internal review mechanism only after persistent follow-up.

That matters as a rule-of-law problem even apart from the merits of the underlying allegations. Montana law assigns POST a serious statutory role in officer certification and decertification. In [Butte v. Butte Police, 559 P.3d 1248 (Mont. 2024)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/butte-v-butte-police-10753816?utm_source=mcp), the Montana Supreme Court described POST's statutory duty to provide for certification, recertification, suspension, and revocation of public safety officers. The case is not about civilian complaints, and it does not resolve the adequacy of POST's intake system. But it confirms that POST is not peripheral. It is one of the state's central accountability mechanisms for who may lawfully exercise peace-officer power.

When a body with that role handles civilian complaints through opaque screening, unexplained closure, or review mechanisms that remain internal and poorly disclosed, the problem is not just disappointing administration. It is a structural weakness in a state-designated accountability system.

## Civil-Rights Significance Under Federal Law

Federal law does not impose liability merely because a complaint process is frustrating. The stronger argument is narrower and more disciplined: when an agency's complaint and supervisory structure effectively permits repeated constitutional violations to go uninvestigated or undisciplined, that structure can become relevant evidence of municipal custom, deliberate indifference, or supervisory causation.

That is where the complaint-disparity evidence matters. In [Heriberto Rodriguez v. County of Los Angeles, 891 F.3d 776 (9th Cir. 2018)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/heriberto-rodriguez-v-county-of-4279559?utm_source=mcp), the Ninth Circuit explained that a custom of encouraging excessive force may be inferred from widespread violations for which officers are not discharged or reprimanded, and that a plaintiff may prove Monell-type liability through evidence of failure to investigate and discipline employees in the face of widespread constitutional violations. That does not mean Missoula liability is established by statistics alone. It does mean that repeated zero-accountability outcomes in categories like force and discrimination are legally relevant when paired with evidence of opaque review, internalized screening, or tolerated misconduct.

The First Amendment is also part of this picture. The Supreme Court has confirmed that the Petition Clause protects appeals to courts and other government-established forums for redress of grievances. [Borough of Duryea v. Guarnieri, 564 U.S. 379 (2011)](https://app.midpage.ai/document/borough-of-duryea-v-guarnieri-219105?utm_source=mcp). Guarnieri arose in the public-employment context, so it should not be overstated. But it remains useful for a limited point: formal complaint channels are not constitutionally trivial. When the state creates a forum that purports to receive grievances, the accessibility and intelligibility of that forum have constitutional significance, especially where complainants plausibly fear retaliation or reputational harm.

## Advocacy Implications

For civil-rights organizations, the most defensible advocacy position is that Montana does not presently need a better slogan about police accountability. It needs auditable intake, explainable screening, and independent review.

The existing record supports the following advocacy demands:

1. A public-facing complaint map showing each stage from local intake to POST review, including deadlines, closure reasons, and review options.
2. Written closure notices that identify the general basis for non-proceeding and the available next steps.
3. A redaction-first disclosure regime for police-misconduct records rather than categorical secrecy.
4. Independent intake options outside the accused department.
5. Legislative review of temporal screening rules that defeat continuing-pattern complaints.
6. Public reporting that distinguishes complaints, allegations, dispositions, and discipline by category.

These are modest reforms. None requires a finding that every allegation is true. They require only that a system claiming accountability operate in a way that permits civilians, journalists, advocates, and counsel to test that claim against a real record.

## What This Record Offers Potential Civil Counsel

For lawyers evaluating potential representation, this page is not meant to replace the record-specific materials already published in the project. It is meant to organize them.

The strongest litigation-support value here is the combination of:

* statistical disparity evidence from the MPD complaint scorecard,
* primary correspondence documenting POST intake and closure,
* Montana constitutional authority favoring disclosure and participation,
* and Ninth Circuit authority recognizing that failure to investigate or discipline repeated violations can support municipal-liability theories.

That package does not prove a case by itself. It does, however, provide a coherent framework for pleading why the accountability structure is relevant to causation, notice, deliberate indifference, and damages. It also gives civil-rights organizations a public-interest framing that is more durable than any one underlying incident: the issue is whether Montana's police-accountability institutions are accessible and reviewable by the public they are supposed to protect.
