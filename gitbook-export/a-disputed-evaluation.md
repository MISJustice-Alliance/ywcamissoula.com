# The Dr. Marta Miranda Evaluation Dispute: Records, Review, and Accountability

*A 2016 letter documents an evaluator's response to a contested court report. Establishing what regulators and courts subsequently did requires a more complete institutional record.*

**Source note:** This article draws on primary documents: Dr. Miranda’s October 27, 2016 letter, the archived health-provider complaint form, and Nuno’s two-page complaint concerning Patricia Fulton, together with statutes, court rules, judicial opinions, and original professional guidance. A complaint documents its author’s allegations; it does not itself establish their truth. Source notes identify what was reviewed and the limits of verification.[1](#source-1)[2](#source-2)[30](#source-30)

## A dispute with several distinct questions

In an October 27, 2016 letter, psychologist Marta Miranda acknowledged writing a psychological report for Edmonds Municipal Court and receiving a request from Elvis Nuno to amend or withdraw it and refund his fee. She declined the refund, offered to review specific requested changes without additional charge, and described how a continuing disagreement could accompany the report to Nuno's probation officer.[1](#source-1)

An accessible complaint form attributed to Nuno challenges the report's accuracy, the handling of psychological testing, communications with collateral sources, and fees. Those are serious allegations. The form establishes what its author alleged; it does not establish that the allegations were substantiated, that a licensing authority opened an investigation, or that a court found misconduct.[2](#source-2)

The public-interest question is how a person can obtain, examine, and challenge a consequential professional report—and how an outside reviewer can determine whether the challenge received a substantive response. Answering that question requires keeping three records separate: the evaluator's professional file, the court's case record, and any licensing agency's complaint file. Evidence about one does not automatically establish the contents or status of the others.

The December 2025 D.C. acknowledgment establishes legal sufficiency review at that time, but the reviewed record does not establish the matter’s status after the October 2026 supplement.[34](#source-34) “Unresolved complaint” would therefore be an unreliable procedural description. The documented dispute and the unanswered research questions are clearer than the available evidence of either agency's disposition.

## The earlier ABHC attempt described in the Fulton complaint

Nuno’s earlier complaint concerning attorney Patricia Fulton supplies a separate part of the evaluation history. The two-page complaint says he retained Fulton on December 15, 2015. On page 1, he alleges that evaluation work at Associated Behavioral Health Center (ABHC) involved extensive, multi-day testing but produced neither a full evaluation nor access to the resulting records. He says ABHC initially resisted a refund, relenting only after he raised the prospect of a health-regulatory complaint. These are his allegations; the reviewed document does not include ABHC’s response, the testing file, or a finding that its conduct violated professional rules.[30](#source-30)

The complaint also alleges limited contact between Fulton and his therapist, rejection of other proposed witness interviews, and communications that Nuno believed prejudiced ABHC’s assessment before examination. He expressly presents the last point as his opinion based on reported conversations with his therapist and an ABHC psychiatrist. He further alleges that ABHC later told him it had warned Fulton beforehand that it probably could not provide the evaluation sought, without either party telling him before testing. The underlying communications are needed to determine what was said, what service ABHC agreed to provide, and why it did not produce the requested evaluation.[30](#source-30)

Page 2 connects that unsuccessful attempt to a plea obligation: Nuno says he still lacked the required mental-health evaluation, had been summoned to explain, and understood he faced up to a year in jail. He alleges Fulton promised to send the court an ABHC letter seeking to avoid the hearing, but his assigned public defender subsequently confirmed the court had not received it. The complaint says the ABHC letter was attached, but that attachment is absent from the two-page copy reviewed. No court order, proof of transmission, Fulton or ABHC response, or bar disposition was examined with this source.[30](#source-30)

This earlier allegation of a letter not reaching the court is distinct from the later reported disappearance of Miranda’s completed evaluation. Together they explain why proof of attempted compliance, document delivery and institutional receipt matters to Nuno’s account. They do not establish a common cause or deliberate interference. The complaint’s exact execution date, the ABHC appointment dates, and the order linking that work to the later Miranda evaluation remain unverified; a project label of “2016” is not a filing receipt.[30](#source-30)

## What is challenged—and what Miranda said

The complaint alleges that the report attributed statements to Nuno that he did not make, presented comments selectively, misrepresented collateral interviews, and discounted test results. It asserts that recordings could verify some discrepancies. It also describes disagreement from a treating therapist and claims that the final cost substantially exceeded an initial quotation.[2](#source-2)

None of those assertions can be resolved merely by repeating the complaint. A reliable accuracy review would compare the exact report passages with complete recordings, authenticated transcripts, testing records, and contemporaneous source statements. It would distinguish a false quotation from a disputed paraphrase, and either from a clinical inference with which another professional disagrees. A methodological review would ask why a result was excluded or given less weight, rather than assume that every excluded result was improperly discarded.

Miranda's accessible response deserves equal precision. She says Nuno's October 22 email did not identify specific edits. She requests page-and-paragraph references, offers to consider changes, and says she may accept or reject them. If changes are made, she says she will send a revised report to Nuno and his probation officer. If disagreement continues, she says she will attach his requested changes and her response and forward them to the officer. She also offers to waive a previously charged copying and administrative fee.[1](#source-1)

That letter contradicts any unqualified account that Miranda never responded or offered no amendment process. It does not disprove the underlying allegations, establish that earlier fees were proper, or demonstrate that a substantive correction or disagreement statement was ultimately transmitted. Its November deadline also does not, by itself, establish the limits of any statutory access or amendment right.

No current response from Miranda to the proposed article is available in the accessible record. That is different from saying she declined to comment. No right-of-reply inquiries were sent for this review.

## Washington State oversight: identifying the actual disposition

Washington's Examining Board of Psychology has statutory regulatory responsibilities under chapter 18.83 RCW, including adopting an ethics code. Health-professional discipline operates under the Uniform Disciplinary Act. RCW 18.130.080 distinguishes a submitted complaint from a determination that it merits investigation; RCW 18.130.095 distinguishes initial assessment, investigation, and closure after investigation.[4](#source-4)

These distinctions prevent several common reporting errors. A complaint number can document intake without proving an investigation. A screening closure is not a disciplinary adjudication. A no-action disposition after investigation must be read for its actual grounds before anyone characterizes it as a ruling on the report's accuracy.

The project describes complaint 2017-3122PY as denied without an explanation of how it was investigated. That description is a lead for further reporting, not a substitute for the agency letter. The accessible complaint form supplies neither a disposition nor proof of agency receipt.[2](#source-2)[3](#source-3)

RCW 18.130.095 is particularly relevant to obtaining that missing record: it treats complaints not selected for investigation differently from complaints closed without action after investigation, and requires an explanation for the latter category. Other disclosure restrictions may still protect particular information.[4](#source-4) Without the actual agency file, this review cannot determine whether the Washington matter was screened out, investigated and closed, informally resolved, or addressed through another process. It cannot identify findings, sanctions, reconsideration requests, or review outcomes.

Nor does the absence of a disciplinary order in this review establish a clean disciplinary history. An ordinary web search is not an authenticated license-history search, and unpublished or confidential materials may exist. Conversely, the complainant's dissatisfaction does not establish an inadequate investigation. That assessment requires the agency's stated scope, evidence reviewed, and reasons.

Whether Miranda complied requires that policy, the custody arrangements and responses to actual requests—not an inference from the move itself.[9](#source-9)

Whether D.C. authorities can address a particular act depends on the professional's credential history, the conduct alleged, applicable statutory grounds, and the relationship between the conduct and the District's jurisdiction. ## The legal and ethical questions the record raises

### The evaluator's role and the information given to participants

A report prepared for a legal decision serves a different purpose from treatment. The American Psychological Association's *Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychology*, published in 2013, address impartiality, conflicts, explanations to examinees and collateral sources, and separation of observations from conclusions. They distinguish informing a court-ordered examinee from obtaining voluntary consent. They expressly describe themselves as advisory and reject liability or discipline based solely on inconsistency with the guidelines.[6](#source-6)

The complaint's allegations about collateral interviews therefore identify a legitimate question: what were participants told about the evaluator's role, the purpose of the interview, confidentiality, and likely use of their statements? An answer requires engagement documents, the court's directive, notices, releases, and the actual interview record. The allegations alone do not establish a disclosure violation.

It also matters whether a court specifically appointed Miranda, required an evaluation while allowing Nuno to select a practitioner, or approved some other arrangement. A court-ordered evaluation and a court-appointed evaluator are not interchangeable descriptions. The accessible letter confirms a report for the court; it does not establish the appointment mechanism.[1](#source-1)

### Accuracy, methodology, and opportunities to challenge a report

Washington ER 702 governs qualifying expert testimony that assists a decision-maker; ER 705 permits inquiry on cross-examination into the facts and data underlying an expert opinion. Neither rule establishes that this particular report was admitted, relied upon, or immune from challenge.[7](#source-7)

The next evidentiary questions are concrete: Which report version reached the court? Was it filed, admitted, or merely discussed? What finding or order relied on it? What objections were made, and how did the judge rule? The October 2016 letter's reference to the court and probation officer supports the report's intended legal use, not a finding of actual judicial reliance or its effect.[1](#source-1)

The project’s published hearing account now supplies a specific lost-record allegation: a December 2, 2025 hearing on a sealing motion at which the judge reportedly could not find the evaluation. That secondary account is discussed below; the recording, transcript, minutes and custodian’s search documentation remain necessary to verify the hearing and explain the absence.[22](#source-22)

### Access, amendment, and retention are different obligations

Washington law provides procedures for requesting access to qualifying health-care information and for requesting correction or amendment. RCW 70.02.100 contemplates a written refusal and a statement of disagreement; it does not guarantee adoption of the patient's preferred wording. Applicability depends on the record and relationship covered by the statute.[8](#source-8)

For psychologists, WAC 246-924-354 requires documentation, including evaluative reports, and at least eight years' retention after the last professional contact. It also requires a continuity policy addressing record requests when practice ends or moves out of Washington. The cited rule's history shows this retention language predates the 2016 dispute.[9](#source-9)

An evaluation associated with 2016 is not automatically subject to an indefinite retention requirement. Nor does the passage of eight years prove lawful destruction. Relevant facts include the last professional contact, subsequent custody, whether the records still exist, and any other applicable preservation obligation. Nothing in the accessible record establishes destruction, its date, or its lawfulness.

HIPAA requires separate analysis. Its access provision applies to covered entities and qualifying records and contains an exception for information compiled in reasonable anticipation of, or for use in, legal proceedings. That exception is directly relevant to a court-related evaluation; it does not establish that all associated records are exempt or resolve rights under another law.[10](#source-10) The October 18 supplement describes an outstanding records request and asks the regulator to establish receipt of the signed authorization. Whether a legal access deadline was missed depends on coverage, the completed request’s receipt date, applicable exceptions and the response history.[34](#source-34)

### Court records and agency records follow different access routes

Washington GR 31 addresses access to judicial case records, subject to legal restrictions. A request to a court for a filed report is therefore distinct from a request to the evaluator or a public-records request to the Department of Health.[11](#source-11)

For Washington agency records, RCW 42.56.520 generally requires an initial response within five business days; that response can include acknowledgment and a reasonable production estimate. It is not a universal five-day production deadline. RCW 42.56.550 provides judicial review and contains filing limits.[12](#source-12)

D.C. public records are governed by the District's own disclosure law, including access provisions, privacy and qualifying investigatory exemptions, and review mechanisms. A complaint-status inquiry is not necessarily a public-records request, and nondisclosure does not itself prove that no investigation occurred.[13](#source-13) No actual withholding, redaction, nonexistence response, or exemption determination in either matter was verified here.

### Constitutional protections and civil remedies require additional facts

In *Estelle v. Smith*, the Supreme Court found constitutional violations arising from the prosecution's use of a court-ordered psychiatric examination in a capital sentencing proceeding, including failure to provide the required warnings and opportunity for counsel's advice. The decision did not establish a universal right to have counsel physically present throughout every psychological interview.[14](#source-14)

Its relevance here would depend on the kind and stage of proceeding, compulsion, notices, counsel's involvement, waivers, and the use actually made of statements. A disputed report, without those facts, does not establish a Fifth, Sixth, or Fourteenth Amendment violation. A claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 additionally requires a deprivation of a federal right attributable to conduct under color of state law; professional disagreement is not enough.[15](#source-15)

Civil damages involve further barriers. Washington recognizes witness immunity in circumstances addressed by *Bruce v. Byrne-Stevens & Associates Engineers, Inc.* and quasi-judicial immunity for the family-court investigator's functions addressed in *Reddy v. Karr*. Those cases make the professional's actual function and relationship to a proceeding important; neither adjudicated Miranda's conduct.[16](#source-16)

A professional-negligence theory also requires a legally applicable duty, breach, causation, and injury. Washington's health-care negligence and limitation statutes may be relevant, but their applicability, judicial interpretation, accrual, and any tolling would require a claim-specific analysis. No conclusion that a damages claim is timely, barred, or viable is justified by the documents reviewed here.[17](#source-17) Regulatory review, evidentiary objections, correction rights, and damages litigation are different processes with different remedies.

### Institutional custody and the legal duty to disclose

**The court’s responsibility for its own records and the prosecution’s discovery duties are distinct, and both matter.** CrRLJ 4.7 addresses expert reports within prosecutorial possession or control and efforts to obtain identified discoverable material held by others. CrRLJ 7.6(f) directly provides discovery of evidence supporting an alleged probation violation. These rules support directing a focused request to the prosecution while separately seeking a custodian’s account of the court file. They do not establish that the prosecutor controls every judicial archive or that Nuno has satisfied every condition.[24](#source-24)[25](#source-25)

Washington’s retention framework depends on record classification, disposition dates and preservation holds. The court schedule reviewed is version 7.0, approved October 4, 2023—not a 2025 enactment based on its web address. CrRLJ 7.2 separately requires permanent preservation of sentencing and judgment records. Whether this report falls within that permanent record or another category requires the filing and sentencing record. The related resolution article explains the schedule’s different categories and triggers.[26](#source-26)

For sealing specified records, GR 15 requires preserved docket identifiers and an order and findings; redaction preserves the original under seal. Court-ordered destruction has its own documentation requirements. Routine destruction under approved schedules is separately permitted, so a missing motion alone does not prove unlawful disposal.[27](#source-27)

Washington State Archives expressly distinguishes recommended destruction logs from a statutory obligation to create them. Existing destruction and custody-transfer records generally have a 50-year retention period under CORE; ordinary IT audit trails have a different, business-need standard, subject to applicable holds. Neither establishes that every addition or removal must generate a permanent electronic entry. Edmonds’ local policies and the systems actually used still need examination.[28](#source-28)

A missing report can impair proof of compliance and the ability to challenge its conclusions. That raises a records-integrity question even before a constitutional claim is established. Federal lost-evidence doctrine distinguishes apparently exculpatory, irreplaceable evidence from merely potentially useful evidence, for which bad faith is required; Washington applies that distinction in *Wittenbarger*. The loss reported here has not been adjudicated under either test.[29](#source-29)

The practical accountability issue remains: identify what was received, where it was kept, what searches occurred, and what explains its present absence before attributing noncompliance to the person evaluated. Obtaining another copy may restore useful evidence; it would not itself explain the original custody gap. Conversely, a gap does not establish deliberate removal, report inaccuracy, or a violation by Miranda.

## What this case can contribute to the structural debate

The related GitBook essay, *The Civil Rights Gap: Structural Failures & Barriers to Securing Legal Representation*, proposes that limited evaluator choice, judicial reliance, and weak disciplinary oversight can reinforce one another. Its evaluator section needs a more disciplined evidentiary foundation.[18](#source-18)

**Evaluator availability.** The project alleges that Miranda was the only practitioner with the necessary credentials. This review found no contemporaneous court list, credential rule, referral record, or availability survey establishing that proposition. Even proof of a constrained choice would require care before calling a market a monopoly. Geography, qualification criteria, cost, waiting times, and whether alternatives could be approved all matter.

**Impartiality.** An evaluator can reach an adverse conclusion without violating impartiality. A departure would need evidence about methods, source treatment, undisclosed relationships, or misleading presentation—not simply the result or the fact of court involvement.

**Judicial reliance.** An intended court report is not proof that later judges adopted it, much less that it determined several later proceedings. Each alleged instance requires an identifiable record and decision.

**Regulatory reinforcement.** A later institution might misunderstand a no-action disposition as validation of a report. That is a hypothesis worth testing. This review identified no accessible statement in which a court or agency actually treated the Washington disposition that way. The effect should not be reported as an established chain of events.

**Board composition and performance.** The current Washington statute specifies nine psychologists and two public members; the current D.C. statute specifies four psychologists, a behavior analyst, and a consumer member. Those provisions establish professional majorities today, not bias in this case or the composition of a particular historical reviewing panel. Rates of investigation, findings, and discipline require independent data with meaningful denominators and case categories.[19](#source-19)

### Independent evidence on evaluator accountability

New York’s *Report of the Blue-Ribbon Commission on Forensic Custody Evaluations*, delivered in December 2021, identifies concerns beyond this individual dispute. Drawing on research, committee deliberations and public listening sessions, it concludes that some judges overuse evaluations and give them undue weight. Its recommendations include statewide certification and accessible complaints procedures (Recommendation 3), continuing training and peer review (4), conflict disclosures (6), and equitable access to reports and underlying data, with privacy safeguards (7). Under Recommendation 8, a majority supported legislation removing quasi-judicial immunity; opponents warned about retaliatory litigation and favored decertification safeguards. These are New York custody-policy findings and proposals, not binding Washington law or findings about Miranda.[20](#source-20)

Palo Alto University’s practice article approaches accountability from the evaluator’s perspective. It recommends clear role and confidentiality explanations, documented methods and limitations, multiple information sources, appropriate testing, and independence from partisan pressure. It treats one-sided information, role conflicts and misleading presentation as risks requiring safeguards. Its account also recognizes that dissatisfaction can generate complaints even when an evaluation is defensible. This is professional commentary, not a disciplinary finding or a statistical study of board enforcement.[21](#source-21)

For this dispute, those sources provide a framework for examining the available evidence: whether the report’s factual assertions can be checked, whether its methods and exclusions are explained, whether the subject can meaningfully contest it, and whether the complaint process produces an intelligible outcome. The complaint and Miranda’s response identify issues for that examination; neither resolves them alone. The comparison supports examining these safeguards across different proceedings while keeping the governing legal rules and individual facts distinct.

## Practical implications

For affected individuals and their advisers, the useful starting point is a precise record trail: the order or referral; the engagement and disclosure documents; each version of the report; the identified passages challenged; available recordings and test materials; and the delivery and response history of each request. Preservation and access must respect governing law, court orders, test security, and third-party privacy.

For journalists and researchers, a disposition should be reported with its date, issuing authority, procedural stage, and actual reasons. “Complaint submitted,” “investigation opened,” “closed without charges,” and “violation found” describe different events. The absence of a document from a reporter's collection should be described as a reporting limit, not attributed to an agency as destruction or refusal.

For legislators, the researchable questions include whether report custody is traceable, whether subjects receive clear instructions for access and disagreement, and whether agencies publish useful disposition data. Proposed reforms might include clearer custody protocols, understandable closure explanations, and privacy-protective aggregate reporting. These are policy proposals, not findings that either regulator violated existing duties here.

Any individual remedy depends on jurisdiction, the type and stage of proceeding, applicable deadlines, and the person's circumstances. These observations are general legal information; they do not establish a right to disregard an order, reopen a judgment, or obtain damages.
