# When Every Door Opens to a Wall

## An Open Letter on Accountability, Due Process, and the Cost of Trying to Be Heard

*Elvis Nuno — MISJustice Alliance | September 28, 2026*

{% include ".gitbook/includes/analysis-page-disclaimer-seo-geo-standard.md" %}

***

To the public, to the courts, to law-enforcement agencies, to professional regulators, and to every institution that tells people there is a process for reporting misconduct:

I am writing because I have spent years trying to use the systems that are supposed to exist when a person believes their rights have been violated, when records are wrong, when law enforcement will not intervene, when legal representation fails, or when government action causes harm.

I filed reports. I submitted detailed timelines. I provided records, correspondence, court materials, and specific questions. I tried to use grievance procedures, complaint processes, court motions, disciplinary channels, public-defender offices, and other mechanisms presented as avenues for accountability.

What I found was not a reliable path to remedy.

I found doors that appear to open, only to lead to a cement wall.

At best, the response has often been a brief conclusion without meaningful explanation: a sparse reply, a form closure, a single word such as "strategy," or a statement that the matter does not warrant action. At worst, attempting to seek help has seemed to expose me to further risk—retaliation, economic harm, reputational damage, escalating legal pressure, loss of safety, or pressure to become silent, leave, or simply disappear from the places where I had built a life.

This is not a demand that every complaint be accepted without scrutiny, or that every grievance produce the outcome the complainant wants. It is a demand for something more basic: a process that can be understood, accessed, reviewed, challenged, and explained.

A complaint process is not accountability merely because it has a form, an office, or a website. It is accountability only when the public can know what was reviewed, who made the decision, what standard was applied, what facts were accepted or rejected, and what path remains when a decision is wrong.

## A Remedy That Exists Only on Paper

The public is repeatedly told that there are channels for redress. There are complaint forms, professional standards offices, disciplinary authorities, law-enforcement oversight procedures, court motions, public-records processes, and appeals.

But a remedy does not become real merely because it exists in theory.

A meaningful remedy requires that an ordinary person be able to:

- Identify the correct office and decision-maker.
- Understand the governing procedure and deadlines.
- Know what evidence is being considered.
- Receive a reasoned response.
- Correct factual errors.
- Challenge a flawed decision.
- Obtain independent review when the agency itself, its employees, or its professional network may be implicated.
- Seek relief without creating an unreasonable risk of retaliation or incarceration.

My experience has been the opposite. I have repeatedly encountered systems that invite the public to submit information, then refuse to answer the most basic questions about what happened to that information or why no action was taken.

Who reviewed the evidence?

What materials were actually read?

What standard was applied?

What facts were found to be untrue, unproven, or irrelevant?

What evidence was ignored?

What appeal exists?

What happens when the agency asked to investigate is itself part of the problem?

When these questions are not answered, government and legal institutions become black boxes. The person on the outside is expected to comply, trust, wait, and accept the result—without being allowed to see how the decision was made.

That is not transparency. It is not due process. And it is not accountability.

## Detailed Evidence, Minimal Answers

I have provided detailed information concerning what I believe to be serious violations of my rights and serious failures of the institutions charged with protecting those rights. These matters include, among other things, alleged failures by law enforcement to address harassment and economic sabotage; false reports; testimony that I believe was untruthful; arrest and confinement that I contend were unlawful; failures to investigate exculpatory evidence; and legal representation that I believe failed to protect my ability to seek timely civil remedies.

I have not asked anyone to take those allegations on faith. I have attempted to provide records, chronology, context, documentary evidence, and specific factual questions.

Yet the responses have often been so brief that they do not demonstrate meaningful engagement with the record.

When a person submits a detailed complaint and receives only a conclusory answer—"not warranted," "strategy," "insufficient," or another unexplained closure—the institution has not necessarily shown that the complaint was wrong. It has shown only that it has chosen not to explain itself.

That distinction matters.

A person does not need to win every grievance to deserve a meaningful answer. At a minimum, a public office or professional regulator should be able to state:

- Which allegations it considered.
- Which documents it reviewed.
- What legal or factual standard it applied.
- Whether conflicting evidence was evaluated.
- Whether relevant witnesses or agencies were contacted.
- Why the evidence did or did not support further action.
- What review or appeal rights remain.

I am not asking the public to assume that every conclusion I have reached is correct. I am asking why the institutions responsible for review have so often refused to show their work.

## The Cost of Trying to Resolve It

This legal odyssey has not been a series of isolated incidents. It has consumed years of my life.

I was repeatedly required to travel back and forth at great financial, emotional, and psychological cost. Each trip involved uncertainty: lost work, travel expenses, lodging, legal costs, the possibility of detention, new conditions, new accusations, and another round of trying to explain the same record to people who appeared unwilling or unable to engage with it meaningfully.

The burden was not merely logistical. It became a form of punishment in itself.

There comes a point at which a person is no longer making a free choice between litigation options. They are trying to survive the cost of the process. They are trying to stop the travel, stop the financial losses, stop the fear of arrest, stop the damage to work and relationships, and regain some ability to live a normal life.

In my case, I entered a plea under circumstances that I experienced as coercive. I understood it as a tragic resolution—not justice, not vindication, and not a full acknowledgment of what I believed had happened—but a resolution nonetheless. I believed it ended the ordeal.

The project record includes a [declaration describing an Edmonds Municipal Court hearing](/overview/sworn-declaration-of-elvis-nuno-detailed-summary) at which I alleged that my attorney presented a choice between pleading guilty to Assault 4 DV and being taken into Snohomish County Jail. That account should be evaluated against the underlying hearing record, plea documents, and [Edmonds case file](/washington-cases/edmonds-case-2015-2017); however, it reflects the coercive reality I experienced at the time.

I believed that the matter was over.

It was not.

## Seeking Relief Should Not Be a Trap

Years later, I personally filed a motion in Edmonds Municipal Court concerning what I contend was an unauthorized disclosure of protected health information connected to a psychological evaluation.

Preparing and filing that motion was difficult enough. But in the process of trying to use the court system to seek clarification and relief, I learned that there were multiple outstanding warrants associated with my cases, including what I was told was a felony warrant.

I have been told that at least part of the issue may involve fees, but I have not been able to obtain clear written verification identifying the exact legal basis, case number, warrant type, financial obligation, current status, available procedure, or conditions for resolving the matter safely.

That lack of clarity is not a minor administrative problem.

Had I simply appeared in person to file a motion, ask questions, review the file, or request an explanation, I believe I could have been taken into custody. The risk was not theoretical. It meant that using the court process to seek due process could itself result in incarceration before I was able to meaningfully challenge the record or understand what was being demanded of me.

A court system cannot credibly call itself accessible if a person must risk arrest merely to learn why a warrant exists.

A person should be able to obtain written confirmation of:

- Every active warrant.
- The issuing court and case number.
- The warrant's date and type.
- The stated factual and legal basis.
- Whether the matter concerns a charge, a missed appearance, court costs, fees, probation, compliance conditions, or another alleged obligation.
- The bail or bond conditions, if any.
- The procedure to recall, quash, contest, or obtain a hearing regarding the warrant.
- Whether a remote, scheduled, counsel-assisted, or otherwise safe appearance is available.

Without that information, "just appear and deal with it" is not a neutral instruction. It can amount to a demand that a person surrender liberty before receiving even the most basic explanation.

## The Missing Evaluation

There is another unresolved issue that illustrates the larger problem.

When I filed my [motion in Edmonds](/washington-state-complaints/motion-to-seal-or-redact-court-record-dr-marta-miranda-evaluation-edmonds-munici), my understanding was that the judge stated he could not locate the psychological evaluation at all in the court file. I have also sought assistance from the Edmonds public-defender office in trying to locate it. So far, those efforts have not produced the evaluation or a clear explanation of where it is, whether it was ever filed, who possessed it, who transmitted it, whether it was maintained under seal or in a confidential repository, or what became of it.

I do not know why it cannot be found.

I do not know whether it was never filed, filed under another case number, maintained in a sealed or confidential portion of the record, retained by a provider or agency rather than the court, misindexed, lost, transferred, destroyed under a retention policy, or improperly removed.

I am not accusing any specific person of illegal conduct without proof. I am asking for a documented answer.

If an evaluation was referenced, disclosed, transmitted, relied upon, or used in a matter affecting a person's liberty, reputation, legal status, or mental-health record, then the official system must be able to account for it. The court, prosecutor, defense office, evaluator, medical custodian, and any other relevant records holder should be able to identify where the document came from, who had access to it, whether any authorization existed, how it was handled, and where it is now.

The absence of the document from the available court record raises a serious records-integrity question. It should not be dismissed as a clerical inconvenience.

A person cannot meaningfully challenge the accuracy, disclosure, use, or legal relevance of an evaluation while being denied access to the evaluation itself.

## An Impossible Choice

The demands tied to these unresolved Washington matters do not exist in a vacuum.

Even if I wanted to simply give in to every demand being made of me in Washington or Edmonds, doing so could require me to leave my elderly father without vital care and support. My father's wellbeing is not an abstract concern or a scheduling inconvenience. A sudden, extended absence could create a genuinely dangerous situation.

I also have responsibility for my household and my cat. But the primary concern is my father: an elderly person whose safety and stability depend in meaningful part on my ability to remain present and available.

This creates an impossible choice:

- Travel and risk detention without clear written information about the warrants or a defined path to resolution; or
- Remain home to provide vital care, while unresolved warrants and legal uncertainty continue to hang over my life.

No person should be forced to choose between seeking due process and protecting a dependent parent.

No system should demand that a caregiver surrender to an uncertain period of custody without first providing a clear, lawful, and humane mechanism to address the matter.

Courts and agencies have tools that can reduce needless harm: remote hearings, scheduled warrant-review hearings, counsel-assisted appearances, written warrant confirmations, temporary stays, payment-plan review, remote filing, appointed-counsel screening, and accommodations for documented caregiving emergencies.

The question is not whether the system has options. The question is whether it will make those options legible and available before irreversible harm occurs.

## What I Am Asking For

I am not asking for special treatment. I am asking for equal access to a system that says it is open to the public.

I am asking for:

1. **Reasoned written decisions.** Agencies, regulators, and complaint bodies should identify the allegations reviewed, evidence considered, governing standard, and reasons for closure or inaction.

2. **Transparent grievance procedures.** Every institution should clearly publish its jurisdiction, decision-maker, deadlines, evidence requirements, review process, appeal rights, and available remedies.

3. **Independent review where conflicts exist.** When law enforcement, prosecutors, court-connected personnel, professional networks, or agencies are implicated, there must be a credible independent avenue for investigation.

4. **Written warrant transparency.** Courts should provide clear written confirmation of any active warrant, its stated basis, its conditions, and a defined process for safely seeking review.

5. **A documented search for the missing evaluation.** Relevant custodians should identify whether the psychological evaluation was received; where it was filed, stored, sealed, transferred, or retained; who had custody or access; and what records exist regarding its handling or disappearance from the available file.

6. **Safe access to court.** People facing unresolved warrants should not have to risk surprise detention merely to obtain records, file motions, or ask the court what is happening. Remote, scheduled, counsel-assisted, and emergency-review options should be available.

7. **Caregiving accommodations.** Courts should provide procedures for people with documented obligations to dependent elderly parents, children, or medically vulnerable household members, so that compliance does not create preventable injury or death at home.

8. **Protection against retaliation.** People who report misconduct, seek records, challenge government errors, or petition for redress should not face retaliatory legal pressure, unexplained escalation, or punishment for attempting to exercise constitutional rights.

## The Larger Issue

This letter is about more than one court, one grievance, one warrant, one evaluation, one attorney, or one agency.

It is about what happens when the public is told that there are remedies for false reports, wrongful arrest, unlawful confinement, professional misconduct, improper disclosure of private information, retaliation, and violations of constitutional rights—but the actual processes are inaccessible, unexplained, contradictory, or dangerous to use.

A legal system should not require a person to become their own investigator, paralegal, archivist, public-records specialist, advocate, and crisis manager merely to obtain a clear answer.

A grievance process should not be a ritual in which the public submits detailed information and receives a conclusion without reasoning.

A court should not be a place where seeking to clarify a record becomes a trap that risks incarceration.

A government institution should not be able to rely on missing records, opaque procedures, unexplained warrants, or silence while insisting that the person affected must simply trust the system.

## Closing

I am not asking anyone to assume that every allegation I have raised has already been proven. I am asking for something that should not be controversial: an accountable process capable of reviewing evidence, answering questions, preserving records, explaining decisions, and providing a safe path for people to seek redress.

I am asking institutions to stop treating people who seek clarification as problems to be managed out of sight.

I am asking courts and agencies to show the public what they reviewed, explain why they acted or declined to act, identify the legal basis for the demands they make, and provide a real avenue for correction.

Accountability cannot be a word printed on a website.

It has to be something a person can actually reach.
